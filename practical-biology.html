BIOLOGY • O/L 0510

🔬 Practical Biology

Master the practical skills, experiments, observations, calculations, graphs and investigations needed for O/L Biology.

1. 🔬 What is Practical Biology?

Practical Biology is the study of biological processes through observation, experimentation, measurement and investigation.

In O/L Biology, practical questions test whether you can:

  • identify apparatus and biological materials;
  • follow experimental procedures;
  • make accurate observations;
  • record results in tables;
  • draw biological specimens correctly;
  • calculate values;
  • plot and interpret graphs;
  • identify variables;
  • state conclusions;
  • suggest improvements;
  • identify sources of error;
  • work safely in the laboratory.

⭐ Exam Tip

Practical questions are not only about remembering experiments. You must understand why each step is performed and what the result means.

2. 🧪 Laboratory Safety

Always follow your teacher's laboratory instructions.

  • Wear appropriate protective equipment when required.
  • Do not taste chemicals or biological materials.
  • Do not eat or drink in the laboratory.
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Keep the working area clean.
  • Report spills, breakages and accidents immediately.
  • Use heat sources carefully.
  • Wash hands after practical work.
  • Never deliberately culture unknown microorganisms outside approved school procedures.

⚠️ Safety Principle

Safety procedures are part of good experimental technique. If a question asks how to make an experiment safer, mention appropriate protective equipment, careful handling and teacher-approved procedures.

3. 📋 Planning an Investigation

A good investigation normally contains:

  1. Aim
  2. Prediction or hypothesis
  3. Variables
  4. Apparatus and materials
  5. Method
  6. Results
  7. Analysis
  8. Conclusion
  9. Evaluation

🧠 Example

Aim: To investigate the effect of temperature on enzyme activity.

Independent variable: Temperature.

Dependent variable: Rate of the reaction.

Control variables: Amount and concentration of enzyme, amount and concentration of substrate, pH and reaction time.

4. 🎯 Aim

The aim states what the experiment is designed to investigate.

A good aim often follows the pattern:

To investigate the effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable].

Example: To investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis.

5. 💡 Hypothesis / Prediction

A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting what you expect to happen.

Example:

If light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis will increase until another factor becomes limiting.

A good prediction should be related to the variables being investigated.

6. 📌 Variables

Variable Meaning Example
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed. Temperature
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed. Rate of reaction
Control variables Factors kept constant. pH, volume, concentration

⭐ Remember

Independent = I change it.
Dependent = Data I measure.
Control = Keep it constant.

7. 🧰 Common Laboratory Apparatus

  • Test tube — holds small amounts of substances.
  • Beaker — holds and mixes liquids.
  • Measuring cylinder — measures liquid volume.
  • Conical flask — holds and mixes solutions.
  • Dropper — transfers small amounts of liquid.
  • Thermometer — measures temperature.
  • Balance — measures mass.
  • Stopwatch — measures time.
  • Ruler — measures length.
  • Microscope — magnifies small specimens.
  • Glass slide — supports a specimen for microscopy.
  • Coverslip — covers the specimen.
  • Forceps — handles small objects.
  • Scalpel — cuts specimens under appropriate supervision.
  • Tripod and gauze — support apparatus during heating.
  • Water bath — provides controlled heating of samples.

8. 🔍 Measuring Instruments

Choose an instrument suitable for the quantity being measured.

Quantity Instrument Common Unit
Length Ruler mm or cm
Mass Balance g
Volume Measuring cylinder cm³ or mL
Temperature Thermometer °C

9. 📏 Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true value.

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.

To improve accuracy:

  • use appropriate apparatus;
  • read scales correctly;
  • avoid parallax errors;
  • measure carefully;
  • use instruments with suitable resolution.

10. 👀 Making Observations

An observation is something detected using the senses or an instrument.

Examples include:

  • colour change;
  • formation of a precipitate;
  • production of bubbles;
  • change in temperature;
  • movement;
  • change in size;
  • change in mass;
  • change in appearance.

⚠️ Observation vs Conclusion

Observation: The solution changed from blue to orange.

Conclusion: A reducing sugar is present.

11. 📊 Recording Results

Results should be recorded clearly in a table.

A good results table should:

  • have a clear title or description;
  • include headings;
  • include units in the headings;
  • record values consistently;
  • avoid unnecessary decoration.

12. 🔁 Repeats and Reliability

Repeating an experiment improves reliability.

If several measurements are taken, anomalous results can be identified and investigated.

A mean can be calculated from repeated measurements when appropriate.

Mean = Sum of measurements ÷ Number of measurements

13. ⚠️ Anomalous Results

An anomalous result is one that does not fit the general pattern of the other results.

Possible causes include:

  • measurement error;
  • incorrect timing;
  • incorrect volume;
  • equipment problems;
  • incorrect experimental technique.

Do not automatically remove an anomalous result. It should first be identified and investigated.

14. 📈 Drawing Graphs

Graphs are used to show relationships between variables.

For a standard graph:

  • Put the independent variable on the x-axis.
  • Put the dependent variable on the y-axis.
  • Label both axes.
  • Include units.
  • Use an appropriate scale.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Draw a suitable line or curve.

📌 Graph Rule

Independent variable → X-axis
Dependent variable → Y-axis

15. 📉 Types of Graphs

Different types of data may require different graphs.

  • Line graph: useful for continuous data.
  • Bar chart: useful for categories or discontinuous data.
  • Scatter graph: shows a relationship between two measured variables.

16. 📐 Interpreting Graphs

When interpreting a graph, look for:

  • increasing trends;
  • decreasing trends;
  • maximum values;
  • minimum values;
  • plateaus;
  • optimum points;
  • anomalous points.

Always support your answer with data from the graph when possible.

17. 🧮 Calculations in Practical Biology

O/L practical questions may require calculations involving:

  • mean;
  • rate;
  • percentage;
  • magnification;
  • percentage change;
  • ratios.

18. ⏱️ Calculating Rate

Rate = Quantity ÷ Time

For example, if 40 bubbles are produced in 20 minutes:

Rate = 40 ÷ 20 = 2 bubbles per minute.

19. 📊 Percentage Change

Percentage change = (Change ÷ Original value) × 100

The change is:

Final value − Original value

20. 🔬 Biological Drawings

Biological drawings are accurate scientific drawings of specimens viewed with the eye or microscope.

A good biological drawing should:

  • be large enough to show important features;
  • use clear, single lines;
  • not be shaded;
  • not use artistic colouring;
  • have labels with straight lines;
  • include a title;
  • show the correct proportions.

21. 🔎 Using a Light Microscope

A light microscope is used to magnify specimens that are too small to see clearly with the naked eye.

Main parts include:

  • eyepiece lens;
  • objective lenses;
  • stage;
  • stage clips;
  • coarse adjustment knob;
  • fine adjustment knob;
  • mirror or light source;
  • diaphragm;
  • arm;
  • base.

🔬 Diagram Space — Light Microscope

Suggested diagram: Labelled light microscope.

22. 🔬 Preparing a Temporary Slide

A general procedure is:

  1. Place a small specimen on a clean slide.
  2. Add a suitable drop of water or stain.
  3. Lower a coverslip carefully.
  4. Remove excess liquid if necessary.
  5. Place the slide on the microscope stage.
  6. Start with the low-power objective.
  7. Focus using the appropriate adjustment controls.
  8. Move to higher power if necessary.

⭐ Why Start on Low Power?

Low power gives a wider field of view and makes it easier to locate and focus the specimen.

23. 🧫 Staining Biological Specimens

Many cells are nearly transparent, making their structures difficult to see. A stain increases contrast.

Examples of stains used in school microscopy may include iodine solution or methylene blue, depending on the specimen and practical procedure.

⚠️ Important

The purpose of staining is to make structures easier to see; the exact stain used depends on the specimen.

24. 🔬 Magnification

Magnification tells us how many times larger an image appears compared with the actual object.

Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

The units must be the same before calculating.

25. 📏 Converting Units for Magnification

Useful conversions include:

  • 1 cm = 10 mm
  • 1 mm = 1000 µm
  • 1 cm = 10,000 µm

For example, if image size is measured in millimetres and actual size is in micrometres, convert one value so that both use the same unit.

26. 🧮 Magnification Example

Image size = 20 mm
Actual size = 0.5 mm

Magnification = 20 ÷ 0.5

Magnification = ×40

27. 🌱 Food Tests

Food tests are practical tests used to identify nutrients in food.

Food substance Test Positive result
Starch Iodine solution Blue-black
Reducing sugar Benedict's solution + heating Green/yellow/orange/red precipitate depending on amount
Protein Biuret test Lilac/purple
Lipid Ethanol emulsion test White/milky emulsion

28. 🥔 Testing a Food for Starch

Procedure:

  1. Place the food sample on a suitable surface.
  2. Add iodine solution.
  3. Observe the colour.

A blue-black colour indicates the presence of starch.

29. 🍬 Benedict's Test for Reducing Sugar

Procedure:

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube.
  2. Add Benedict's solution.
  3. Heat the mixture using an appropriate water bath.
  4. Observe the colour change or precipitate.

A positive result may range from green through yellow/orange to red depending on the amount of reducing sugar present.

30. 🥚 Biuret Test for Protein

The Biuret test is used to test for protein.

A positive result is a lilac/purple colour.

31. 🥜 Test for Lipids

In the ethanol emulsion test, a food sample is mixed with ethanol and then water is added.

A white or milky emulsion indicates lipid.

32. 💧 Diffusion Investigation

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

A practical investigation can demonstrate diffusion using a suitable coloured substance moving through water or another appropriate medium.

🔬 Diagram Space — Diffusion Experiment

Suggested diagram: Particles spreading from high concentration to low concentration.

33. 💧 Osmosis Investigation

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

Potato tissue can be used in a school investigation of osmosis.

The mass or length of potato pieces can be measured before and after exposure to solutions of different concentrations.

34. 🥔 Potato Osmosis Experiment

General method:

  1. Cut equal-sized potato pieces.
  2. Measure their initial mass.
  3. Place them into solutions of different concentrations.
  4. Leave them for the same amount of time.
  5. Remove and gently blot the pieces.
  6. Measure their final mass.
  7. Calculate the change or percentage change in mass.

📌 Variables

Independent: Solution concentration.

Dependent: Change in mass.

Controls: Potato size, volume of solution, temperature, time and potato source.

35. 🌿 Photosynthesis Practical

A classic practical investigation is testing whether a leaf contains starch after photosynthesis.

The experiment can investigate whether a factor such as light or chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

36. 🌑 Destarching a Plant

A plant is destarched by keeping it in darkness for a sufficient period so that stored starch is used during respiration.

This helps ensure that starch detected later was produced during the experiment.

⭐ Why Destarch?

To remove or greatly reduce previously stored starch and make the experiment a fairer test of starch production during the investigation.

37. 🍃 Testing a Leaf for Starch

General procedure:

  1. Heat water and use it to kill the leaf tissue.
  2. Remove the leaf and place it in ethanol in an appropriate water bath.
  3. Allow the chlorophyll to be removed.
  4. Rinse the leaf with warm water to soften it.
  5. Add iodine solution.
  6. Observe the colour.

Blue-black areas indicate starch.

⚠️ Safety

Ethanol is flammable. It must be heated using an appropriate water-bath method under teacher supervision rather than directly over a flame.

38. ☀️ Investigating Light as a Requirement for Photosynthesis

Part of a destarched leaf can be covered so that it receives little or no light while the rest is exposed.

After the starch test, the exposed area should show starch if photosynthesis occurred there.

Conclusion: Light is necessary for photosynthesis.

🌿 Diagram Space — Leaf Experiment

Suggested diagram: Leaf with part covered from light and labelled experimental arrangement.

39. 🟢 Investigating Chlorophyll

A variegated leaf contains green and non-green areas.

After exposure to light and testing for starch, starch should be found mainly in the green areas.

Conclusion: Chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

40. 💨 Investigating Carbon Dioxide

One leaf can be kept in an atmosphere where carbon dioxide is removed while another suitable part of the plant is exposed to normal air.

The leaves are later tested for starch.

If starch forms only where carbon dioxide was available:

Conclusion: Carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis.

41. 🫧 Investigating the Rate of Photosynthesis

Aquatic plants can be used to investigate factors affecting photosynthesis. Oxygen production can be estimated by measuring gas production or another suitable indicator.

Possible independent variables include:

  • light intensity;
  • carbon dioxide availability;
  • temperature.

42. 💡 Light Intensity and Photosynthesis

A lamp can be placed at different distances from an aquatic plant.

The rate of oxygen production can then be measured.

As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis generally increases until another factor becomes limiting.

⚠️ Fair Test

Keep the same plant, temperature, time period and other relevant conditions constant while changing the distance of the light source.

43. 💨 Respiration Practical

Respiration can be investigated by measuring changes associated with oxygen use, carbon dioxide production or heat production.

Living organisms release carbon dioxide during aerobic respiration.

44. 🧪 Testing for Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide can be tested using limewater.

When carbon dioxide is passed through limewater, it turns milky/cloudy because a precipitate forms.

Positive result: Limewater becomes milky/cloudy.

45. 🌡️ Respiration and Heat

Respiration releases energy. Some of this energy is released as heat.

An investigation can compare suitable living material with an appropriate control to detect temperature changes.

🎯 Importance of a Control

The control helps show that any observed temperature change is due to respiration rather than environmental temperature changes or the apparatus itself.

46. 🌱 Transpiration Practical

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through stomata.

The rate of transpiration can be investigated using a potometer or another appropriate method.

🔬 Diagram Space — Potometer

Suggested diagram: Labelled potometer showing a leafy shoot and capillary tube.

47. 💨 Factors Affecting Transpiration

Important factors include:

  • Light intensity: usually increases transpiration as stomata open.
  • Temperature: higher temperature generally increases evaporation.
  • Wind speed: moving air usually increases transpiration.
  • Humidity: high humidity reduces the diffusion gradient for water vapour.

48. 🌬️ Investigating Wind Speed

The same plant material can be exposed to different air movement conditions. The rate of water uptake can then be compared.

A suitable control should be included and other variables kept constant.

49. 🧬 Enzyme Practical Investigations

Enzyme investigations commonly examine how factors affect enzyme activity.

Variables may include:

  • temperature;
  • pH;
  • enzyme concentration;
  • substrate concentration.

The dependent variable may be the time taken for a reaction to reach a particular endpoint or the rate of product formation.

50. 🌡️ Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity

Enzyme activity generally increases as temperature rises until an optimum temperature is reached.

At temperatures above the optimum, enzyme activity decreases because the enzyme's structure can be disrupted.

📈 Graph Pattern

Enzyme activity usually rises to an optimum and then falls at higher temperatures.

51. 🧪 Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity

Each enzyme has a pH range in which it works effectively and usually an optimum pH.

Very acidic or very alkaline conditions can alter the enzyme's structure and reduce its activity.

52. 🥔 Catalase Investigation

Catalase is an enzyme found in many living cells. It catalyses the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.

Hydrogen peroxide → Water + Oxygen

Oxygen production can be used as an indicator of catalase activity.

53. 🫧 Measuring Gas Production

If a reaction produces a gas, the gas volume or another measurable indicator can be used to estimate reaction rate.

For a fair comparison, use the same:

  • reaction time;
  • amount of biological material;
  • concentration of solutions;
  • temperature;
  • apparatus.

54. 🦠 Microorganisms in Practical Biology

Microorganisms can be studied in school using teacher-approved prepared materials or controlled practical activities.

Important ideas include:

  • microorganisms reproduce rapidly under suitable conditions;
  • temperature affects growth;
  • nutrients are needed for growth;
  • some microorganisms are useful;
  • some microorganisms cause disease;
  • aseptic technique helps prevent unwanted contamination.

⚠️ Safety

Unknown environmental microorganisms should not be deliberately grown or opened outside approved laboratory procedures. Follow your teacher's instructions for any school culture experiment.

55. 🧼 Aseptic Technique

Aseptic technique is used to reduce contamination by unwanted microorganisms.

It may involve:

  • cleaning work surfaces;
  • using sterile equipment where required;
  • keeping containers closed as much as possible;
  • avoiding unnecessary contact with sterile materials;
  • following appropriate hygiene and safety procedures.

56. 🧫 Testing for Microbial Growth

In controlled school practical work, microbial growth can be compared under different conditions.

The experiment should be designed so that only the chosen independent variable changes.

Examples include investigating the effect of temperature or disinfectant conditions using teacher-approved materials and procedures.

57. 🧪 Antibiotic Investigation

In controlled laboratory demonstrations, antibiotic effectiveness can be compared by observing the size of zones where bacterial growth is inhibited.

A larger clear zone generally indicates greater inhibition under the specific conditions of the experiment.

⚠️ Important

Microbial cultures should be handled and disposed of only according to approved school laboratory safety procedures.

58. 💧 Testing Water

Water can be investigated for properties such as:

  • temperature;
  • pH;
  • clarity;
  • presence of certain dissolved substances.

Biological water-quality investigations may also consider organisms present and signs of pollution.

59. 🌍 Ecology Fieldwork

Ecological practical work involves studying organisms in their environment.

Common methods include:

  • quadrats;
  • transects;
  • random sampling;
  • systematic sampling;
  • counting organisms;
  • estimating population size.

60. 🟩 Using a Quadrat

A quadrat is a square frame used to sample organisms in a particular area.

It can be used to investigate the abundance or distribution of plants or slow-moving organisms.

🌿 Diagram Space — Quadrat Sampling

Suggested diagram: Quadrat placed randomly in a field or grassland.

61. 📍 Random Sampling

Random sampling gives organisms or locations an equal or appropriate chance of being selected.

It reduces personal bias when selecting sampling locations.

62. 📏 Transects

A transect is a line or belt used to study how organisms change across an environmental gradient.

For example, a transect could be used to investigate how plant distribution changes from a shaded area to an open area.

🌱 Diagram Space — Transect

Suggested diagram: Line transect across a habitat.

63. 🌿 Frequency, Abundance and Distribution

Frequency describes how often a species occurs in sampled areas.

Abundance describes how many individuals or how much of a species is present.

Distribution describes where organisms are found within a habitat.

64. 🌡️ Measuring Environmental Factors

Ecologists may measure abiotic factors such as:

  • temperature;
  • light intensity;
  • humidity;
  • pH;
  • soil moisture;
  • wind speed.

Measurements should be taken consistently to make comparisons valid.

65. 🧪 Control Experiments

A control experiment provides a comparison against which the experimental result can be judged.

Example: If testing whether a substance affects seed germination, a group without the tested substance can act as the control.

⭐ Why Controls Matter

A control helps determine whether the independent variable caused the observed change.

66. ⚖️ Fair Test

An investigation is a fair test when only the independent variable is changed while important control variables are kept constant.

A fair test allows a stronger conclusion to be made.

67. 🔄 Improving an Experiment

Possible improvements include:

  • repeat measurements;
  • use a larger sample size;
  • use more precise measuring equipment;
  • control temperature carefully;
  • keep volumes and concentrations constant;
  • use a suitable control group;
  • reduce measurement errors;
  • use a wider range of values for the independent variable.

68. ❌ Sources of Error

Sources of error can include:

  • incorrect reading of scales;
  • parallax error;
  • reaction-time error when using a stopwatch;
  • temperature fluctuations;
  • inconsistent sample sizes;
  • contamination;
  • equipment limitations.

69. 👁️ Parallax Error

Parallax error occurs when a scale is viewed from an incorrect angle.

To reduce it, read the scale at eye level where appropriate.

70. 📐 Scale and Resolution

The scale of an instrument determines how precisely a quantity can be measured.

Choose an instrument that can measure the required quantity with sufficient resolution.

71. 🧠 Observation, Inference and Conclusion

Term Meaning
Observation What is directly seen, measured or detected.
Inference An explanation suggested from observations.
Conclusion What the results show about the investigation.

72. 📝 Writing a Conclusion

A conclusion should answer the aim and be supported by the results.

Weak: "The experiment worked."

Better: "The rate of photosynthesis increased as light intensity increased over the range tested, shown by the increase in oxygen production."

73. 📈 Identifying Relationships

When two variables change together, describe the relationship carefully.

Useful phrases include:

  • increases as;
  • decreases as;
  • remains approximately constant;
  • reaches a maximum;
  • shows no clear relationship.

74. 🧬 Correlation vs Causation

A relationship between two variables does not automatically prove that one causes the other.

In an experiment designed as a fair test, changing the independent variable and observing the dependent variable can provide evidence for a causal relationship.

75. 🔬 Drawing Specimens Under the Microscope

When drawing a specimen viewed under a microscope:

  • draw only what you actually observe;
  • make the drawing large;
  • use clear single lines;
  • show important structures;
  • do not shade;
  • label accurately;
  • include the magnification or scale when requested.

76. 🧅 Onion Cell Practical

Onion epidermal cells are commonly used to practise microscopy.

The cells can be observed for features such as:

  • cell wall;
  • cell membrane;
  • cytoplasm;
  • nucleus;
  • large vacuole.

🔬 Diagram Space — Onion Epidermal Cells

77. 🌿 Leaf Epidermis Practical

A leaf epidermis can be examined microscopically to observe structures such as stomata and guard cells.

🔬 Diagram Space — Stoma

Suggested diagram: Pair of guard cells surrounding a stomatal pore.

78. 🧫 Cells and Osmosis

Osmosis can cause plant cells to gain or lose water.

When plant cells gain water, they become turgid.

When they lose water, the cell membrane may pull away from the cell wall; this condition is called plasmolysis.

79. 🌱 Seed Germination Investigation

Seeds require suitable conditions for germination.

A basic investigation can test factors such as:

  • water;
  • oxygen;
  • suitable temperature.

The experiment must be designed so that the factor being tested is the main difference between groups.

80. 🌱 Measuring Germination

Germination can be measured by recording:

  • number of seeds germinated;
  • percentage germination;
  • time taken to germinate;
  • root or shoot length after a fixed period.

Percentage germination = (Number germinated ÷ Total number of seeds) × 100

81. 🍞 Yeast Respiration Practical

Yeast can respire anaerobically when oxygen is limited and produce ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy

The carbon dioxide produced can be detected using an appropriate test.

82. 🧪 Experimental Controls in Yeast Investigations

When investigating a factor affecting yeast fermentation, keep important conditions such as:

  • amount of yeast;
  • amount of sugar;
  • concentration of sugar;
  • temperature;
  • time;
  • volume of mixture

constant unless they are the variable being tested.

83. 🫀 Heart Rate Practical

Heart rate can be measured by counting the pulse over a known period.

Heart rate per minute = Number of beats counted ÷ Time in minutes

For example, if 36 beats are counted in 30 seconds:

36 × 2 = 72 beats per minute.

84. 🏃 Exercise Investigation

An investigation can compare pulse rate before and after exercise.

A suitable investigation should include:

  • resting pulse measurement;
  • standardised exercise period;
  • pulse measurement immediately after exercise;
  • measurements during recovery;
  • repeated trials where appropriate.

Exercise increases the demand for oxygen and nutrients and increases the need to remove carbon dioxide.

85. 🫁 Breathing Rate Practical

Breathing rate can be determined by counting breaths over a measured period.

Exercise normally increases breathing rate and depth to increase gas exchange between the lungs and blood.

86. 🌡️ Temperature Control in Experiments

Temperature should be controlled when it could affect the rate of a biological process.

A water bath can provide more even and controlled heating than placing a test tube directly over a flame.

87. 🧪 Dilution and Concentration

When preparing solutions for an investigation, concentration must be controlled carefully.

If different concentrations are being tested, all other relevant conditions should remain the same.

Concentration describes the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution.

88. 📊 Choosing the Correct Graph

Data Suitable display
Continuous measurements Line graph
Separate categories Bar chart
Relationship between two measurements Scatter graph

89. 🧮 Ratios in Biology

Ratios may be used when comparing biological quantities.

For example, if a sample contains 20 red cells and 5 white cells:

20 : 5 = 4 : 1.

Always simplify ratios to their simplest whole-number form unless the question requires otherwise.

90. 📐 Estimating Population Size

Sampling can be used to estimate the number of organisms in a larger area.

The general principle is to sample a known area, count organisms and use the sample data to estimate the larger population.

The reliability of an estimate can be improved by taking more representative samples.

91. 🔍 Identifying Specimens

Biological specimens may be identified using visible characteristics or identification keys.

When using a key:

  1. Start at the first pair of statements.
  2. Choose the statement that matches the specimen.
  3. Follow the indicated number or letter.
  4. Continue until the organism is identified.

92. 🗝️ Biological Keys

A dichotomous key uses pairs of contrasting characteristics to identify an organism.

🌿 Diagram Space — Dichotomous Key

93. 🧠 Practical Question Strategy

When answering a practical question:

  1. Read the aim carefully.
  2. Identify the independent variable.
  3. Identify the dependent variable.
  4. Look for control variables.
  5. Study the results before writing a conclusion.
  6. Use numerical data when asked to support an answer.
  7. Check units in calculations.
  8. Label graphs and diagrams correctly.
  9. Explain why important steps were carried out.

94. 📝 Important Practical Definitions

Experiment: A practical investigation used to test an idea or relationship.

Variable: A factor that can change.

Independent variable: The factor deliberately changed.

Dependent variable: The factor measured.

Control variable: A factor kept constant.

Control: A comparison used to determine the effect of the independent variable.

Hypothesis: A testable prediction.

Observation: Something detected or measured.

Conclusion: A statement explaining what the results show.

Accuracy: Closeness to the true value.

Reliability: Consistency of results.

Validity: The extent to which an investigation tests what it is intended to test.

Anomaly: A result that does not fit the general pattern.

96. ❌ Common O/L Practical Mistakes

  • Putting the independent variable on the wrong graph axis.
  • Forgetting units.
  • Writing conclusions without using the results.
  • Confusing observation with explanation.
  • Failing to identify control variables.
  • Changing more than one variable at a time.
  • Drawing small or heavily shaded biological drawings.
  • Using multiple lines instead of clear single drawing lines.
  • Forgetting to repeat measurements.
  • Ignoring anomalous results without explanation.
  • Using the wrong formula for a calculation.
  • Failing to convert units before calculating magnification.

Food: Iodine = starch, Benedict's = reducing sugar, Biuret = protein, ethanol emulsion = lipid.

Microscope: Start on low power, focus carefully, then increase magnification if needed.

Magnification: Image size ÷ actual size.

Rate: Quantity ÷ time.

Photosynthesis: Light, chlorophyll, carbon dioxide and water are important requirements.

Respiration: Living cells release energy from food.

Osmosis: Movement of water through a partially permeable membrane.

Variables: Independent, dependent and controlled.

Good investigation: Fair, repeatable, measurable and properly controlled.

99. 🏆 Final O/L Practical Biology Summary

Practical Biology is about more than memorising procedures. You should be able to understand an investigation, identify its variables, collect reliable data, analyse the results and use evidence to reach a valid conclusion.

The most important practical skills are: observation, measurement, experimentation, recording, calculation, graphing, interpretation, evaluation and scientific communication.

🎓 Final Reminder

In the practical examination, always ask yourself:

What am I changing? What am I measuring? What must I keep the same? What do the results show? How can I make the experiment more reliable?
