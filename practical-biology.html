BIOLOGY • O/L 0510

🔬 Practical Biology

Master the practical skills, experiments, observations, measurements, calculations, graphs and investigations needed for O/L Biology.

1. 🔬 What is Practical Biology?

Practical Biology involves studying living organisms and biological processes through observation, experimentation, measurement and investigation.

Practical Biology is important because it allows scientists and students to test ideas using evidence rather than simply relying on assumptions.

In the O/L examination, you may be expected to:

  • identify laboratory apparatus;
  • identify biological specimens;
  • describe experimental procedures;
  • make observations;
  • record results;
  • complete tables;
  • perform calculations;
  • draw graphs;
  • interpret graphs;
  • identify variables;
  • state conclusions;
  • identify errors;
  • suggest improvements;
  • draw biological specimens;
  • calculate magnification.

⭐ Exam Tip

Do not only memorise the steps of an experiment. Understand why each step is carried out and what the result means.

2. 🧪 Laboratory Safety

Safety is an essential part of practical Biology. Always follow the instructions of your teacher and the laboratory rules.

  • Wear appropriate protective equipment when required.
  • Do not eat or drink in the laboratory.
  • Never taste chemicals or biological materials.
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Keep the working area clean.
  • Report spills and broken glass immediately.
  • Handle heat sources carefully.
  • Wash your hands after practical work.
  • Dispose of materials according to laboratory instructions.
  • Never deliberately culture unknown microorganisms outside approved school procedures.

⚠️ Safety Rule

When working with microorganisms, biological materials or chemicals, always follow the teacher-approved procedure and laboratory safety instructions.

3. 🎯 Aim of an Investigation

The aim tells us what the investigation is trying to find out.

📝 Example

To investigate the effect of temperature on enzyme activity.

A good aim usually identifies the factor being changed and the factor being measured.

4. 💡 Hypothesis / Prediction

A hypothesis is a testable explanation or prediction about what is expected to happen during an investigation.

For example:

If temperature increases, enzyme activity will increase up to an optimum temperature, after which the activity will decrease.

A prediction should be related to the variables being investigated.

5. 📌 Variables

Variables are factors that can change during an investigation.

Variable Meaning Example
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed. Temperature
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed. Rate of reaction
Control variable A factor kept constant. pH

🧠 Easy Way to Remember

Independent: I change it.

Dependent: Data I measure.

Control: Keep it constant.

8. 👀 Making Observations

An observation is something that is directly seen, measured or detected during an investigation.

Examples include:

  • change in colour;
  • formation of a precipitate;
  • production of bubbles;
  • change in temperature;
  • change in mass;
  • change in length;
  • movement;
  • change in appearance.

9. 🧠 Observation, Inference and Conclusion

Term Meaning Example
Observation Something directly seen, measured or detected. The solution changed from blue to orange.
Inference An explanation suggested from an observation. A reducing sugar may be present.
Conclusion What the results show about the investigation. The food sample contains reducing sugar.

⭐ Remember

Observation = What happened.

Inference = What you think it means.

Conclusion = What the results show.

11. 🔁 Repeating an Experiment

Repeating an experiment improves the reliability of the results.

Repeated measurements can help identify unusual results and allow a mean to be calculated.

Mean = Sum of measurements ÷ Number of measurements

12. ⚠️ Anomalous Results

An anomalous result is a result that does not fit the general pattern of the other results.

Possible causes include:

  • measurement error;
  • incorrect timing;
  • incorrect volume;
  • equipment problems;
  • incorrect technique;
  • unexpected experimental conditions.

13. 📈 Drawing Graphs

Graphs allow relationships between variables to be displayed visually.

  • Independent variable → x-axis
  • Dependent variable → y-axis
  • Label both axes.
  • Include units.
  • Use an appropriate scale.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Draw a suitable line or curve.

⭐ Graph Rule

Independent variable → X-axis

Dependent variable → Y-axis

14. 📊 Types of Graphs

Type Used For
Line graph Continuous data.
Bar chart Separate categories.
Scatter graph Showing the relationship between two measured variables.

15. 📉 Interpreting Graphs

When interpreting a graph, look for:

  • increasing trends;
  • decreasing trends;
  • maximum values;
  • minimum values;
  • plateaus;
  • optimum points;
  • anomalies.

Use actual values from the graph when the question asks you to support your answer with evidence.

16. 🧮 Calculating Rate

Rate = Quantity ÷ Time

Example:

If 40 bubbles are produced in 20 minutes:

40 ÷ 20 = 2 bubbles per minute.

17. 📊 Calculating a Mean

Mean = Total of values ÷ Number of values

Example:

Measurements = 10, 12 and 14

Mean = (10 + 12 + 14) ÷ 3

Mean = 12

18. 📈 Percentage Change

Percentage change = (Change ÷ Original value) × 100

Change is calculated as:

Final value − Original value

19. 🔬 Biological Drawings

A biological drawing is a scientific drawing of a biological specimen.

A good biological drawing should:

  • be large and clear;
  • show correct proportions;
  • use clear single lines;
  • avoid shading;
  • have accurate labels;
  • include a title;
  • show only structures actually observed.

20. 🔍 Light Microscope

A light microscope is used to magnify specimens that are too small to be seen clearly with the naked eye.

Important parts include:

  • eyepiece lens;
  • objective lenses;
  • stage;
  • stage clips;
  • coarse adjustment knob;
  • fine adjustment knob;
  • light source or mirror;
  • diaphragm;
  • arm;

21. 🔬 Preparing a Temporary Slide

  1. Place a small specimen on a clean slide.
  2. Add a suitable drop of water or stain.
  3. Lower a coverslip carefully.
  4. Remove excess liquid if necessary.
  5. Place the slide on the microscope stage.
  6. Start with the low-power objective.
  7. Focus the specimen.
  8. Increase magnification if necessary.

⭐ Why Start With Low Power?

Low power gives a wider field of view and makes it easier to locate and focus the specimen.

22. 🧫 Staining Biological Specimens

Many cells are almost transparent. A stain increases contrast and makes structures easier to see.

Examples of stains used in school microscopy include iodine solution and methylene blue, depending on the specimen.

23. 🔬 Magnification

Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

The image size and actual size must be expressed in the same units.

24. 📏 Converting Units for Magnification

Conversion Equivalent
1 cm 10 mm
1 mm 1000 µm
1 cm 10,000 µm

25. 🧮 Magnification Example

Image size = 20 mm

Actual size = 0.5 mm

Magnification = 20 ÷ 0.5

Magnification = ×40

26. 🍎 Food Tests

Food tests are practical tests used to identify nutrients in food.

Food Substance Test Positive Result
Starch Iodine solution Blue-black
Reducing sugar Benedict's solution + heating Green, yellow, orange or red precipitate depending on amount
Protein Biuret test Lilac/purple
Lipid Ethanol emulsion test White/milky emulsion

27. 🥔 Testing for Starch

Procedure:

  1. Place the food sample on a suitable surface.
  2. Add iodine solution.
  3. Observe the colour.

A blue-black colour indicates starch.

28. 🍬 Benedict's Test for Reducing Sugar

Procedure:

  1. Place the food solution in a test tube.
  2. Add Benedict's solution.
  3. Heat using an appropriate water bath.
  4. Observe the resulting colour or precipitate.

A positive result can range from green through yellow/orange to red depending on the amount of reducing sugar present.

29. 🥚 Biuret Test for Protein

The Biuret test is used to test for protein.

A positive test produces a lilac/purple colour.

30. 🥜 Test for Lipids

The ethanol emulsion test can be used to test for lipids.

A positive test produces a white or milky emulsion.

31. 💧 Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

🔬 Diagram Space — Diffusion

Suggested diagram: Particles moving from high concentration to low concentration.

32. 💧 Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

33. 🥔 Potato Osmosis Experiment

A common school investigation involves potato tissue.

  1. Cut equal-sized potato pieces.
  2. Measure their initial masses.
  3. Place them in solutions of different concentrations.
  4. Leave them for the same period.
  5. Remove and gently blot the pieces.
  6. Measure their final masses.
  7. Calculate the change or percentage change in mass.

📌 Variables

Independent: Solution concentration.

Dependent: Change in mass.

Controls: Potato size, volume of solution, temperature and time.

34. 🌱 Photosynthesis Practical

A common practical investigation is testing a leaf for starch.

The experiment can also investigate whether light, chlorophyll or carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis.

35. 🌑 Destarching a Plant

A plant is kept in darkness for a sufficient period so that stored starch is used during respiration.

⭐ Why Destarch the Plant?

To remove or greatly reduce stored starch so that starch detected later is more likely to have been produced during the experiment.

36. 🍃 Testing a Leaf for Starch

  1. Heat water and use it to kill the leaf tissue.
  2. Place the leaf in ethanol using an appropriate water-bath method.
  3. Allow the chlorophyll to be removed.
  4. Rinse the leaf with warm water.
  5. Add iodine solution.
  6. Observe the colour.

Blue-black areas indicate the presence of starch.

⚠️ Safety

Ethanol is flammable. It should be heated using an appropriate water bath under teacher supervision rather than directly over a flame.

37. ☀️ Investigating Light as a Requirement

Part of a destarched leaf can be covered so that it receives little or no light while the remaining part is exposed.

After testing the leaf for starch, the exposed region should show starch if photosynthesis occurred there.

Conclusion: Light is necessary for photosynthesis.

🌿 Diagram Space — Leaf Light Experiment

Suggested diagram: Leaf with part covered from light.

38. 🟢 Investigating Chlorophyll

A variegated leaf contains green and non-green areas.

After exposure to light and testing for starch, starch should be found mainly in the green areas.

Conclusion: Chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

39. 💨 Investigating Carbon Dioxide

An experiment can compare a leaf exposed to carbon dioxide with a leaf or part of a plant where carbon dioxide has been removed.

The leaves are later tested for starch.

Conclusion: Carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis.

40. 🫧 Investigating the Rate of Photosynthesis

An aquatic plant can be used to investigate photosynthesis.

Oxygen production can be estimated by measuring gas production or another suitable indicator.

Factors that may be investigated include:

  • light intensity;
  • carbon dioxide availability;
  • temperature.

41. 💡 Light Intensity and Photosynthesis

A lamp can be placed at different distances from an aquatic plant. The rate of oxygen production can then be compared.

Increasing light intensity generally increases the rate of photosynthesis until another factor becomes limiting.

42. 💨 Respiration Practical

Respiration can be investigated by studying carbon dioxide production, oxygen use or heat production.

Living organisms release carbon dioxide during aerobic respiration.

43. 🫧 Testing for Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide can be tested using limewater.

When carbon dioxide is passed through limewater, the limewater becomes milky/cloudy.

Positive result: Limewater turns milky/cloudy.

44. 🌡️ Respiration and Heat

Respiration releases energy and some of this energy is released as heat.

A suitable experiment can compare living material with an appropriate control to investigate temperature changes.

45. 💧 Transpiration Practical

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata.

A potometer can be used to estimate water uptake by a leafy shoot.

🌿 Diagram Space — Potometer

Suggested diagram: Fully labelled potometer.

46. 🌬️ Factors Affecting Transpiration

Factor General Effect on Transpiration
Light intensity Usually increases transpiration because stomata tend to open.
Temperature Higher temperature usually increases evaporation.
Wind speed Moving air usually increases transpiration.
Humidity High humidity usually decreases transpiration.

47. 🌱 Enzyme Practical Investigations

Enzyme investigations examine how different factors affect enzyme activity.

Factors can include:

  • temperature;
  • pH;
  • enzyme concentration;
  • substrate concentration.

48. 🌡️ Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity

As temperature increases, enzyme activity generally increases until an optimum temperature is reached.

At temperatures above the optimum, enzyme activity decreases because the enzyme's structure can be disrupted.

📈 Expected Graph

Enzyme activity rises to an optimum and then falls at higher temperatures.

49. 🧪 Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity

Each enzyme has a pH range in which it works effectively and usually has an optimum pH.

Very acidic or very alkaline conditions can reduce enzyme activity.

50. 🥔 Catalase Investigation

Catalase is an enzyme found in many living cells. It catalyses the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide.

Hydrogen peroxide → Water + Oxygen

The oxygen produced can be used as an indicator of enzyme activity.

51. 🫧 Measuring Gas Production

When a biological reaction produces a gas, gas production can be measured using an appropriate method.

For a fair comparison, keep important conditions constant.

  • amount of biological material;
  • concentration of solutions;
  • temperature;
  • reaction time;
  • volume of solutions.

52. 🦠 Microorganisms in Practical Biology

Microorganisms can be studied using teacher-approved prepared materials or controlled school practical activities.

Important factors affecting microbial growth include:

  • temperature;
  • nutrients;
  • water availability;
  • pH;
  • oxygen availability for organisms that require it.

⚠️ Safety

Unknown microorganisms should not be deliberately cultured or opened outside approved laboratory procedures.

53. 🧼 Aseptic Technique

Aseptic technique reduces contamination by unwanted microorganisms.

It can involve:

  • cleaning work surfaces;
  • using sterile equipment when required;
  • keeping containers closed;
  • avoiding unnecessary contact with sterile materials;
  • following correct laboratory hygiene.

54. 🌍 Ecological Fieldwork

Ecological practical work involves studying organisms and environmental factors in their habitats.

Common techniques include:

  • quadrats;
  • transects;
  • random sampling;
  • systematic sampling;
  • counting organisms;
  • measuring abiotic factors.

55. 🟩 Using a Quadrat

A quadrat is a square frame used to sample organisms in a particular area.

It is particularly useful for plants and organisms that do not move significantly.

🌿 Diagram Space — Quadrat Sampling

Suggested diagram: Quadrat placed randomly in a habitat.

56. 📍 Random Sampling

Random sampling reduces personal bias when selecting sampling locations.

A suitable random method should give sampling locations an appropriate chance of being selected.

57. 📏 Transects

A transect is a line or belt used to study changes in organisms across an environment.

For example, a transect can show how plant distribution changes from a shaded area to an open area.

🌱 Diagram Space — Transect

Suggested diagram: Line transect across a habitat.

58. 🌿 Frequency, Abundance and Distribution

Term Meaning
Frequency How often a species occurs in sampled areas.
Abundance How many individuals or how much of a species is present.
Distribution Where organisms are found within a habitat.

59. 🌡️ Measuring Environmental Factors

Ecologists can measure abiotic factors such as:

  • temperature;
  • light intensity;
  • humidity;
  • pH;
  • soil moisture;
  • wind speed.

60. ⚖️ Control Experiments

A control provides a comparison against which the experimental result can be judged.

For example, when investigating whether a substance affects seed germination, a group without the substance can act as the control.

⭐ Why is a Control Important?

A control helps determine whether the independent variable caused the observed change.

61. ⚙️ Fair Test

A fair test is an investigation where the independent variable is changed while important control variables are kept constant.

This makes the comparison between results more valid.

62. 🔄 Improving an Experiment

Ways to improve an investigation include:

  • repeat measurements;
  • increase sample size;
  • use more precise equipment;
  • control temperature;
  • control volumes and concentrations;
  • use an appropriate control;
  • reduce measurement errors;
  • use a wider range of independent-variable values.

63. ❌ Sources of Error

Possible sources of error include:

  • incorrect reading of a scale;
  • parallax error;
  • reaction-time error;
  • temperature fluctuations;
  • inconsistent sample sizes;
  • contamination;
  • limitations of measuring equipment.

64. 👁️ Parallax Error

Parallax error occurs when a scale is viewed from an incorrect angle.

It can be reduced by reading the scale at eye level where appropriate.

65. 📐 Resolution

Resolution refers to the smallest difference that an instrument can reliably distinguish or measure.

Choose equipment with suitable resolution for the investigation.

66. 📝 Writing a Conclusion

A conclusion should answer the aim and be supported by the results.

❌ Weak Conclusion

"The experiment worked."

✅ Better Conclusion

"The rate of photosynthesis increased as light intensity increased over the range tested, shown by the increase in oxygen production."

67. 📈 Identifying Relationships

Useful phrases include:

  • increases as;
  • decreases as;
  • remains approximately constant;
  • reaches a maximum;
  • reaches an optimum;
  • shows no clear relationship.

68. 🔬 Onion Cell Practical

Onion epidermal cells are commonly used to practise microscopy.

Structures that may be observed include:

  • cell wall;
  • cell membrane;
  • cytoplasm;
  • nucleus;
  • large vacuole.

A leaf epidermis can be examined to observe stomata and guard cells.

70. 🌱 Seed Germination Investigation

Seeds require suitable conditions for germination.

An investigation can test the importance of:

  • water;
  • oxygen;
  • suitable temperature.

71. 📊 Measuring Germination

Germination can be measured by recording:

  • number of seeds germinated;
  • percentage germination;
  • time taken to germinate;
  • root or shoot length.

Percentage germination = (Number germinated ÷ Total number of seeds) × 100

72. 🍞 Yeast Respiration Practical

Yeast can respire anaerobically when oxygen is limited and produce ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy

73. 🫀 Heart Rate Practical

Heart rate can be measured by counting pulse beats over a known period.

Heart rate per minute = Number of beats counted ÷ Time in minutes

If 36 beats are counted in 30 seconds:

36 × 2 = 72 beats per minute.

74. 🏃 Exercise Investigation

An investigation can compare pulse rate before exercise, immediately after exercise and during recovery.

Exercise increases the body's demand for oxygen and nutrients and increases the need to remove carbon dioxide.

75. 🫁 Breathing Rate Practical

Breathing rate can be determined by counting breaths over a measured period.

Exercise generally increases breathing rate and depth to increase gas exchange.

76. 🌡️ Temperature Control in Experiments

Temperature should be controlled when it could affect the rate of a biological process.

A water bath can provide controlled heating of samples.

77. 🧪 Concentration in Investigations

Concentration describes the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution.

When comparing different concentrations, all other important conditions should remain constant.

78. 🗝️ Biological Keys

A biological key is used to identify organisms using observable characteristics.

A dichotomous key uses pairs of contrasting statements.

  1. Start at the first pair of statements.
  2. Choose the statement that matches the organism.
  3. Follow the indicated number or letter.
  4. Continue until the organism is identified.

🌿 Diagram Space — Dichotomous Key

79. 📐 Estimating Population Size

Sampling can be used to estimate the number of organisms in a larger area.

The general principle is to sample a known area, count organisms and use the sample data to estimate the population in the larger area.

More representative samples generally improve the reliability of the estimate.

80. 🧮 Ratio in Practical Biology

Ratios can be used to compare biological quantities.

For example, if a sample contains 20 red blood cells and 5 white blood cells:

20 : 5 = 4 : 1

Ratios should normally be simplified to their simplest whole-number form.

81. 🔍 Identifying Specimens

Specimens can be identified using visible characteristics and biological identification keys.

Always base identification on the features that are actually observed.

82. 🧠 Practical Question Strategy

  1. Read the aim carefully.
  2. Identify the independent variable.

  4. Identify important control variables.

83. 🧪 Experimental Design

A well-designed experiment should include:

  • a clear aim;
  • a suitable hypothesis or prediction;
  • clearly identified variables;
  • a suitable method;
  • appropriate measurements;
  • a control where necessary;
  • repeated measurements;
  • careful recording of results.

84. 📊 Presenting Data

Biological data can be presented using:

  • tables;
  • bar charts;
  • line graphs;
  • scatter graphs;
  • labelled diagrams.

85. 🔬 Practical Accuracy

To improve accuracy:

  • use appropriate equipment;
  • read scales correctly;
  • use suitable measuring ranges;
  • avoid parallax errors;
  • measure carefully;
  • repeat measurements.

86. 🔁 Reliability

Reliability refers to the consistency of results.

Reliability can be improved by repeating measurements and using a larger sample where appropriate.

87. 🎯 Validity

An investigation is valid when it properly tests the question or aim it was designed to investigate.

Keeping control variables constant helps maintain a fair test.

88. 📋 Practical Data Table Rules

  • Give every column a clear heading.
  • Put units in the headings where appropriate.
  • Record values consistently.
  • Do not mix units within a column.
  • Use sensible decimal places.
  • Do not leave measured results unexplained.

91. 📝 Important Practical Definitions

Experiment: A practical investigation used to test an idea.

Variable: A factor that can change.

Independent variable: The factor deliberately changed.

Dependent variable: The factor measured.

Control variable: A factor kept constant.

Control: A comparison used to determine the effect of the independent variable.

Hypothesis: A testable prediction or explanation.

Observation: Something directly seen, measured or detected.

Inference: An explanation based on observations.

Conclusion: What the results show about the investigation.

Accuracy: Closeness to the true value.

Reliability: Consistency of results.

Validity: The extent to which an investigation tests what it is intended to test.

Anomaly: A result that does not fit the general pattern.

  • Putting the independent variable on the wrong axis.
  • Forgetting units.
  • Writing conclusions without using the results.
  • Confusing observations with explanations.
  • Failing to identify control variables.
  • Changing several variables at once.
  • Drawing specimens too small.
  • Shading biological drawings.
  • Using unclear labels.
  • Failing to repeat measurements.
  • Ignoring anomalous results.
  • Using the wrong formula.
  • Failing to convert units before calculating magnification.
