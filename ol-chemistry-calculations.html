🧮 Chemical Calculations

O/L Chemistry 0515 — Formulae, relative atomic mass, relative formula mass, moles, molar mass, concentration, percentage composition and chemical calculations.

1. 🧮 Introduction to Chemical Calculations

Chemical calculations allow chemists to determine quantities of substances involved in chemical reactions and compounds.

In O/L Chemistry, calculations are closely connected with chemical formulae, equations, relative atomic masses, moles, masses, concentrations and percentage composition.

🎯 Why Chemical Calculations Matter

  • To calculate the mass of a substance.
  • To calculate the number of moles present.
  • To determine the mass of a product formed.
  • To calculate solution concentration.
  • To determine the percentage of an element in a compound.
  • To determine empirical and molecular formulae.
  • To interpret quantitative information in chemical equations.

💡 Golden Rule

Always identify what is given, identify what is required, choose the correct formula, substitute the values, calculate carefully and include the correct unit.

2. ⚛️ Relative Atomic Mass (Aᵣ)

The relative atomic mass of an element is the average mass of its atoms compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

It is represented by Aᵣ.

📌 Important Point

Relative atomic mass has no unit.

Element Symbol Approximate Aᵣ
Hydrogen H 1
Carbon C 12
Nitrogen N 14
Oxygen O 16
Sodium Na 23
Magnesium Mg 24
Aluminium Al 27
Chlorine Cl 35.5
Potassium K 39
Calcium Ca 40
Iron Fe 56
Copper Cu 63.5
Zinc Zn 65

🧠 Remember

The values used in an examination should come from the periodic table or data provided by the examination.

3. 🧪 Relative Formula Mass (Mᵣ)

The relative formula mass of a substance is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in its chemical formula.

It is represented by Mᵣ.

Relative formula mass has no unit.

🔹 Basic Method

  1. Write the chemical formula.
  2. Count the atoms of each element.
  3. Multiply each number of atoms by its Aᵣ.
  4. Add the values together.

✏️ Worked Example: H₂O

H₂O contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.

Mᵣ = (2 × 1) + 16

Mᵣ = 18

✏️ Worked Example: CO₂

Mᵣ = 12 + (2 × 16)

Mᵣ = 44

✏️ Worked Example: CaCO₃

Mᵣ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16)

Mᵣ = 40 + 12 + 48

Mᵣ = 100

4. 🧩 Calculations Involving Brackets

When a formula contains brackets, the number outside the brackets multiplies every atom inside the brackets.

✏️ Worked Example: Ca(OH)₂

Ca(OH)₂ contains:

  • 1 calcium atom
  • 2 oxygen atoms
  • 2 hydrogen atoms

Mᵣ = 40 + (2 × 16) + (2 × 1)

Mᵣ = 40 + 32 + 2

Mᵣ = 74

✏️ Worked Example: Al₂(SO₄)₃

There are:

  • 2 aluminium atoms
  • 3 sulfur atoms
  • 12 oxygen atoms

Mᵣ = (2 × 27) + (3 × 32) + (12 × 16)

Mᵣ = 54 + 96 + 192

Mᵣ = 342

5. 🔢 The Mole

A mole is the amount of substance containing approximately 6.02 × 10²³ particles.

This number is called the Avogadro constant.

🧠 Avogadro Constant

1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles

The particles may be atoms, molecules, ions or other specified chemical particles.

Substance Particles counted
Helium Atoms
Oxygen gas O₂ molecules
Water H₂O molecules
Sodium ions Na⁺ ions

6. ⚖️ Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.

Its unit is usually g mol⁻¹.

📌 Relationship

The numerical value of the molar mass in g mol⁻¹ is equal to the numerical value of the relative formula mass.

Example:

Mᵣ of H₂O = 18

Therefore, molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹.

7. ⚖️ Calculating Moles from Mass

📐 Formula

Number of moles = mass ÷ molar mass

n = m ÷ M

Where:

  • n = number of moles
  • m = mass in grams
  • M = molar mass in g mol⁻¹

✏️ Worked Example

Calculate the number of moles in 36 g of water, H₂O.

Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹

n = 36 ÷ 18

n = 2 mol

8. ⚖️ Calculating Mass from Moles

📐 Formula

Mass = number of moles × molar mass

m = n × M

✏️ Worked Example

Calculate the mass of 0.5 mol of sodium chloride, NaCl.

Molar mass of NaCl:

23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g mol⁻¹

m = 0.5 × 58.5

m = 29.25 g

9. 🔬 Moles and Number of Particles

The number of particles can be calculated using Avogadro's constant.

📐 Formulae

Number of particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³

Moles = number of particles ÷ 6.02 × 10²³

✏️ Worked Example

Calculate the number of molecules in 2 mol of oxygen gas.

Number of molecules =

2 × 6.02 × 10²³

= 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

10. ⚗️ Mole Ratios from Chemical Equations

The coefficients in a balanced chemical equation show the mole ratio between the substances.

🧪 Example

Word equation:

Hydrogen + oxygen → water

Symbol equation:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Substance Coefficient Mole relationship
H₂ 2 2 mol
O₂ 1 1 mol
H₂O 2 2 mol

💡 Exam Tip

Always balance the equation before using its coefficients as mole ratios.

11. 🔄 Calculations Using Chemical Equations

Stoichiometric calculations use the mole ratio from a balanced chemical equation to determine the amount of another substance.

🪜 General Method

  1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
  2. Calculate the moles of the substance given.
  3. Use the equation to obtain the mole ratio.
  4. Calculate the moles of the required substance.
  5. Convert the moles into the required quantity.

✏️ Worked Example

Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide produced when 12 g of magnesium reacts completely with oxygen.

Word equation:

Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Symbol equation:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Molar mass of Mg = 24 g mol⁻¹

Moles of Mg = 12 ÷ 24 = 0.5 mol

From:

2Mg → 2MgO

Mg : MgO = 1 : 1

Therefore moles of MgO = 0.5 mol.

Molar mass of MgO = 24 + 16 = 40 g mol⁻¹

Mass = 0.5 × 40

Mass of MgO = 20 g

12. 📊 Percentage Composition of a Compound

Percentage composition shows the percentage by mass of each element present in a compound.

📐 Formula

Percentage of element = (mass of element in one mole of compound ÷ molar mass of compound) × 100

✏️ Worked Example: Water

H₂O has a molar mass of 18.

Mass of hydrogen = 2 × 1 = 2

Percentage of hydrogen:

(2 ÷ 18) × 100 = 11.1%

Mass of oxygen = 16

Percentage of oxygen:

(16 ÷ 18) × 100 = 88.9%

Total = 11.1% + 88.9% = 100%

13. 🧩 Empirical Formula

The empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

🪜 Steps for Finding an Empirical Formula

  1. Write the masses or percentages of the elements.
  2. Divide each value by the corresponding Aᵣ.
  3. Find the simplest whole-number ratio.
  4. Write the empirical formula.

✏️ Worked Example

A compound contains 12 g of carbon and 4 g of hydrogen. Determine its empirical formula.

Carbon:

12 ÷ 12 = 1

Hydrogen:

4 ÷ 1 = 4

Ratio = 1 : 4

Empirical formula = CH₄

⚠️ Important

Do not simply divide the given masses by the smallest mass. You must first divide each mass by its relative atomic mass.

14. 🧬 Molecular Formula

The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a compound.

📐 Relationship

Molecular formula = empirical formula × whole number

The whole number is found using:

n = molecular mass ÷ empirical formula mass

✏️ Worked Example

A compound has an empirical formula CH₂ and a molecular mass of 56.

Empirical formula mass:

12 + (2 × 1) = 14

n = 56 ÷ 14 = 4

Multiply CH₂ by 4:

Molecular formula = C₄H₈

15. 🧪 Concentration of Solutions

Concentration describes the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution.

💧 Concentration in g dm⁻³

📐 Formula

Concentration = mass of solute ÷ volume of solution

C = m ÷ V

If mass is in grams and volume is in dm³, concentration is expressed in g dm⁻³.

✏️ Worked Example

10 g of sodium chloride is dissolved to make 2 dm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration.

C = 10 ÷ 2

C = 5 g dm⁻³

🧪 Concentration in mol dm⁻³

📐 Formula

Concentration = moles ÷ volume

C = n ÷ V

The volume must be in dm³.

⚠️ Volume Conversion

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³

Therefore:

1 cm³ = 0.001 dm³

To convert cm³ to dm³:

divide by 1000

To convert dm³ to cm³:

multiply by 1000

16. 🔄 Concentration, Moles and Mass

Many examination questions require more than one calculation. The three important relationships are:

📐 The Three Key Relationships

n = m ÷ M

C = n ÷ V

m = n × M

✏️ Worked Example

5.85 g of NaCl is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate its concentration in mol dm⁻³.

Step 1: Calculate molar mass of NaCl.

M = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g mol⁻¹

Step 2: Calculate moles.

n = 5.85 ÷ 58.5

n = 0.10 mol

Step 3: Convert volume.

500 cm³ = 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.500 dm³

Step 4: Calculate concentration.

C = 0.10 ÷ 0.500

C = 0.20 mol dm⁻³

17. 💨 Gas Volume Calculations

Gas volumes can be related to the amount of gas present using the molar gas volume specified by the examination or question.

📌 Important Examination Rule

Always use the molar gas volume stated in the question or provided data. Do not automatically assume a value if the question gives a different condition.

📐 General Relationship

Number of moles = gas volume ÷ molar gas volume

Therefore:

Gas volume = number of moles × molar gas volume

✏️ Worked Example

A question states that the molar gas volume under its specified conditions is 24 dm³ mol⁻¹. Calculate the volume occupied by 0.5 mol of a gas.

Volume = 0.5 × 24

Volume = 12 dm³

18. ⚗️ Gas Calculations from Chemical Equations

When gases react, a balanced equation gives the mole relationship between the gases.

🧪 Example

Word equation:

Hydrogen + oxygen → water

Symbol equation:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The gaseous reactants have a mole ratio:

H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1

Under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, the gas-volume ratio follows the same coefficient ratio.

19. 📈 Percentage Yield

The actual amount of product obtained in an experiment may be less than the theoretical amount expected.

📐 Formula

Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100

✏️ Worked Example

The theoretical yield of a product is 20 g, but only 16 g is obtained experimentally.

Percentage yield:

(16 ÷ 20) × 100

= 80%

🧠 Why Can Yield Be Less Than 100%?

  • The reaction may not go to completion.
  • Some reactants may undergo side reactions.
  • Some product may be lost during separation.
  • The product may remain dissolved or attached to apparatus.

20. 🧪 Percentage Purity

Percentage purity indicates the proportion of a sample that is actually the desired substance.

📐 Formula

Percentage purity = (mass of pure substance ÷ mass of impure sample) × 100

✏️ Worked Example

A 50 g sample contains 45 g of a pure substance. Calculate its percentage purity.

Purity = (45 ÷ 50) × 100

Purity = 90%

21. ⚖️ Reacting Masses

Reacting-mass calculations determine how much of one substance reacts with or is produced from another substance.

🪜 Reliable Method

  1. Write the word equation.
  2. Write and balance the symbol equation.
  3. Calculate the molar mass of the known substance.
  4. Convert its mass to moles.
  5. Use the mole ratio.
  6. Convert the required moles into mass.

🧪 Example Reaction

Word equation:

Calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

The mole ratio is:

CaCO₃ : CaO : CO₂ = 1 : 1 : 1

22. 🚦 Limiting Reactant — Basic Idea

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely used up first and therefore limits the amount of product that can be formed.

🧠 Simple Example

Imagine that one product requires:

2 units of A + 1 unit of B → 1 unit of product

If there are only enough A particles for two products but enough B particles for five products, A limits the reaction.

For more advanced numerical limiting-reactant calculations, compare the available mole amounts with the coefficients in the balanced equation.

23. 🔺 Useful Calculation Relationships

🧮 Mass–Mole Relationship

Mass = moles × molar mass

Moles = mass ÷ molar mass

Molar mass = mass ÷ moles

🧪 Concentration–Mole Relationship

Concentration = moles ÷ volume

Moles = concentration × volume

Volume = moles ÷ concentration

26. 🎯 Accuracy and Significant Figures

When a question gives a required number of decimal places or significant figures, follow that instruction for the final answer.

🔹 Decimal Places

Decimal places count the digits after the decimal point.

Example:

4.376 to 2 decimal places = 4.38

🔹 Significant Figures

Significant figures indicate the meaningful digits in a measured or calculated value.

💡 Exam Tip

Do not round intermediate calculations unnecessarily. Keep extra digits during working and round the final answer as instructed.

27. ⚗️ Chemical Equations in Calculations

A balanced equation is essential because it gives the correct quantitative relationship between reacting substances.

🧪 Example

Word equation:

Zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Mole ratio:

Zn : HCl : ZnCl₂ : H₂ = 1 : 2 : 1 : 1

🧠 Important

Never use an unbalanced equation for quantitative calculations. The coefficients must represent the correct mole ratio.

28. ⚠️ Common Calculation Mistakes

Mistake Correct Approach
Using an unbalanced equation Balance the equation first.
Forgetting brackets in a formula Multiply every atom inside the bracket.
Using mass directly as moles Use n = m ÷ M.
Using cm³ as dm³ Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.
Forgetting units Write the correct unit with the answer.
Rounding too early Keep useful digits until the final answer.
Using the wrong Aᵣ Check the periodic table/data provided.
Ignoring mole ratios Use the coefficients of the balanced equation.

🧠 Important Definitions

  • Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ): The average mass of atoms of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • Relative formula mass (Mᵣ): The sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms represented in a chemical formula.
  • Mole: An amount of substance containing approximately 6.02 × 10²³ particles.
  • Avogadro constant: The number of particles in one mole, approximately 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  • Molar mass: The mass of one mole of a substance.
  • Concentration: The amount of solute present in a given volume of solution.
  • Empirical formula: The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
  • Molecular formula: The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a molecular substance.
  • Percentage composition: The percentage by mass of an element present in a compound.
  • Percentage yield: The actual yield expressed as a percentage of the theoretical yield.
  • Percentage purity: The percentage of a sample that consists of the desired pure substance.
  • Stoichiometry: The quantitative relationship between substances involved in a chemical reaction.
  • Limiting reactant: The reactant that is completely used up first and limits the amount of product formed.
