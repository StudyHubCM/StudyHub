🧬 Molecular & Cell Biology

A/L Biology — 0710 | Cell ultrastructure, biological molecules, membranes, enzymes, cell division, DNA, RNA, genetics and biotechnology.

🔬 1. Cell Structure & Ultrastructure

At A/L level, cell biology goes beyond identifying organelles. You must understand the ultrastructure of cells, how organelles are organised, how they function, and how their structure is related to their specialised roles.

🧠 What is ultrastructure?

Ultrastructure refers to the fine structural details of cells and organelles that can be observed using an electron microscope.

Electron microscopes have much greater resolving power than light microscopes, allowing structures such as ribosomes, membranes and internal organelle membranes to be studied.

🔬 Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

Feature Prokaryotic cell Eukaryotic cell
Nucleus No membrane-bound nucleus True nucleus enclosed by nuclear envelope
DNA Mainly circular DNA in nucleoid region Linear chromosomes associated with proteins
Membrane-bound organelles Absent Present
Ribosomes 70S 80S in cytoplasm
Cell size Usually smaller Usually larger
Examples Bacteria and archaea Animals, plants, fungi and protists

🧫 Major Eukaryotic Organelles

Organelle Structure Main function
Nucleus Double membrane with nuclear pores; contains chromosomes Stores genetic information and controls gene expression
Nucleolus Dense region inside nucleus Produces rRNA and assembles ribosomal subunits
Ribosome rRNA and protein; two subunits Protein synthesis
Rough ER Membrane network with ribosomes Synthesis and transport of proteins
Smooth ER Membrane network without ribosomes Lipid synthesis and other metabolic functions
Golgi apparatus Stacked flattened membrane sacs Modification, sorting and packaging of cell products
Mitochondrion Double membrane; inner membrane forms cristae Aerobic respiration and ATP production
Chloroplast Double membrane; thylakoids and stroma Photosynthesis
Lysosome Membrane-bound vesicle containing hydrolytic enzymes Intracellular digestion and recycling
Vacuole Membrane-bound compartment Storage and, in plants, maintenance of turgor
Cytoskeleton Protein filaments Shape, movement, intracellular transport and cell division

🧬 Endosymbiotic Theory

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from free-living prokaryotic organisms that became incorporated into ancestral eukaryotic cells.

Evidence includes:

  • They contain their own circular DNA.
  • They contain ribosomes similar to bacterial ribosomes.
  • They reproduce by a process resembling binary fission.
  • They possess double membranes.
  • They are similar in size to many bacteria.

🧪 2. Biological Molecules

Biological molecules are substances involved in the structure and functioning of living organisms. At A/L level, emphasis is placed on their chemical structure, properties, biological roles and reactions.

🍞 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They include monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides.

Group Examples Importance
Monosaccharides Glucose, fructose, galactose Immediate energy and building blocks
Disaccharides Maltose, sucrose, lactose Transport and dietary carbohydrates
Polysaccharides Starch, glycogen, cellulose Energy storage and structural support

🌱 Starch, Glycogen and Cellulose

  • Starch is the major storage carbohydrate in plants.
  • Glycogen is an important storage carbohydrate in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose forms an important structural component of plant cell walls.

Their different properties arise partly from differences in the bonds and arrangement of glucose units.

🥩 Proteins

Proteins are polymers made from amino acids joined by peptide bonds.

Amino acids contain an amino group, a carboxyl group and a variable R group.

🔗 Protein Structure

Primary structure: amino acid sequence.

Secondary structure: local folding such as α-helices and β-sheets.

Tertiary structure: overall three-dimensional folding of one polypeptide.

Quaternary structure: association of two or more polypeptide chains.

🧈 Lipids

Lipids include triglycerides, phospholipids and steroids. They are generally insoluble or poorly soluble in water.

  • Triglycerides provide concentrated energy storage.
  • Phospholipids are major components of cell membranes.
  • Some lipids function as hormones or signalling molecules.

🧬 Nucleic Acids

DNA and RNA are nucleic acids made from nucleotide subunits. Each nucleotide contains a pentose sugar, phosphate group and nitrogenous base.

Feature DNA RNA
Sugar Deoxyribose Ribose
Bases A, T, C, G A, U, C, G
Structure Usually double-stranded Usually single-stranded
Main role Stores hereditary information Functions in gene expression and protein synthesis

💧 Condensation and Hydrolysis

A condensation reaction joins molecules together while releasing water. A hydrolysis reaction breaks a bond using water.

🧪 Biological Test Reminder

A/L students should know the principles and observations of standard food tests, including tests for reducing sugars, starch, proteins and lipids.

🚪 3. Membranes & Transport

The cell surface membrane separates the internal environment of the cell from its surroundings and controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell.

🧬 Fluid Mosaic Model

The membrane is described by the fluid mosaic model. It contains a phospholipid bilayer with proteins embedded within or associated with it.

  • Phospholipids form the basic bilayer.
  • Hydrophilic phosphate heads face aqueous environments.
  • Hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inward.
  • Integral proteins may span the membrane.
  • Peripheral proteins are associated with membrane surfaces.
  • Cholesterol affects membrane stability and fluidity.
  • Carbohydrate chains occur on some membrane proteins and lipids.

🚪 Membrane Transport Mechanisms

Process Energy required? Direction Example
Simple diffusion No Down concentration gradient Movement of small lipid-soluble molecules
Facilitated diffusion No Down electrochemical gradient Transport through channel or carrier proteins
Osmosis No direct ATP requirement Down water potential gradient Water movement across selectively permeable membranes
Active transport Yes Against electrochemical gradient Ion uptake by cells
Endocytosis Yes Material enters in vesicles Uptake of large particles or molecules
Exocytosis Yes Material leaves in vesicles Secretion of proteins

💧 Water Potential

Water potential describes the tendency of water molecules to move. Water moves from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a selectively permeable membrane.

Pure water has a water potential of zero under the standard reference convention. Solutes lower water potential, making it more negative.

🌱 Turgor and Plasmolysis

  • Turgid cell: water enters and the cell contents press against the cell wall.
  • Flaccid cell: the cell has lost some water and turgor pressure.
  • Plasmolysis: the cell surface membrane pulls away from the cell wall after substantial water loss.

⚡ 4. Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being permanently consumed.

🔑 Enzyme Specificity

An enzyme has an active site whose shape and chemical properties allow particular substrate molecules to bind.

The traditional lock-and-key model illustrates specificity, while the induced-fit model explains that binding can cause changes in enzyme conformation that help catalysis.

⚙️ Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Factor Effect
Temperature Rate usually increases to an optimum, then decreases sharply if enzyme structure is disrupted.
pH Each enzyme has a suitable pH range; extreme pH can alter bonding and active-site shape.
Substrate concentration Rate rises until enzyme active sites become effectively saturated.
Enzyme concentration Increasing enzyme concentration can increase rate when substrate is available in sufficient quantity.
Inhibitors Reduce enzyme activity by interfering with substrate binding or catalytic activity.

🧪 Competitive and Non-competitive Inhibition

Feature Competitive inhibition Non-competitive inhibition
Binding Inhibitor competes for the active site Inhibitor binds at another site
Active-site effect Substrate cannot bind while inhibitor occupies the site Binding can alter enzyme conformation
Effect of increasing substrate May reduce the inhibitor's effect Does not generally overcome inhibition in the same way

📈 Enzyme Rate Curves

A graph of reaction rate against substrate concentration typically rises rapidly at low substrate concentration and approaches a maximum rate as enzyme active sites become saturated.

🔄 5. Mitosis & Meiosis

🔬 Mitosis

Mitosis is nuclear division that produces two genetically similar daughter nuclei. It is important for growth, tissue repair and asexual reproduction in some organisms.

The main stages are:

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense and the spindle develops.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes align at the equator.
  • Anaphase: sister chromatids separate.
  • Telophase: chromosomes reach opposite poles and nuclei reform.
  • Cytokinesis: cytoplasm divides to form daughter cells.

🧬 Meiosis

Meiosis is a specialised form of cell division that produces haploid cells and increases genetic variation.

It consists of two nuclear divisions: meiosis I and meiosis II.

  • Homologous chromosomes pair during prophase I.
  • Crossing over can occur between non-sister chromatids.
  • Homologous chromosomes separate during anaphase I.
  • Sister chromatids separate during anaphase II.
  • Four haploid cells are produced from one starting cell.

🧬 Sources of Genetic Variation During Meiosis

  • Crossing over produces new combinations of alleles.
  • Independent assortment creates different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • Random fertilisation provides an additional source of variation.
Feature Mitosis Meiosis
Number of divisions One Two
Daughter cells Usually two Usually four
Chromosome number Maintained Halved
Genetic similarity Usually genetically similar Genetically variable
Crossing over Normally absent Occurs during prophase I
Main biological role Growth, repair and cell replacement Production of haploid reproductive cells and genetic variation

🧬 6. DNA, RNA & Protein Synthesis

🧬 DNA Structure

DNA is composed of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands forming a double helix. The strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.

  • Adenine pairs with thymine.
  • Cytosine pairs with guanine.
  • Nucleotides within each strand are joined by phosphodiester bonds.
  • The sugar-phosphate backbone forms the outside of each strand.

🔁 DNA Replication

DNA replication is semi-conservative, meaning each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesised strand.

  1. DNA strands separate.
  2. Each original strand acts as a template.
  3. Free nucleotides pair with exposed bases.
  4. DNA polymerase joins nucleotides into new strands.
  5. Two DNA molecules are produced.

🧪 Important Replication Enzymes

Enzyme Role
Helicase Separates the DNA strands by disrupting hydrogen bonding.
DNA polymerase Adds DNA nucleotides to a growing strand and participates in proofreading.
Primase Produces RNA primers needed for DNA synthesis.
DNA ligase Joins DNA fragments in the newly synthesised strand.

📝 Transcription

Transcription is the synthesis of RNA using a DNA template. RNA polymerase forms an RNA strand complementary to the template DNA strand.

In eukaryotic cells, the initial RNA transcript is processed before mature mRNA leaves the nucleus.

🏭 Translation

Translation is the process by which ribosomes use the nucleotide sequence of mRNA to assemble a polypeptide.

  • mRNA carries the codon sequence.
  • tRNA carries amino acids.
  • Each tRNA has an anticodon complementary to an mRNA codon.
  • Ribosomes coordinate codon recognition and peptide-bond formation.

🔤 Genetic Code

The genetic code is generally:

  • Triplet: three bases form a codon.
  • Degenerate: several codons can specify the same amino acid.
  • Non-overlapping: each base is read as part of one codon in a reading frame.
  • Nearly universal: most organisms use the same basic code, with some exceptions.
  • Specific: each codon specifies one amino acid or a stop signal.

🧬 7. Genetics & Variation

Genetics is the study of heredity and variation. At A/L level, genetic analysis requires understanding genes, alleles, chromosomes, inheritance patterns and sources of variation.

🧬 Key Genetic Terms

Term Meaning
Gene A DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product or trait.
Allele An alternative form of a gene at a particular locus.
Locus The position of a gene or DNA sequence on a chromosome.
Genotype The genetic constitution of an organism for the genes being considered.
Phenotype Observable characteristics resulting from genotype and environmental influences.
Homozygous Having two identical alleles at a locus.
Heterozygous Having two different alleles at a locus.

🌱 Mendelian Inheritance

Mendelian genetics examines inheritance patterns using principles such as segregation and independent assortment.

During gamete formation, paired alleles segregate so that each gamete receives one allele of a gene.

🧮 Genetic Crosses

Students should be able to construct and interpret monohybrid and, where required, dihybrid crosses, including genotype and phenotype ratios.

🧪 Exam Method

  1. Identify the alleles.
  2. Determine the parental genotypes.
  3. Determine possible gametes.
  4. Construct the cross.
  5. Determine offspring genotypes.
  6. Convert genotype information into phenotype information.
  7. State ratios or probabilities clearly.

🧬 Variation

Variation refers to differences between individuals of the same species. It can arise from genetic and environmental causes.

  • Mutation creates new genetic variants.
  • Crossing over creates new combinations of alleles.
  • Independent assortment creates different chromosome combinations.
  • Random fertilisation contributes further variation.
  • Environmental factors can influence phenotype.

🧬 Mutation

A mutation is a change in genetic material. Mutations may involve a single base or larger sections of DNA and chromosomes.

Mutation type General description
Substitution One base is replaced by another.
Insertion One or more bases are added.
Deletion One or more bases are removed.
Frameshift An insertion or deletion changes the reading frame when the number of bases involved is not a multiple of three.

🧬 Chromosomal Variation

Changes in chromosome number or structure can affect phenotype and development. Examples include nondisjunction, deletion, duplication, inversion and translocation.

🧫 8. Biotechnology & Genetic Engineering

Biotechnology uses biological organisms, cells or molecules to produce useful products or processes. Modern biotechnology increasingly uses molecular genetics.

🧬 Recombinant DNA Technology

Recombinant DNA technology involves combining DNA sequences from different sources to create a new DNA molecule.

✂️ Restriction Enzymes

Restriction endonucleases recognise particular DNA sequences and cut DNA at specific sites. Some produce sticky ends, which can facilitate the joining of complementary DNA fragments.

🧪 DNA Ligase

DNA ligase joins DNA fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds in the DNA backbone.

🧬 Plasmids

Plasmids are small circular DNA molecules commonly found in bacteria. They can replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome and are widely used as vectors in genetic engineering.

🧫 Basic Genetic Engineering Sequence

  1. Identify the desired gene.
  2. Obtain or amplify the DNA containing the desired sequence.
  3. Cut DNA and vector DNA using appropriate restriction enzymes.
  4. Join the DNA fragment to the vector using DNA ligase.
  5. Introduce the recombinant DNA into suitable host cells.
  6. Select or identify cells containing the desired construct.
  7. Allow expression or replication of the inserted genetic material as required.

🧬 Polymerase Chain Reaction — PCR

PCR is used to amplify a selected DNA sequence, producing many copies from a small starting amount of DNA.

PCR stage What happens?
Denaturation DNA strands separate.
Annealing Primers bind to complementary target sequences.
Extension A thermostable DNA polymerase extends the primers by adding nucleotides.

🧬 Gel Electrophoresis

Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments according to their movement through a gel under an electric field. DNA fragments are negatively charged and migrate towards the positive electrode.

Smaller DNA fragments generally move through the gel more easily and therefore travel farther than larger fragments under the same conditions.

🌾 Applications of Biotechnology

Area Example application

⚖️ Ethical Considerations

Biotechnology raises scientific, environmental, economic and ethical questions. Important considerations include safety, environmental effects, ownership of genetic information, access to technologies and responsible use of genetic engineering.

🔗 9. How Molecular & Cell Biology Connects

These topics are strongly interconnected. A/L questions often combine several areas rather than testing each concept in isolation.

Starting concept Connection
DNA Contains genetic information used in gene expression.
Gene expression DNA information is used to produce functional RNA or proteins.
Proteins Many proteins act as enzymes, receptors, transporters and structural components.
Enzymes Control metabolic reactions by lowering activation energy.
Membranes Control transport and allow communication between cells and their surroundings.
Cell division Allows growth and reproduction while maintaining or reducing chromosome number as appropriate.
Mutation Can alter DNA and potentially affect proteins and phenotype.
Biotechnology Uses molecular and cellular knowledge for practical applications.

🧠 Big Picture

DNA → RNA → Protein → Cell function → Organism phenotype

Changes at the molecular level can therefore influence cellular processes and, in some circumstances, observable characteristics of an organism.

⚠️ 12. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ❌ Saying that all cells have a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • ❌ Confusing cell wall with cell surface membrane.
  • ❌ Saying mitochondria produce energy rather than explaining ATP production through respiration.
  • ❌ Confusing diffusion with active transport.
  • ❌ Describing osmosis simply as movement of water from high to low concentration without specifying water potential.
  • ❌ Saying enzymes are used up during reactions.
  • ❌ Confusing homologous chromosomes with sister chromatids.
  • ❌ Saying meiosis produces genetically identical cells.
  • ❌ Confusing DNA replication with transcription.
  • ❌ Confusing codons with anticodons.
  • ❌ Treating genotype and phenotype as the same thing.
  • ❌ Assuming every mutation is harmful.
  • ❌ Confusing restriction enzymes with DNA ligase.
  • ❌ Saying PCR separates DNA fragments rather than amplifying a target sequence.
  • ❌ Forgetting that smaller DNA fragments generally migrate farther through a gel.

🏁 15. Final A/L Takeaway

🧬 Molecular & Cell Biology in One Picture

Cells are built from organised structures and biological molecules. Their membranes regulate exchange with the environment, while enzymes control biochemical reactions.

DNA stores genetic information. DNA can replicate and its information can be expressed through RNA and protein synthesis.

Mitosis supports growth and cell replacement, while meiosis produces haploid cells and contributes to genetic variation.

Mutations and genetic recombination contribute to variation, while modern biotechnology allows scientists to analyse, amplify and manipulate genetic material for useful applications.

🎯 Mastering this unit means understanding how molecular events produce cellular processes, and how cellular processes contribute to the biology of the whole organism.
