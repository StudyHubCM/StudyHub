🫀 Animal & Human Physiology

A/L Biology — 0710 | Circulation, blood, gas exchange, digestion, excretion, homeostasis, nervous coordination, receptors, hormones, movement and human reproduction.

1. ❤️ Circulation

🫀 What is circulation?

Circulation is the transport of substances around an organism. In humans, the circulatory system transports oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, metabolic wastes, heat and immune components.

Humans have a closed double circulatory system. Blood remains inside blood vessels and passes through the heart twice during one complete circuit.

🔄 Double circulation

Circulation Route Main function
Pulmonary circulation Heart → lungs → heart Gas exchange and oxygenation of blood
Systemic circulation Heart → body tissues → heart Supplies tissues and removes wastes

🫀 Structure of the human heart

  • Right atrium: receives deoxygenated blood from the venae cavae.
  • Right ventricle: pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
  • Left atrium: receives oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins.
  • Left ventricle: pumps oxygenated blood to the whole body.
  • Valves: prevent backflow of blood.
  • Septum: separates the left and right sides.
  • Coronary arteries: supply the heart muscle with oxygen and nutrients.

🚪 Major blood vessels

Blood vessel Direction of blood flow Important example
Artery Away from the heart Aorta
Vein Towards the heart Vena cava
Capillary Connects arterioles and venules Tissue capillaries

💓 Cardiac cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events occurring during one complete heartbeat.

  1. Atrial systole: atria contract and push blood into the ventricles.
  2. Ventricular systole: ventricles contract and eject blood into arteries.
  3. Diastole: heart muscle relaxes and chambers fill with blood.

⚡ Electrical control of the heart

The heartbeat is coordinated by electrical activity within cardiac muscle. The sinoatrial node (SAN) acts as the natural pacemaker. Electrical excitation spreads through the atria and reaches the atrioventricular node (AVN), followed by conduction through the conducting fibres to coordinate ventricular contraction.

📈 Exam focus

Be able to explain why the left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall: it must generate greater pressure to pump blood through the systemic circulation.

2. 🩸 Blood

🩸 Blood as a transport tissue

Blood consists of plasma and formed cellular components: red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

Component Structure / feature Main function
Red blood cells Biconcave, flexible, lack a nucleus when mature Transport oxygen using haemoglobin
White blood cells Nucleated cells with different specialised forms Defence against pathogens
Platelets Small cell fragments Blood clotting
Plasma Liquid component of blood Transports dissolved substances and distributes heat

🔴 Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a protein containing iron that binds oxygen reversibly. In the lungs, where oxygen concentration is high, haemoglobin combines readily with oxygen. In respiring tissues, oxygen is released.

The reversible reaction can be represented as:

Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin

📊 Oxygen dissociation curve

The oxygen dissociation curve shows the relationship between oxygen partial pressure and the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen.

The curve is sigmoidal because binding of one oxygen molecule changes haemoglobin's affinity for subsequent oxygen molecules.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert a labelled oxygen dissociation curve showing oxygen partial pressure on the x-axis and percentage haemoglobin saturation on the y-axis.

🛡️ Defence by white blood cells

  • Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens.
  • Lymphocytes are involved in specific immune responses.
  • Antibodies bind specifically to foreign antigens.
  • Memory cells can allow a faster response after later exposure to the same antigen.

🩹 Blood clotting

Blood clotting prevents excessive blood loss and helps protect damaged tissue from entry by microorganisms.

Platelets contribute to a cascade of reactions that leads to conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin. Fibrin forms a mesh that helps trap blood cells and form a clot.

3. 🫁 Respiration & Gas Exchange

🌬️ Gas exchange

Gas exchange is the movement of respiratory gases between an organism and its environment, or between respiratory surfaces and the blood.

In humans, gas exchange occurs mainly in the alveoli of the lungs.

🫁 Adaptations of alveoli

  • Very large surface area.
  • Walls are only one cell thick.
  • Rich capillary supply.
  • Moist surface for gases to dissolve.
  • Continuous ventilation maintains concentration gradients.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert a labelled diagram showing an alveolus surrounded by capillaries, with oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusion directions.

💨 Ventilation

Stage Main events
Inspiration Diaphragm contracts and flattens; external intercostal muscles contract; thoracic volume increases and pressure falls.
Expiration At rest, diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax; thoracic volume decreases and pressure rises.

⚡ Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration releases energy from organic molecules using oxygen. In simplified form:

Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy

Most ATP production during aerobic respiration occurs through oxidative phosphorylation associated with the electron transport chain in mitochondria.

🔥 Anaerobic respiration in humans

During vigorous exercise, oxygen delivery may become insufficient to meet the immediate demand for aerobic ATP production. Anaerobic glycolysis can provide ATP rapidly, producing lactate.

Lactate is subsequently processed when oxygen availability improves.

🏃 Exercise and respiration

  • Breathing rate increases.
  • Depth of breathing increases.
  • Heart rate increases.
  • More blood is directed to active muscles.
  • Oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal increase.

4. 🍽️ Digestion & Absorption

🍴 Digestion

Digestion is the mechanical and chemical breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into smaller soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

🧪 Main digestive enzymes

Enzyme Substrate Products
Amylase Starch Maltose and shorter carbohydrates
Maltase Maltose Glucose
Proteases Proteins / polypeptides Smaller peptides and amino acids
Lipases Triglycerides Fatty acids and monoglycerides

🧪 Role of the liver and pancreas

  • Liver: produces bile and performs many metabolic functions.
  • Bile: neutralises acidic chyme and emulsifies fats.
  • Pancreas: releases digestive enzymes and alkaline pancreatic juice into the small intestine.

🧬 Absorption in the small intestine

The small intestine is specialised for absorption. Its lining contains numerous villi and microvilli, greatly increasing surface area.

  • Thin epithelial barrier.
  • Large surface area.
  • Dense capillary network.
  • Lacteals for absorption of products of lipid digestion.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert a labelled villus showing the epithelium, blood capillaries, lacteal and directions of nutrient absorption.

🚚 Transport of absorbed nutrients

Glucose and amino acids enter blood capillaries and are transported through the hepatic portal system to the liver before entering the general circulation. Many products of lipid digestion are incorporated into chylomicrons and transported through the lymphatic system before entering the bloodstream.

5. 💧 Excretion & Osmoregulation

🚮 Excretion

Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste products and substances present in excess from the body.

Major excretory products include carbon dioxide, nitrogenous wastes and excess water and mineral ions.

🫘 The kidney

The kidneys remove nitrogenous waste and regulate the water and ion content of the internal environment.

🔬 Nephron

Region Main function
Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule Ultrafiltration of blood
Proximal convoluted tubule Selective reabsorption of useful substances and water
Loop of Henle Establishes a medullary osmotic gradient important for water conservation
Distal convoluted tubule Further regulation of ions and pH
Collecting duct Regulates final water reabsorption and urine concentration

💧 Ultrafiltration

High blood pressure in the glomerulus causes small molecules and water to pass into Bowman's capsule. Large plasma proteins and blood cells are normally retained in the blood.

♻️ Selective reabsorption

Useful substances such as glucose, amino acids and much of the filtered water and ions are reabsorbed from the nephron into the blood.

💧 ADH and water balance

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) increases the water permeability of collecting ducts by promoting insertion of aquaporin water channels. More water is therefore reabsorbed into the blood and urine becomes more concentrated.

When the body has excess water, ADH secretion decreases and less water is reabsorbed.

6. 🌡️ Homeostasis

🏠 Definition

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite changes in internal or external conditions.

🔁 Negative feedback

Negative feedback detects a change and activates responses that oppose the original change, helping restore a variable towards its normal range.

🌡️ Thermoregulation

Condition Response Effect
Body temperature too high Vasodilation and increased sweating Greater heat loss
Body temperature too low Vasoconstriction, shivering and reduced sweating Reduced heat loss and increased heat production

🍬 Blood glucose regulation

Blood glucose concentration is regulated mainly by the hormones insulin and glucagon, secreted by the pancreas.

  • High blood glucose: insulin promotes uptake of glucose and storage as glycogen.
  • Low blood glucose: glucagon promotes processes that increase blood glucose, including glycogen breakdown.

🔄 Negative feedback pathway

Stimulus → receptor/sensor → control centre → effector → corrective response → variable moves towards the set range.

7. 🧠 Nervous Coordination

Nervous coordination allows organisms to detect changes, process information and produce rapid responses.

🧠 Neurones

Type Function
Sensory neurone Carries impulses from receptors towards the central nervous system.
Relay neurone Connects neurones within the central nervous system.
Motor neurone Carries impulses from the central nervous system to effectors.

🧠 Central and peripheral nervous systems

  • Central nervous system (CNS): brain and spinal cord.
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): nerves connecting the CNS with receptors, muscles and glands.

⚡ Reflex actions

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that helps protect the body.

Typical pathway:

Stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector → response

🧠 Brain regions

Region Major functions
Cerebrum Conscious thought, memory, learning, interpretation of sensory information and voluntary movement.
Cerebellum Coordination, balance and precision of movement.
Medulla oblongata Controls important involuntary functions such as heart rate and breathing.
Hypothalamus Links nervous and endocrine control and contributes to homeostasis.

8. ⚡ Nerve Impulses & Synapses

⚡ Resting potential

A neurone at rest has a potential difference across its membrane, with the inside relatively negative compared with the outside.

This electrical state is maintained by ion concentration gradients and membrane transport mechanisms, including the sodium-potassium pump and selective ion permeability.

⚡ Action potential

When a stimulus reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium ions enter the axon, causing depolarisation. Sodium channels then inactivate while potassium channels allow potassium ions to leave, producing repolarisation.

A brief refractory period helps ensure that action potentials travel in one direction and limits the maximum firing frequency.

🚀 Factors affecting conduction speed

  • Axon diameter.
  • Presence of myelin.
  • Temperature within the normal physiological range.

In myelinated axons, action potentials are regenerated at the nodes of Ranvier. This is called saltatory conduction.

🔗 Synapses

A synapse is a junction where communication occurs between a neurone and another cell. At a chemical synapse, neurotransmitter is released from the presynaptic neurone and diffuses across the synaptic cleft.

  1. Action potential arrives at presynaptic terminal.
  2. Calcium ion channels open.
  3. Calcium ions enter the presynaptic terminal.
  4. Synaptic vesicles fuse with the membrane.
  5. Neurotransmitter is released.
  6. Neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  7. Ion channels change activity and may initiate a new electrical response.
  8. Neurotransmitter is removed by breakdown, reuptake or diffusion.

➡️ One-way transmission

Chemical synapses normally transmit information in one direction because neurotransmitter release machinery is concentrated on the presynaptic side while receptors are concentrated on the postsynaptic side.

9. 👁️ Receptors & Sense Organs

Receptors are specialised cells or nerve endings that detect particular stimuli and convert stimulus energy into electrical signals.

Receptor type Stimulus detected Example
Photoreceptor Light Rod and cone cells of retina
Mechanoreceptor Mechanical deformation Hearing and touch receptors
Chemoreceptor Chemicals Taste and smell receptors
Thermoreceptor Temperature changes Temperature-sensitive nerve endings
Nociceptor Potentially damaging stimuli Pain receptors

👁️ The human eye

  • Cornea: major refractive surface.
  • Iris: controls pupil diameter.
  • Pupil: opening through which light enters.
  • Lens: fine-focuses light onto the retina.
  • Retina: contains photoreceptors.
  • Optic nerve: carries information towards the brain.

🌑 Rods and cones

Feature Rods Cones
Light sensitivity High Lower
Colour vision Do not provide colour discrimination Provide colour discrimination
Visual detail Lower spatial resolution Higher spatial resolution
Distribution Widely distributed in retina Especially concentrated in the fovea

🔍 Accommodation

Accommodation is the adjustment of lens shape to focus images of objects at different distances on the retina.

  • Near object: ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments loosen and the lens becomes more rounded.
  • Distant object: ciliary muscles relax, suspensory ligaments become taut and the lens becomes less rounded.

10. 🧪 Endocrine System & Hormones

🧪 Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers released by endocrine cells or glands into the blood and transported to target cells that possess appropriate receptors.

🧬 Endocrine glands

Gland / tissue Hormone Major function
Pituitary gland Several hormones Regulates growth, reproduction, water balance and other endocrine glands.
Thyroid gland Thyroxine Regulates metabolic activity and supports normal development.
Pancreas Insulin and glucagon Regulates blood glucose concentration.
Adrenal glands Adrenaline and other hormones Stress responses and regulation of salt/water balance.
Ovaries Oestrogen and progesterone Female reproductive functions and menstrual cycle regulation.
Testes Testosterone Male reproductive development and functions.

🔄 Hormonal coordination

Many endocrine systems operate through negative feedback. A change in a physiological variable influences hormone secretion, and the resulting effect reduces the original stimulus.

🦋 Thyroid regulation

The hypothalamus and pituitary regulate thyroid activity through a hormone cascade. Thyroid hormones then participate in negative feedback to reduce further stimulation when their concentration becomes sufficiently high.

🍬 Insulin and glucagon

Insulin and glucagon work antagonistically to help maintain blood glucose within an appropriate range.

11. 🦴 Muscles & Movement

💪 Types of muscle tissue

Muscle type Control Location / feature
Skeletal muscle Usually voluntary Attached to bones and produces body movement.
Smooth muscle Involuntary Walls of organs such as the intestine and blood vessels.
Cardiac muscle Involuntary Heart wall; specialised for rhythmic contraction.

🦴 Antagonistic muscles

Skeletal muscles generally pull rather than push. Movement around a joint is therefore often produced by pairs of antagonistic muscles.

Example: at the elbow, the biceps and triceps act antagonistically.

Movement Biceps Triceps
Flexion Contracts Relaxes
Extension Relaxes Contracts

🔬 Skeletal muscle structure

Skeletal muscle contains bundles of muscle fibres. Each muscle fibre contains myofibrils made of repeating sarcomeres containing actin and myosin filaments.

⚡ Sliding filament mechanism

  1. A motor neurone stimulates a muscle fibre.
  2. Calcium ions are released inside the muscle fibre.
  3. Calcium allows actin-myosin interaction.
  4. Myosin heads bind to actin and perform power strokes.
  5. ATP is required for the cross-bridge cycle.
  6. Repeated interactions cause sarcomeres to shorten.
  7. The whole muscle develops tension and may shorten.

12. 🌸 Human Reproduction

♂️ Male reproductive system

  • Testes: produce sperm and testosterone.
  • Epididymis: sperm maturation and storage.
  • Sperm duct: transports sperm.
  • Accessory glands: contribute fluid to semen.
  • Penis: organ involved in sexual reproduction.

♀️ Female reproductive system

  • Ovaries: produce ova and reproductive hormones.
  • Oviducts: transport the ovum; fertilisation usually occurs here.
  • Uterus: site where the embryo implants and develops.
  • Cervix: lower opening of the uterus.
  • Vagina: muscular canal connecting the cervix to the exterior.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert labelled diagrams of the male and female human reproductive systems.

🧬 Gametogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the production of sperm in the testes. Oogenesis is the process leading to formation of female gametes in the ovaries.

🥚 Ovulation and menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is controlled by interactions between the hypothalamus, pituitary gland and ovaries.

Hormone Major role
FSH Stimulates development of ovarian follicles and supports sperm production in males.
LH Triggers ovulation and supports formation of the corpus luteum; stimulates testosterone production in males.
Oestrogen Produced mainly by developing follicles; contributes to rebuilding the uterine lining and feedback regulation.
Progesterone Produced mainly by the corpus luteum after ovulation; maintains the uterine lining.

🧬 Fertilisation

Fertilisation occurs when a sperm nucleus fuses with the nucleus of the female gamete, producing a diploid zygote.

👶 Implantation and pregnancy

After fertilisation, the early embryo undergoes cell division and eventually implants into the lining of the uterus.

The placenta forms an exchange interface between maternal and fetal circulations. Oxygen and nutrients move towards the fetus, while carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes move away from the fetus.

🍼 Placental functions

  • Exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide.
  • Transfer of nutrients.
  • Removal of fetal metabolic wastes.
  • Production of hormones important for pregnancy.
  • Provides an important barrier, although it does not block every substance or pathogen.

🧪 Hormonal control of pregnancy

During early pregnancy, embryonic tissues produce human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which supports the corpus luteum. Later, the placenta becomes an important source of reproductive hormones.

13. 🔗 Integration of Human Physiological Systems

🧠 One body, many systems

Human physiology is best understood as an integrated network rather than a collection of isolated systems.

System Major contribution Important connection
Digestive system Provides absorbed nutrients Blood transports nutrients to tissues.
Respiratory system Provides oxygen and removes carbon dioxide Circulatory system transports respiratory gases.
Circulatory system Transport Connects organs and tissues.
Kidneys Excretion and internal chemical regulation Controlled partly by hormones such as ADH.
Nervous system Rapid coordination Works with endocrine control.
Endocrine system Hormonal coordination Regulates metabolism, reproduction and homeostasis.

Example: during exercise, muscles require more ATP. This increases oxygen and nutrient demand and carbon dioxide production. The nervous and endocrine systems coordinate changes in heart rate, breathing and blood flow, while the respiratory and circulatory systems increase gas transport.

16. ⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • ❌ Saying arteries always carry oxygenated blood — the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
  • ❌ Saying veins always carry deoxygenated blood — pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.
  • ❌ Saying the heart pumps oxygenated blood from the right ventricle — it pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
  • ❌ Saying the potometer directly measures transpiration — it measures water uptake as an indirect estimate.
  • ❌ Confusing ultrafiltration with selective reabsorption.
  • ❌ Saying ADH removes water from the blood — ADH promotes water reabsorption into the blood.
  • ❌ Saying hormones travel through ducts — endocrine hormones enter the bloodstream.
  • ❌ Confusing sensory neurones with motor neurones.
  • ❌ Saying synapses are simply gaps between cells without explaining neurotransmitter transmission.
  • ❌ Confusing rods with cones.
  • ❌ Saying muscles push bones — skeletal muscles generate pulling forces.
  • ❌ Treating the menstrual cycle as being controlled by only one hormone.

19. 🏆 Final Takeaway

Animal and human physiology is the study of how body systems function and work together to keep an organism alive.

At A/L level, do not learn each system as an isolated list of facts. Understand the links between structure and function, transport, energy supply, coordination, homeostasis and reproduction.

The strongest answers explain not only what happens, but also how it happens, why it happens and how the structure of the system makes the function possible. 🧠🔬
