🧪 Physiological Experiments

A/L Biology — 0710 | Experimental design, physiological investigations, practical techniques, data analysis and interpretation.

1. 🧪 Meaning of Physiology and Physiological Experiments

Physiology is the branch of Biology concerned with the functions and activities of living organisms and their parts.

A physiological experiment is a controlled investigation used to study how a biological process works or how it responds to a particular factor.

Examples include investigations of:

  • 🫁 Respiration
  • 🌱 Photosynthesis
  • 🧬 Enzyme activity
  • 💧 Osmosis
  • 🍃 Transpiration
  • ❤️ Heart and circulation
  • 🌡️ Temperature regulation
  • 🦠 Fermentation
  • 🌿 Water uptake by plants

2. 🔬 Scientific Method

A good biological investigation generally follows these stages:

  1. Observation
  2. Identification of a problem
  3. Research/background information
  4. Hypothesis
  5. Experimental design
  6. Collection of data
  7. Analysis of results
  8. Conclusion
  9. Evaluation and improvement

💡 Exam tip: A conclusion should be based on the experimental results. Do not simply repeat the hypothesis.

3. 🎯 Variables

Variable Meaning Example
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed by the investigator. Temperature
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed. Rate of enzyme reaction
Controlled variable A factor kept constant so it does not affect the result. pH or enzyme concentration

🧠 Remember:
Independent = I change it.
Dependent = I measure it.
Controlled = I keep it the same.

4. ⚖️ Controls

A control provides a comparison against which the experimental treatment can be judged.

For example, when investigating whether light is necessary for photosynthesis:

  • Experimental plant → exposed to light.
  • Control plant → kept under suitable conditions but without light.

The control helps determine whether the observed effect is actually due to the independent variable.

5. 🔁 Repeats and Replicates

Experiments should often be repeated because repeats:

  • Increase reliability.
  • Help identify anomalous results.
  • Reduce the effect of random error.
  • Allow a mean to be calculated.

If several independent samples are tested, these are replicates.

⚠️ Repeating an experiment does not automatically remove systematic error. A consistently incorrect instrument can produce repeated but inaccurate measurements.

6. 🧰 Apparatus and Materials

Apparatus should be selected according to what must be measured or controlled.

Apparatus Common use

7. 📝 Writing an Experimental Procedure

A good procedure should:

  • Be written in logical order.
  • State quantities where appropriate.
  • State the independent variable.
  • Explain how the dependent variable is measured.
  • Identify important controlled variables.
  • Include repeats where appropriate.
  • Allow another student to reproduce the investigation.

❌ Weak: "Put the enzyme in the tube and see what happens."

✅ Better: State the volume/concentration, temperature, time, substrate concentration and exactly how the reaction is measured.

8. 👀 Observations

An observation is information obtained using the senses or measuring instruments.

Examples:

  • Colour changed from brown to blue-black.
  • Gas bubbles were produced.
  • The solution became less cloudy.
  • The volume of gas decreased.
  • The specimen increased in mass.

Avoid writing an explanation as an observation.

9. 📋 Results Tables

Good tables should:

  • Have a clear title or heading.
  • Include units in headings.
  • Place independent-variable data in one column.
  • Place dependent-variable data in another column.
  • Use consistent decimal places where appropriate.

10. 📈 Graphs

The independent variable normally goes on the x-axis, while the dependent variable goes on the y-axis.

Important graph rules:

  • Use an appropriate scale.
  • Label both axes.
  • Include units.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Use a suitable line or curve.
  • Do not join experimental points with unnecessary zigzags.
  • Use most of the graph space.

11. 🧮 Rate and Percentage Calculations

Rate

Rate = change ÷ time

For example:

If 20 cm³ of gas is produced in 40 seconds:

Rate = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 cm³ s⁻¹

Percentage change

Percentage change = [(final value − initial value) ÷ initial value] × 100

12. 📊 Mean, Range and Anomalies

Mean

Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values

Range

Range = highest value − lowest value

Anomalous result

An anomalous result is a result that does not fit the general pattern shown by the other measurements.

⚠️ Do not automatically delete an anomalous result. Identify it, investigate possible causes and explain whether it should be excluded.

13. 🔎 Interpretation of Results

Interpretation explains what the results show.

Useful phrases include:

  • "As temperature increased, the rate increased..."
  • "The rate reached a maximum at..."
  • "There was no further increase after..."
  • "The results suggest that..."
  • "There is a positive/negative correlation between..."

14. 🧠 Conclusions

A conclusion should answer the experimental question and refer directly to the evidence.

Example: "The results show that increasing temperature increased enzyme activity up to the optimum temperature, after which activity decreased."

15. ⚠️ Sources of Experimental Error

Error type Description
Random error Unpredictable variation between measurements.
Systematic error A consistent error affecting measurements in the same direction.
Human error Error caused by incorrect technique or reading.
Instrument error Error associated with the measuring instrument.

16. 🔧 Improvements

Possible improvements include:

  • Use more accurate measuring equipment.
  • Increase the number of repeats.
  • Calculate means.
  • Control temperature more precisely.
  • Use a water bath where appropriate.
  • Standardise sample size.
  • Use a larger sample size.
  • Improve sealing of apparatus.
  • Use appropriate controls.
  • Reduce human judgement where possible by using instruments.

17. 🫁 Respiration — Respirometer Experiment

A respirometer can be used to investigate oxygen uptake during respiration.

Respiring organisms consume oxygen. If carbon dioxide produced is absorbed by a suitable absorbent, the total gas volume or pressure decreases.

Basic principle

Oxygen uptake → decrease in gas volume/pressure → measurable movement of a fluid marker.

Variables

  • Independent variable → temperature, organism type or another chosen factor.
  • Dependent variable → change in gas volume/marker position per unit time.
  • Controlled variables → organism mass, time, apparatus volume and conditions.

18. 🌱 Photosynthesis Experiments

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic compounds.

Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

Light energy is captured by chlorophyll.

Factors that can be investigated include:

  • 💡 Light intensity
  • 🌫️ Carbon dioxide concentration
  • 🌡️ Temperature
  • 🌿 Leaf area
  • 🌱 Chlorophyll availability

19. 💡 Limiting Factors of Photosynthesis

A limiting factor is a factor present at a level that restricts the rate of a process.

Important limiting factors include:

  • Light intensity
  • Carbon dioxide concentration
  • Temperature

Increasing one factor will only increase the rate while that factor remains limiting.

📈 At high light intensity, another factor such as carbon dioxide or temperature may become limiting.

20. 🧪 Testing a Leaf for Starch

This experiment demonstrates whether photosynthesis has produced stored starch.

Procedure

  1. Remove a leaf from the plant.
  2. Place it in hot water to kill the cells and stop reactions.
  3. Remove pigments using ethanol in a suitable supervised laboratory setup.
  4. Rinse the leaf.
  5. Add iodine solution.
  6. Observe the colour change.

Result

A blue-black colour indicates the presence of starch.

⚠️ Ethanol is flammable and should be handled using appropriate laboratory safety procedures and teacher supervision.

21. 🧬 Enzyme Activity

Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of chemical reactions without being permanently consumed.

Enzyme experiments commonly investigate how enzyme activity changes with:

  • Temperature
  • pH
  • Substrate concentration
  • Enzyme concentration

Measuring enzyme activity

Activity can be measured using:

  • Time taken for a reaction to reach a defined endpoint.
  • Product formed per unit time.
  • Substrate removed per unit time.

22. 🌡️ Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity

Increasing temperature generally increases enzyme activity initially because molecules have more kinetic energy and collide more frequently.

Beyond the optimum temperature, excessive heat disrupts bonds maintaining the enzyme's structure. The active site changes shape and the enzyme becomes less effective.

This loss of functional structure is called denaturation.

23. 🧪 Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity

Enzymes have an optimum pH at which their activity is greatest.

Very acidic or alkaline conditions can alter interactions within the protein and change the shape of the active site.

Each enzyme may have a different optimum pH.

24. 🧬 Substrate Concentration and Enzyme Activity

Increasing substrate concentration increases the rate initially because more substrate molecules collide with enzyme active sites.

At sufficiently high substrate concentration, most active sites are occupied. The enzymes become saturated and the rate approaches a maximum.

25. 💧 Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

The movement continues until equilibrium is approached, unless opposed by other forces such as pressure.

Potato osmosis investigation

  1. Cut potato pieces of similar size.
  2. Measure their initial mass.
  3. Place them in solutions of different concentrations.
  4. Leave them for a fixed period.
  5. Remove and gently blot them.
  6. Measure their final mass.
  7. Calculate percentage change in mass.

26. 📈 Osmosis Graph

A graph of percentage change in mass against solution concentration can be used to estimate the concentration at which there is no net change in mass.

This point is associated with the approximate water potential of the tissue relative to the surrounding solution under the experimental conditions.

27. 🍃 Transpiration and the Potometer

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through stomata.

A potometer estimates water uptake by a plant shoot. Because most water taken up is eventually lost through transpiration, it can provide an estimate of transpiration rate.

⭐ Important: A potometer measures water uptake, not transpiration directly.

28. 🌬️ Factors Affecting Transpiration

Factor General effect on transpiration Reason
Light intensity Usually increases Stomata tend to open for photosynthesis.
Temperature Usually increases Higher temperature increases evaporation and diffusion.
Wind speed Usually increases Removes humid air around the leaf.
Humidity Usually decreases Reduces the water vapour concentration gradient.

29. ❤️ Heart and Circulation Investigations

Human cardiovascular investigations can include safe, non-invasive measurements such as:

  • Resting pulse rate
  • Pulse rate after controlled exercise
  • Recovery time
  • Blood pressure using appropriate equipment

Physiological investigations involving students should be non-invasive, age-appropriate, supervised and stopped if discomfort occurs.

Pulse rate investigation

  1. Allow the participant to rest.
  2. Measure resting pulse rate.
  3. Carry out a safe, standardised activity.
  4. Measure pulse rate immediately afterward.
  5. Continue measuring during recovery.
  6. Compare the results.

30. 🩸 Blood Pressure Investigation

Blood pressure is the pressure exerted by circulating blood against the walls of blood vessels.

A blood pressure measurement commonly gives:

  • Systolic pressure — pressure during ventricular contraction.
  • Diastolic pressure — pressure during ventricular relaxation.

Blood pressure should be measured using appropriate equipment and technique.

31. 🫁 Breathing Rate Investigation

Breathing rate can be investigated by counting breaths per minute under different safe conditions.

For example:

  • Resting condition
  • After controlled physical activity
  • During recovery

Breathing rate generally increases during exercise because tissues require more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide.

32. 🌡️ Human Temperature Investigations

Human core temperature is maintained within a relatively narrow range through homeostatic mechanisms.

Safe investigations may involve comparing temperature measurements under normal environmental conditions or observing changes after mild, controlled activity.

Investigations should never deliberately expose students to dangerous temperatures or harmful conditions.

33. 🌱 Water Uptake by Roots

Roots absorb water mainly by osmosis through root hair cells.

A physiological investigation can examine how environmental conditions affect water uptake.

Important variables may include:

  • Water availability
  • Solution concentration
  • Temperature
  • Root surface area

34. 🧪 Membrane Permeability — Beetroot Investigation

Beetroot cells contain pigments inside their vacuoles. Damage to cell membranes can allow pigment to leak into the surrounding solution.

This can be used to investigate factors affecting membrane permeability, such as temperature.

General principle

Greater pigment leakage → greater colour intensity of the surrounding solution.

A colorimeter can be used to obtain quantitative measurements.

35. 📊 Colorimetry

A colorimeter measures the amount of light absorbed or transmitted by a coloured solution at a selected wavelength.

General procedure

  1. Prepare appropriate standards if required.
  2. Set the appropriate wavelength/filter.
  3. Use a blank to account for background absorbance.
  4. Place the sample in the cuvette.
  5. Measure absorbance or transmission.
  6. Repeat measurements where appropriate.

A calibration curve can be used to estimate the concentration of an unknown sample.

36. 📈 Calibration Curves

A calibration curve is produced by measuring known concentrations and plotting their instrument readings.

To determine an unknown:

  1. Measure the unknown sample.
  2. Find its reading on the y-axis.
  3. Draw across to the calibration curve.
  4. Drop down to the concentration axis.
  5. Estimate the unknown concentration.

37. 🧪 Catalase Experiment

Catalase is an enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide.

Hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen

Oxygen production can be estimated by measuring gas volume or another suitable indicator of reaction rate.

Factors that can be investigated

  • Temperature
  • pH
  • Substrate concentration
  • Enzyme concentration

38. 🍞 Fermentation by Yeast

Yeast can release energy anaerobically by fermentation.

Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy

Carbon dioxide production can be used as an indicator of fermentation rate.

Factors that can be investigated include:

  • Temperature
  • Sugar concentration
  • pH
  • Type of sugar

39. 🌿 Stomatal Distribution

Stomata are pores that allow gas exchange and contribute to transpiration.

Stomatal distribution can be investigated using a suitable epidermal impression or microscopy technique.

Results may be expressed as the number of stomata per unit area.

40. 🔬 Microscopy in Physiological Investigations

Microscopy allows cells and tissues involved in physiological processes to be examined.

Important skills include:

  • Preparing specimens.
  • Using low power before high power.
  • Focusing correctly.
  • Adjusting illumination.
  • Estimating or calculating magnification.
  • Making accurate biological drawings.

Magnification

Magnification = image size ÷ actual size

41. 📉 Choosing the Correct Graph

Graph Best use
Line graph Continuous numerical variables.
Scatter graph Investigating relationships/correlation between two numerical variables.
Bar chart Comparing separate categories.
Histogram Continuous data grouped into intervals.

42. 🔗 Correlation and Causation

Correlation means that two variables show a relationship or pattern.

Correlation alone does not prove that one variable causes the other.

A third variable may influence both.

⭐ Exam phrase: "The results show a correlation, but further controlled experiments would be needed to establish causation."

43. 🎯 Validity, Reliability, Accuracy and Precision

Term Meaning
Validity Whether the experiment actually tests what it is intended to test.
Reliability Whether repeated measurements give consistent results.
Accuracy How close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.
Precision How close repeated measurements are to each other.

44. 🧠 Evaluating an Experiment

When evaluating an investigation, consider:

  • Was there a suitable control?
  • Were variables properly controlled?
  • Was the sample size large enough?
  • Were there enough repeats?
  • Were anomalous results investigated?
  • Was the measuring equipment suitable?
  • Was the method precise enough?
  • Could temperature or other environmental factors have changed?
  • Was the experiment valid?

45. 🧪 Experimental Design Example

Question

How does temperature affect the activity of an enzyme?

Hypothesis

Enzyme activity will increase with temperature up to an optimum, then decrease at higher temperatures.

Independent variable

Temperature.

Dependent variable

Rate of enzyme reaction.

Controlled variables

  • pH
  • Enzyme concentration
  • Substrate concentration
  • Reaction volume
  • Measurement time

Repeats

Repeat each temperature treatment and calculate a mean.

Results

Record temperature and reaction rate in a suitable table.

Graph

Plot temperature on the x-axis and reaction rate on the y-axis.

Conclusion

Identify the temperature giving the highest reaction rate and describe what happens above the optimum.

46. ❌ Common Experimental Mistakes

  • Confusing independent and dependent variables.
  • Forgetting units.
  • Using an unsuitable graph.
  • Failing to repeat measurements.
  • Not controlling important variables.
  • Calling every unusual value an error.
  • Removing anomalous data without justification.
  • Claiming causation from correlation alone.
  • Writing a conclusion without referring to evidence.
  • Confusing water uptake with direct measurement of transpiration.
  • Ignoring the need for a control.
  • Using an unsafe or poorly controlled procedure.

48. 📝 Practical Exam Questions

Question 1

A student investigates the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. Identify the independent and dependent variables.

Answer: Independent variable = temperature. Dependent variable = enzyme reaction rate.

Question 2

Why should the experiment be repeated?

Answer: Repeats improve reliability, allow a mean to be calculated and help identify anomalous results.

Question 3

Why should pH be kept constant when investigating temperature?

Answer: To ensure that changes in enzyme activity are caused by temperature rather than pH.

Question 4

What does a blue-black colour indicate in the iodine test?

Answer: The presence of starch.

Question 5

What does a potometer measure?

Answer: Water uptake by a plant shoot, which can be used to estimate transpiration rate.

51. 🏆 Final Takeaway

A/L physiological experimentation is not simply about memorising individual experiments. You must understand how to investigate biological processes scientifically.

A strong Biology practical student should be able to move from:

Question → Hypothesis → Variables → Control → Method → Results → Calculations → Graph → Interpretation → Conclusion → Evaluation

🌱 The major physiological investigations include photosynthesis, respiration, enzyme activity, osmosis, transpiration, water uptake, membrane permeability, fermentation and safe human physiological investigations.
