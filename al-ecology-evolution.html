🌍 Ecology & Evolution

A/L Biology — 0710 | Ecosystems, energy flow, nutrient cycles, population ecology, communities, adaptation, evolution, biodiversity, conservation and human environmental impact.

1. 🌍 Ecosystems

🌱 What is an ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a functional unit consisting of a community of organisms interacting with one another and with the non-living components of their environment.

An ecosystem therefore contains both biotic and abiotic components.

🧬 Levels of ecological organisation

Level Meaning Example
Organism A single living individual. One elephant
Population Members of the same species living in an area at the same time. Elephants in a forest
Community All populations of different species living and interacting in an area. Plants, animals and microorganisms in a forest
Ecosystem A community together with its abiotic environment. Forest ecosystem
Biome A large ecological region characterised by climate and dominant vegetation. Tropical rainforest
Biosphere The global zone of life containing all ecosystems. Earth's biosphere

🌤️ Abiotic factors

Abiotic factors are non-living environmental factors that influence organisms.

  • 🌡️ Temperature
  • 💧 Water availability
  • ☀️ Light intensity
  • 🌧️ Rainfall
  • 🌬️ Wind
  • 🧪 Soil pH
  • 🧂 Mineral availability
  • 💨 Oxygen concentration
  • 🌊 Salinity
  • 🏔️ Altitude

🌿 Biotic factors

  • Competition
  • Predation
  • Herbivory
  • Parasitism
  • Mutualism
  • Disease
  • Availability of food
  • Availability of mates

🌱 Producers, consumers and decomposers

Group Role Example
Producer Produces organic compounds from inorganic materials, usually using light energy. Green plant
Primary consumer Feeds directly on producers. Grasshopper
Secondary consumer Feeds on primary consumers. Frog
Decomposer Breaks down dead organic material and releases nutrients. Fungus or bacterium

2. 🔄 Energy Flow

☀️ Energy enters ecosystems

Most ecosystems receive their energy from the Sun. Photosynthetic organisms convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic compounds.

Energy then passes through feeding relationships from producers to consumers and eventually to decomposers.

🍃 Food chains

A food chain represents a sequence of feeding relationships through which energy and matter are transferred.

Example:

Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk

🕸️ Food webs

A food web consists of interconnected food chains. It provides a more realistic representation of feeding relationships within a community.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert a food web containing at least three trophic levels and use arrows to show the direction of energy transfer.

🔢 Trophic levels

Trophic level Typical organism
1st Producer
2nd Primary consumer
3rd Secondary consumer
4th Tertiary consumer

🔥 Why energy decreases along food chains

Energy is lost between trophic levels because organisms:

  • Respire and release energy as heat.
  • Use energy for movement.
  • Use energy for active transport and other cellular processes.
  • Do not consume every part of their food.
  • Do not digest or assimilate all consumed material.
  • Lose some material in faeces and other waste.

📊 Ecological pyramids

Ecological pyramids can represent numbers, biomass or energy at successive trophic levels.

Pyramid What it represents
Pyramid of numbers Number of organisms at each trophic level.
Pyramid of biomass Total mass of living material at each trophic level.
Pyramid of energy Energy available at each trophic level, usually per unit area per unit time.

⚡ Productivity

Gross primary productivity (GPP) is the total rate at which producers capture energy and store it as organic material.

Net primary productivity (NPP) is the rate of energy stored as new plant biomass after respiratory losses:

NPP = GPP − respiratory losses

📌 Exam tip

Do not say that energy is "recycled" in an ecosystem. Energy flows through an ecosystem and is eventually dissipated as heat, whereas chemical nutrients are recycled.

3. ♻️ Nutrient Cycles

Unlike energy, elements such as carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus are continuously recycled between organisms and the physical environment.

🌫️ Carbon cycle

Carbon dioxide is removed from the atmosphere by photosynthesis and incorporated into organic compounds.

Carbon returns to the environment through:

  • Respiration.
  • Decomposition.
  • Combustion.
  • Some geological processes.

🧪 Nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen is essential for amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids. Atmospheric nitrogen cannot normally be used directly by plants.

🦠 Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen-fixing microorganisms convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms that can enter biological nitrogen cycles.

🔄 Important nitrogen-cycle processes

Process Main role
Nitrogen fixation Converts atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable nitrogen compounds.
Nitrification Converts ammonia/ammonium through nitrite to nitrate.
Assimilation Plants absorb inorganic nitrogen and incorporate it into organic compounds.
Ammonification Decomposers convert organic nitrogen compounds into ammonia/ammonium.
Denitrification Some bacteria convert nitrate to gaseous nitrogen under suitable low-oxygen conditions.

🌱 Nitrogen fixation and root nodules

Some bacteria form associations with leguminous plants. Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules can fix atmospheric nitrogen into forms that enter the nitrogen cycle.

🖼️ Diagram space

Insert a labelled nitrogen-cycle diagram including fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification.

🌊 Phosphorus cycle

Phosphorus is important in ATP, nucleic acids and phospholipids. Unlike carbon and nitrogen, its major natural cycle has no significant gaseous atmospheric phase.

Weathering releases phosphate ions from rocks. Plants absorb phosphate, animals obtain it through feeding, and decomposition returns phosphorus to the environment.

4. 📈 Population Ecology

👥 Population

A population is a group of individuals of the same species living in the same geographical area at the same time and capable of interacting and, where appropriate, reproducing.

📊 Population size

Population size is affected by four major processes:

  • Births increase population size.
  • Deaths decrease population size.
  • Immigration increases population size.
  • Emigration decreases population size.

Therefore:

Population change = births + immigration − deaths − emigration

📈 Population growth

When resources are abundant and limiting factors are weak, populations may increase rapidly.

Under idealised unlimited-resource conditions, population growth can be approximately exponential and produces a J-shaped curve.

🟢 Carrying capacity

Carrying capacity is the maximum population size that an environment can sustain over a particular period under particular conditions.

When limiting factors become stronger, population growth slows and may fluctuate around the carrying capacity, producing a typical S-shaped logistic growth pattern.

Insert a graph comparing exponential population growth with logistic growth and clearly label carrying capacity.

⚠️ Density-dependent and density-independent factors

Type Meaning Examples
Density-dependent Effects generally become stronger as population density increases. Competition, disease, predation
Density-independent Effects are not primarily determined by population density. Floods, droughts, storms, fires

🔍 Population sampling

Ecologists often estimate population size instead of counting every individual.

🌿 Quadrat sampling

Quadrats are commonly used to estimate the abundance or distribution of plants and other relatively immobile organisms.

🐾 Capture-mark-release-recapture

This method can be used to estimate populations of mobile animals.

A simple estimate is:

Population estimate ≈ (number initially marked × total caught later) ÷ number of marked individuals recaptured

The method depends on assumptions such as adequate mixing, marks not substantially changing survival or capture probability, and a closed population during the sampling period.

5. 🐾 Communities & Interactions

A community consists of populations of different species living and interacting within an area.

🤝 Major ecological interactions

Interaction Effect Example
Competition Both participants are negatively affected by competition for limited resources. Plants competing for light
Predation Predator benefits; prey is harmed. Snake eating a frog
Herbivory Herbivore benefits; plant is harmed. Grasshopper feeding on leaves
Parasitism Parasite benefits; host is harmed. Tapeworm in a human
Mutualism Both species benefit. Rhizobium and legume
Commensalism One benefits while the other is not significantly affected. Epiphyte using a tree for support

🏠 Ecological niche

A species' ecological niche describes its role and position in an ecosystem, including how it uses resources and interacts with other organisms and environmental conditions.

🌳 Habitat vs niche

Habitat Niche
The place where an organism lives. The organism's ecological role and resource use.
Describes location and environmental conditions. Describes how the organism lives and functions within the ecosystem.

⚔️ Competitive exclusion

When two species occupy extremely similar niches and compete for the same limiting resource, long-term coexistence may be difficult if one species consistently has a competitive advantage.

🧩 Resource partitioning

Species with overlapping requirements may reduce competition by using different resources, locations or times. This is called resource partitioning or niche differentiation.

6. 🌿 Adaptation

🧬 Definition

An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases the ability of an organism to survive and reproduce in a particular environment.

🔬 Types of adaptation

Type Meaning Example
Structural Physical feature of an organism. Thick waxy cuticle of xerophytic plants
Physiological Internal biochemical or functional process. Ability of kidneys to produce concentrated urine
Behavioural Behaviour that improves survival or reproductive success. Migration

🌵 Adaptations to dry environments

Xerophytic plants may possess:

  • Reduced leaf surface area.
  • Thick waxy cuticle.
  • Sunken or fewer stomata.
  • Leaf hairs.
  • Extensive root systems.
  • Water-storage tissues.

🐪 Animal adaptations

Animals may adapt to environmental conditions through features that improve water conservation, temperature regulation, feeding, movement or protection.

🎭 Camouflage

Camouflage reduces the probability of detection by predators or prey. Selection can favour inherited characteristics that improve camouflage.

⚠️ Adaptation is not the same as acclimatisation

Adaptation Acclimatisation
Inherited characteristic shaped across generations. Change occurring within an individual's lifetime in response to environmental conditions.
Has a genetic basis. Does not necessarily involve a change in inherited DNA sequence.

7. 🧬 Evolution & Natural Selection

🧬 Evolution

Biological evolution is change in the inherited characteristics of populations across generations.

🔀 Sources of genetic variation

  • Mutation.
  • Crossing over during meiosis.
  • Independent assortment of chromosomes.
  • Random fertilisation.
  • Gene flow between populations.

Mutation creates new genetic variants. Sexual reproduction reshuffles existing genetic variation.

🌿 Natural selection

Natural selection occurs when individuals with heritable characteristics that improve survival or reproductive success tend to leave more surviving offspring. As a result, associated alleles may become more common in the population over generations.

🔄 Steps of natural selection

  1. Individuals within a population show variation.
  2. Some variation is heritable.
  3. Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support.
  4. Individuals face competition and other selective pressures.
  5. Individuals with advantageous heritable characteristics have greater reproductive success.
  6. These characteristics become more common over generations.

🧠 Important idea

Individuals do not evolve because they "need" to. Natural selection acts on existing heritable variation, causing populations to evolve across generations.

🦋 Example: industrial melanism

Where environmental pollution historically darkened tree surfaces, darker forms of some moth populations were better camouflaged from visual predators. Where conditions later changed, the selective advantage could change as well.

🦠 Antibiotic resistance

Bacterial populations may contain genetic variants with different levels of antibiotic resistance. When an antibiotic is applied, susceptible bacteria are more likely to be eliminated while resistant bacteria are more likely to survive and reproduce. Resistance can therefore become more common in the population.

🧬 Artificial selection

Artificial selection occurs when humans choose organisms with desirable characteristics to reproduce.

Examples include selective breeding of crop plants and domesticated animals.

🧬 Genetic drift

Genetic drift is a change in allele frequencies caused by random sampling of individuals, especially in small populations.

🌪️ Founder effect

The founder effect occurs when a new population is established by a small number of individuals. The new population may have allele frequencies that differ from those of the original population simply because of sampling.

📉 Bottleneck effect

A population bottleneck occurs when a population experiences a severe reduction in size. The surviving individuals may carry only a subset of the original genetic variation.

🚚 Gene flow

Gene flow occurs when alleles move between populations through migration and subsequent reproduction.

8. 🌳 Biodiversity & Conservation

🌍 Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life. It can be considered at genetic, species and ecosystem levels.

🧬 Levels of biodiversity

Level Meaning
Genetic diversity Variation in genetic characteristics within and between populations.
Species diversity Variety and relative abundance of species in a community.
Ecosystem diversity Variety of ecosystems and ecological processes in a region.

🌱 Importance of biodiversity

  • Supports ecosystem functioning.
  • Provides food and raw materials.
  • Provides medicinal resources.
  • Supports pollination and nutrient cycling.
  • Helps maintain genetic resources for agriculture.
  • Provides cultural, educational and recreational value.

⚠️ Threats to biodiversity

  • Habitat destruction.
  • Deforestation.
  • Overexploitation.
  • Pollution.
  • Invasive species.
  • Climate change.
  • Illegal wildlife exploitation.
  • Fragmentation of habitats.

🛡️ Conservation

Conservation involves protecting biodiversity and managing biological resources so that ecosystems and species can persist into the future.

🌳 In-situ conservation

In-situ conservation protects organisms within their natural habitats.

Examples include national parks, wildlife reserves and protected forests.

🏛️ Ex-situ conservation

Ex-situ conservation protects organisms outside their natural habitats.

Examples include botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks, zoos and captive-breeding programmes.

In-situ Ex-situ
Conservation occurs in the natural habitat. Conservation occurs outside the natural habitat.
Maintains organisms within their ecological communities. Useful when populations are critically threatened or natural habitats are unavailable.
Examples: protected areas and national parks. Examples: seed banks and captive breeding.

🌍 Conservation in Cameroon

Cameroon contains important forest, savanna, montane, freshwater and coastal ecosystems. Conservation is important for maintaining wildlife, forests, watersheds, genetic resources and ecosystem services.

Conservation strategies can include protected areas, sustainable forestry, habitat restoration, community participation, anti-poaching measures, environmental education and sustainable resource use.

9. 🌡️ Human Environmental Impact

Human activities can alter ecosystems by changing habitats, resource availability, nutrient cycles, climate and species distributions.

🌳 Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal of forest cover. It can result in:

  • Habitat loss.
  • Habitat fragmentation.
  • Loss of biodiversity.
  • Soil erosion.
  • Changes in local water cycles.
  • Release of stored carbon.

🏭 Pollution

Pollution type Possible ecological effect
Air pollution Respiratory damage, acid deposition and effects on vegetation.
Water pollution Reduced water quality and harm to aquatic organisms.
Plastic pollution Ingestion, entanglement and long-term accumulation of waste.
Pesticide pollution Can harm non-target organisms and disrupt food webs.
Thermal pollution Changes water temperature and dissolved oxygen conditions.

🌊 Eutrophication

Eutrophication occurs when excessive nutrients enter an aquatic ecosystem, often from fertiliser runoff or sewage.

  1. Nutrient concentration increases.
  2. Algae and aquatic plants may grow rapidly.
  3. Light penetration decreases.
  4. Some plants die.
  5. Decomposers break down the dead organic material.
  6. Microbial respiration consumes dissolved oxygen.
  7. Oxygen concentration can fall.
  8. Fish and other aquatic organisms may die or leave.

🌡️ Climate change

Human activities have increased atmospheric concentrations of several greenhouse gases, strengthening the greenhouse effect and contributing to global warming and broader climate change.

Major greenhouse gases include carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide.

🔥 Greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation, reducing the rate at which heat escapes directly to space.

The natural greenhouse effect makes Earth suitable for life, but increased greenhouse-gas concentrations can strengthen warming.

🌍 Consequences of climate change

  • Changes in species distributions.
  • Changes in seasonal biological events.
  • Increased heat stress.
  • Changes in rainfall patterns.
  • Sea-level rise.
  • Increased risks to some ecosystems and species.
  • Greater pressure on agriculture and water resources in vulnerable regions.

♻️ Sustainable management

Sustainable management aims to meet human needs while maintaining the long-term functioning and productivity of ecosystems.

  • Reforestation and afforestation where appropriate.
  • Sustainable harvesting.
  • Protected areas.
  • Waste reduction and recycling.
  • Efficient use of water.
  • Renewable energy development.
  • Environmental education.
  • Habitat restoration.
  • Sustainable agriculture.

10. 🔬 Ecological Fieldwork & Sampling

📏 Measuring abiotic factors

Factor Possible instrument
Temperature Thermometer
Light intensity Light meter
pH pH meter or suitable indicator
Humidity Hygrometer
Wind speed Anemometer

🌿 Transects

A transect is used to study how organisms or environmental factors change along a gradient, such as from a forest edge towards the centre of a forest.

A line transect records organisms touching or occurring along a line. A belt transect samples organisms within a defined strip along the gradient.

🎯 Random sampling

Random sampling reduces deliberate selection of sampling locations and can improve representativeness when properly designed.

📊 Reliability of ecological data

  • Use sufficient sample size.
  • Repeat measurements.
  • Use consistent sampling methods.
  • Control important variables where possible.
  • Record units clearly.
  • Identify anomalous results rather than automatically deleting them.

11. 🧬 Evidence for Evolution

🦴 Fossil evidence

Fossils provide evidence of organisms that lived in the past and show that species and biological communities have changed through geological time.

🦴 Comparative anatomy

Homologous structures have the same basic evolutionary origin but may have different functions. Similar underlying structures in vertebrate limbs provide evidence of common ancestry.

Analogous structures perform similar functions but evolved independently and do not indicate recent common ancestry of the structures themselves.

Homologous Analogous
Similar underlying structural origin. Similar function but different evolutionary origin.
Evidence of common ancestry. Often results from convergent evolution.
Example: forelimbs of humans and bats. Example: wings of birds and insects.

🧬 Molecular evidence

DNA and protein sequence comparisons can reveal evolutionary relationships. Species with more similar sequences generally have a more recent common ancestor than species with greater sequence differences, provided the sequences and methods are appropriate for the comparison.

🌳 Phylogenetic trees

A phylogenetic tree represents hypotheses about evolutionary relationships. Branch points represent common ancestors, while branches represent lineages.

12. 🧬 Speciation

🌱 What is speciation?

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which new species arise.

🚧 Reproductive isolation

Speciation requires the development of barriers to gene flow that prevent populations from freely exchanging genes.

🏔️ Allopatric speciation

Allopatric speciation occurs when populations become geographically separated. Different mutations, selection pressures and genetic drift can cause the populations to diverge. If reproductive isolation eventually develops, they may become separate species.

🌿 Sympatric speciation

Sympatric speciation occurs without a physical geographical barrier. Reproductive isolation can arise through mechanisms such as polyploidy in plants, habitat differentiation or changes in mating behaviour.

🔒 Prezygotic and postzygotic barriers

Type Meaning
Prezygotic isolation Prevents fertilisation from occurring.
Postzygotic isolation Reduces viability or fertility after fertilisation.

13. 🌳 Biodiversity, Stability & Ecosystem Function

Biodiversity can influence ecosystem functioning because species perform different ecological roles and may respond differently to environmental changes.

🔗 Species interactions and stability

Complex food webs can provide multiple pathways for energy and matter transfer. However, greater complexity does not automatically make every ecosystem immune to disturbance.

🧩 Keystone species

A keystone species has an ecological effect that is disproportionately large relative to its abundance. Removing such a species can cause major changes in community structure.

🌱 Ecosystem services

Service Example
Provisioning Food, timber, freshwater and medicinal resources.
Regulating Climate regulation, flood control and pollination.
Cultural Recreation, education and cultural value.
Supporting Nutrient cycling and soil formation.

14. 🧠 Ecology & Evolution Connection

🔗 Why ecology and evolution are connected

Environmental conditions determine which characteristics improve survival and reproduction. Over generations, natural selection can change populations.

At the same time, evolutionary changes can alter ecological interactions, resource use and community structure.

For example, if a population experiences a persistent change in food availability, individuals with inherited characteristics that allow more effective use of the available resource may leave more offspring.

This can change allele frequencies and eventually influence the population's ecological role.

16. ⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • ❌ Saying energy is recycled in ecosystems — energy flows through ecosystems and is eventually dissipated as heat.
  • ❌ Saying nutrients flow in one direction — nutrients are recycled.
  • ❌ Confusing a habitat with a niche.
  • ❌ Saying all adaptations arise because organisms need them.
  • ❌ Saying an individual evolves during its lifetime — evolution refers to changes in populations across generations.
  • ❌ Confusing natural selection with genetic drift.
  • ❌ Saying mutation occurs because an organism needs a particular characteristic.
  • ❌ Confusing homologous and analogous structures.
  • ❌ Saying all mutations are harmful — mutations can be harmful, neutral or beneficial depending on context.
  • ❌ Assuming correlation automatically proves causation in ecological data.
  • ❌ Calling every organism in a food chain a consumer — producers occupy the first trophic level.
  • ❌ Saying decomposers simply "destroy" nutrients — decomposition releases nutrients that can re-enter ecosystems.
  • ❌ Confusing carrying capacity with population size at one particular moment.
  • ❌ Saying conservation means preventing all human use of natural resources — sustainable management can allow carefully controlled use.
  • ❌ Forgetting that climate change can affect species distributions, timing of biological events and ecosystem processes.

19. 🏆 Final Takeaway

Ecology explains how organisms interact with one another and with their environment, while evolution explains how populations change across generations.

The two areas are strongly connected. Environmental conditions create selective pressures, ecological interactions influence survival and reproduction, and evolutionary change can alter the relationships between organisms.

For A/L Biology, focus on understanding the processes, not simply memorising definitions. Be able to interpret graphs, analyse ecological data, explain cause-and-effect relationships, construct evolutionary arguments and apply concepts to unfamiliar ecosystems.

🌍 Ecology asks how life interacts with its environment.
🧬 Evolution asks how populations change through generations.
🔗 Together, they explain much of the diversity and organisation of life on Earth.
