🧪 Practical Chemistry

O/L Chemistry 0515 — Experimental skills, laboratory apparatus, measurements, separation techniques, chemical tests, observations, graphs, errors and Alternative-to-Practical examination skills.

1. 🧪 Introduction to Practical Chemistry

Practical Chemistry involves using experiments and observations to investigate chemical substances, reactions and properties.

At O/L level, students are expected to understand common laboratory procedures, identify apparatus, make observations, interpret experimental results and use evidence to reach appropriate conclusions.

🎯 Core Practical Skills

  • Identifying laboratory apparatus.
  • Measuring mass, volume and temperature.
  • Reading scales correctly.
  • Separating mixtures.
  • Testing gases and aqueous substances.
  • Recording observations accurately.
  • Constructing and interpreting tables.
  • Plotting and interpreting graphs.
  • Identifying variables.
  • Recognising sources of experimental error.
  • Drawing conclusions from experimental evidence.

🇨🇲 GCE O/L Focus

The Cameroon GCE Board's O/L Chemistry report identifies Alternative to Practical as part of Paper 2. The section contains compulsory questions that mainly test skills and aptitudes in experimental Chemistry.

Therefore, practical Chemistry knowledge is important even when you are preparing for written examination questions.

2. 🦺 Laboratory Safety

Laboratory safety rules reduce the risk of accidents and protect students, teachers and laboratory equipment.

🔹 Important Safety Rules

  • Follow the instructions given by the teacher or examiner.
  • Wear appropriate protective equipment when required.
  • Keep the working area clean and organised.
  • Never taste laboratory chemicals.
  • Do not deliberately inhale chemical vapours.
  • Report spills, breakages and accidents immediately.
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Use appropriate equipment when heating substances.
  • Keep combustible materials away from flames.
  • Wash hands after practical work.

⚠️ Important

In an examination, safety questions usually test whether you understand the appropriate precaution for a procedure.

Hazard symbols communicate important information about the risks associated with substances.

Hazard Meaning

Learn both the appearance and meaning of the common hazard symbols used in your laboratory.

5. ⚖️ Measuring Mass

Mass is measured using a balance.

🔹 Good Measurement Practice

  • Check that the balance is correctly zeroed before use.
  • Place the container on the balance carefully.
  • Avoid adding substances unnecessarily.
  • Record the mass with the appropriate precision.

🧠 Exam Point

Mass is commonly measured in grams (g), although other units may be used depending on the instrument and question.

6. 🧪 Measuring Liquid Volume

Different pieces of apparatus have different levels of accuracy for measuring liquid volume.

Apparatus Typical Purpose
Beaker Approximate volume only.
Measuring cylinder Measuring a specified liquid volume.
Pipette Accurately transferring a fixed volume.
Burette Accurately delivering measured variable volumes.

💡 Exam Tip

If a question asks for a precise fixed volume, a pipette is generally more appropriate than a beaker.

7. 👀 Reading the Meniscus

When reading the volume of a liquid in a narrow graduated container, the liquid surface may form a curved meniscus.

🔹 Correct Reading

  • Place the apparatus on a stable, level surface.
  • Position your eyes at the level of the liquid.
  • Avoid viewing the scale from above or below.
  • Read the appropriate part of the meniscus.

8. 🌡️ Measuring Temperature

Temperature is measured using a thermometer.

🔹 Good Technique

  • Ensure the thermometer is appropriate for the temperature range.
  • Place the sensing part correctly in the substance.
  • Do not allow it to touch the container unnecessarily.
  • Wait for the reading to become stable.
  • Read the scale at eye level.

⚠️ Common Error

A temperature reading should not be taken while the thermometer is still changing rapidly.

9. 🧻 Filtration

Filtration is used to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid.

🔹 Principle

The liquid passes through the filter paper while the insoluble solid is retained.

Term Meaning
Residue Solid left on the filter paper.
Filtrate Liquid that passes through the filter paper.

10. 💨 Evaporation

Evaporation is used to remove a solvent from a solution, leaving the dissolved solid behind.

🧪 Example

Salt solution can be heated so that water is removed and salt remains.

🔹 When to Use Evaporation

Evaporation is useful when the main aim is to obtain the dissolved solid rather than recover the solvent.

11. 💎 Crystallisation

Crystallisation is used to obtain a dissolved solid in the form of crystals from a solution.

🔹 Basic Principle

  1. Prepare or obtain the solution.
  2. Remove some solvent carefully.
  3. Allow the solution to cool where appropriate.
  4. Allow crystals to form.
  5. Separate the crystals from the remaining liquid.
  6. Dry the crystals appropriately.

🧠 Why Not Always Evaporate to Complete Dryness?

Some substances may decompose or become contaminated if heated excessively. Crystallisation can therefore be used when the aim is to obtain crystals of the dissolved substance.

12. ♨️ Simple Distillation

Distillation can be used to separate a solvent from a solution by taking advantage of differences in boiling points.

🔹 Principle

  1. The mixture is heated.
  2. The component with the appropriate lower boiling point vaporises.
  3. The vapour passes into a cooler region.
  4. The vapour condenses into a liquid.
  5. The liquid is collected as the distillate.
Term Meaning
Vapour Gaseous form of a substance.
Condensation Change from gas to liquid.
Distillate Liquid collected after condensation.

13. 🧴 Separating Immiscible Liquids

Immiscible liquids are liquids that do not mix to form one uniform liquid phase.

They can be separated using a separating funnel.

14. 🎨 Chromatography

Chromatography is a separation technique used to separate and investigate components of a mixture.

🔹 Basic Principle

Different substances move through the stationary material at different rates because they have different interactions with the stationary and mobile phases.

🔹 Paper Chromatography

  1. Place a small sample on the baseline.
  2. Place the paper in a suitable solvent.
  3. Ensure the solvent does not initially cover the sample spot.
  4. Allow the solvent to move through the paper.
  5. Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top.
  6. Mark the solvent front.
  7. Allow the chromatogram to dry.

🖼️ Diagram Space — Paper Chromatography

Leave space here for a labelled chromatogram showing the baseline, sample spots, separated spots and solvent front.

🔹 Rf Value

📐 Formula

Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent front

Rf values are useful when comparing substances under the same chromatographic conditions.

15. 💨 Collecting Gases

Gases can be collected using methods selected according to their properties, such as solubility in water and density relative to air.

🔹 Collection Over Water

A gas that is sufficiently insoluble in water can be collected by displacement of water.

🔹 Collection by Displacement of Air

A gas may also be collected by allowing it to displace air. The appropriate arrangement depends on the density of the gas relative to air.

16. 🔥 Tests for Common Gases

Gas tests are based on characteristic observable results.

Gas Test Positive Observation
Hydrogen Introduce a lighted splint carefully to the gas. A characteristic squeaky pop is heard.
Oxygen Use a glowing splint. The glowing splint relights.
Carbon dioxide Pass the gas through limewater. Limewater turns milky.
Chlorine Use appropriate damp indicator paper under controlled laboratory conditions. Characteristic bleaching effect may be observed.
Ammonia Use damp red litmus paper. It turns blue.

🧪 Carbon Dioxide Reaction

Word equation:

Carbon dioxide + calcium hydroxide → calcium carbonate + water

Symbol equation:

CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

17. 🧪 Testing Acids and Alkalis

Indicators can be used to distinguish acidic, neutral and alkaline solutions.

Indicator Acidic Neutral Alkaline
Litmus Red Purple Blue
Universal indicator Red/orange/yellow depending on pH Green around pH 7 Blue/purple depending on pH

🔹 pH Scale

The pH scale is used to describe how acidic or alkaline an aqueous solution is.

📊 General pH Interpretation

  • pH below 7 → acidic.
  • pH 7 → neutral.
  • pH above 7 → alkaline.

18. 🫧 Test for Carbonate Ions

Carbonate ions can be identified by adding a suitable acid. Carbon dioxide gas is released.

🧪 Positive Test

Add dilute acid to the sample.

Observation: Effervescence occurs.

The gas produced turns limewater milky.

🧪 Example Reaction

Word equation:

Calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O

19. 🧪 Test for Chloride Ions

Chloride ions can be tested using an appropriate silver-ion reagent after suitable acidification.

🔬 Positive Observation

A white precipitate indicates chloride ions under the standard test conditions.

🧪 Ionic Equation

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

20. 🧪 Test for Sulfate Ions

Sulfate ions can be tested using a suitable barium-ion reagent under standard test conditions.

🔬 Positive Observation

A white precipitate of barium sulfate indicates sulfate ions under the appropriate conditions.

🧪 Ionic Equation

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

21. 🧪 Test for Ammonium Ions

Ammonium ions can be identified by warming the substance with a suitable alkali.

🔬 Positive Observation

Ammonia gas is released. It turns damp red litmus paper blue.

🧪 Ionic Equation

NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)

22. 🎨 Tests for Some Metal Ions

Some metal ions can be distinguished using reactions that produce characteristic precipitate colours.

Ion Typical Observation with Hydroxide
Cu²⁺ Blue precipitate.
Fe²⁺ Green precipitate.
Fe³⁺ Red-brown precipitate.
Al³⁺ White precipitate.
Ca²⁺ White precipitate under suitable conditions.

🧠 Important

A precipitate colour is an observation. The identification of the ion is an inference based on the test result.

23. ☁️ Precipitation Reactions

A precipitation reaction occurs when two solutions react to form an insoluble solid called a precipitate.

🧪 Example

Word equation:

Silver nitrate + sodium chloride → silver chloride + sodium nitrate

Symbol equation:

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

👀 Observation

A white precipitate forms.

24. 👀 Observations and Inferences

This distinction is extremely important in practical Chemistry.

🔹 Observation

An observation is what can be directly seen, heard or measured during an experiment.

🔹 Inference

An inference is a conclusion drawn from observations and chemical knowledge.

Observation Inference
Effervescence occurs. A gas is being produced.
Limewater turns milky. Carbon dioxide is present.
A white precipitate forms. An insoluble solid has formed.
Temperature increases. The process releases heat to the surroundings.
A solution changes colour. A chemical change may have occurred.

⚠️ Common Exam Mistake

Do not write an inference when the question asks specifically for an observation.

25. 🌈 Recording Colour Changes

Colour changes are useful observations in practical Chemistry.

🔹 Good Recording

  • State the initial colour.
  • State the final colour.
  • Describe a clear change.
  • Avoid vague descriptions such as "something happened".

✏️ Example

"The solution changes from blue to colourless."

26. ☁️ Recording Precipitates

A precipitate is an insoluble solid formed from a reaction involving substances in solution.

A good observation should describe:

  • Whether a precipitate forms.
  • Its colour.
  • Any further observable change if relevant.

✏️ Example

"A white precipitate forms."

27. 🫧 Effervescence

Effervescence is the rapid formation of bubbles in a liquid caused by the release of gas.

🧠 Important

Effervescence indicates gas production, but it does not by itself identify which gas has been produced.

28. 🌡️ Temperature Changes

Chemical processes may cause observable temperature changes.

Type Effect on Surroundings
Exothermic Releases energy as heat to the surroundings.
Endothermic Absorbs energy from the surroundings.

📌 Experimental Observation

A rise in temperature of the reaction mixture suggests an exothermic process, while a fall may indicate an endothermic process, provided the measurement is made appropriately.

29. ⏱️ Investigating Rate of Reaction

The rate of a reaction describes how quickly reactants are converted into products.

Practical investigations can monitor a measurable change such as gas volume, mass or time taken to reach a particular endpoint.

🔹 Factors Commonly Investigated

  • Temperature.
  • Concentration.
  • Surface area of a solid.
  • Presence of a catalyst.

🧪 Example Reaction

Word equation:

Calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O

The volume of carbon dioxide produced can be monitored to compare reaction rates.

30. 🎯 Variables in Experiments

Variables are quantities or conditions that may change during an experiment.

Variable Meaning
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed.
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed.
Control variables Factors kept constant where possible.

🧠 Example

If investigating how temperature affects reaction rate:

  • Independent variable: temperature.
  • Dependent variable: reaction rate or measured reaction time.
  • Control variables: other relevant conditions such as reactant quantities and concentration.

31. ⚖️ Fair Tests

A fair test changes the independent variable while keeping other relevant factors constant.

🔹 Why Fair Tests Matter

If several important variables change simultaneously, it becomes difficult to determine which factor caused the observed result.

🎯 Exam Question Pattern

You may be asked to state one variable that should be kept constant or explain why it should remain unchanged.

32. 🔁 Repeating Measurements

Repeating measurements can help identify unusual results and improve confidence in experimental findings.

🔹 Anomalous Result

An anomalous result is one that does not fit the general pattern shown by the other results.

💡 Exam Tip

Do not automatically delete an unusual result. Identify it as anomalous only when the experimental evidence supports that conclusion.

33. 🎯 Accuracy and Precision

🔹 Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true or accepted value.

🔹 Precision

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.

🧠 Remember

A set of measurements can be precise without being accurate if they are closely grouped but far from the accepted value.

34. ⚠️ Experimental Errors

Experimental errors are differences between measured results and the values that would ideally be obtained.

🔹 Common Sources

  • Incorrect reading of a scale.
  • Parallax error.
  • Instrument limitations.
  • Heat loss to the surroundings.
  • Incomplete reaction.
  • Loss of material during transfer.
  • Contamination.
  • Human timing differences.

📌 Important

A good evaluation should identify a realistic source of error rather than simply saying "human error".

35. 🛠️ Improving Experimental Reliability

🔹 Possible Improvements

  • Repeat measurements.
  • Use more precise measuring apparatus where appropriate.
  • Read scales at eye level.
  • Control important variables.
  • Use consistent timing methods.
  • Record results immediately.
  • Use appropriate quantities for the investigation.
  • Take care to avoid contamination.

36. 📋 Recording Results in Tables

Experimental results should be presented clearly so that patterns can be identified easily.

🔹 Good Table Rules

  • Give the table a clear heading when appropriate.
  • Put quantities in separate columns.
  • Include units in column headings.
  • Use consistent decimal places where appropriate.
  • Record observations clearly.
  • Keep the data organised.

Graphs help display relationships between experimental variables.

🔹 Good Graph Practice

  • Label both axes.
  • Include units where appropriate.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Use a suitable line or curve.
  • Give the graph a meaningful title where appropriate.

38. 📊 Interpreting Graphs

When interpreting an experimental graph, look for:

  • Increasing or decreasing trends.
  • Direct or inverse relationships where appropriate.
  • Maximum or minimum values.
  • Anomalous points.
  • Changes in gradient.
  • Values that can be estimated from the graph.

39. 🧪 Basic Titration Principles

Titration is a quantitative technique used to determine the concentration or amount of a substance by reacting it with a solution of known concentration.

🔹 Important Apparatus

  • Burette.
  • Pipette.
  • Conical flask.
  • Indicator.
  • Funnel for filling the burette.

🖼️ Diagram Space — Titration Setup

Leave space here for a labelled titration diagram showing the burette, conical flask, pipette and indicator.

🔹 Titre

The titre is the volume of solution delivered from the burette during a titration.

🔹 Concordant Results

Concordant titres are closely agreeing titres obtained from repeated titrations.

⚠️ Exam Awareness

If a titration question provides burette readings, subtract the initial reading from the final reading to obtain the volume delivered.

40. ⚗️ Neutralisation Experiments

Neutralisation occurs when an acid reacts with a base to form a salt and water.

🧪 Word Equation

Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

🧪 Symbol Equation

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Indicators can be used to help identify the endpoint of an acid-base titration.

41. 💧 Testing Water

Water can be investigated using physical and chemical observations.

🔹 Water as a Solvent

Water dissolves many ionic and polar substances and is therefore an important solvent in laboratory Chemistry.

🔹 Chemical Tests for Water

Water can be identified using suitable tests based on its ability to cause a characteristic change in an appropriate test substance.

🧠 Exam Tip

Always distinguish between testing for water itself and testing a solution for substances dissolved in the water.

42. 🧩 Choosing a Separation Method

The correct separation method depends on the physical properties of the substances in the mixture.

Mixture Suitable Method Property Used
Insoluble solid + liquid Filtration Solubility and particle size.
Dissolved solid + solvent Evaporation / crystallisation Volatility and solubility.
Solvent + dissolved substance Distillation Boiling point.
Immiscible liquids Separating funnel Density and immiscibility.
Coloured dissolved substances Chromatography Different movement through phases.

43. 🧪 Purity of a Substance

Practical Chemistry can be used to investigate whether a sample contains one substance or a mixture.

Chromatography is particularly useful for investigating mixtures because separate components can produce separate spots.

🧠 Pure vs Mixture in Chromatography

Under suitable conditions, a pure substance may produce a single spot, while a mixture may produce several spots.

44. 🧠 Drawing Conclusions

A conclusion should be based on the evidence obtained during the experiment.

🔹 Good Conclusion

A good conclusion:

  • Answers the experimental question.
  • Uses the results as evidence.
  • Does not claim more than the data support.

✏️ Example

If increasing temperature consistently reduces the time needed for a reaction to reach a specified endpoint, the evidence supports the conclusion that increasing temperature increased the reaction rate over the investigated range.

45. 🔍 Identifying Unknown Substances

Unknown substances can be identified by carrying out a sequence of appropriate tests.

🪜 General Approach

  1. Observe the physical appearance where relevant.
  2. Test whether the substance is acidic, neutral or alkaline.
  3. Test for relevant anions.
  4. Test for relevant cations.
  5. Test gases if a gas is produced.
  6. Compare the observations with known chemical properties.
  7. Use all available evidence before reaching a conclusion.

⚠️ Important

Do not identify an unknown substance from one observation alone when several substances could give the same result.

48. 📝 Writing a Procedure

A procedure describes how an experiment is carried out.

🔹 A Good Procedure Should

  • Be arranged in logical order.
  • Identify important quantities.
  • Identify important apparatus.
  • State important measurements.
  • Explain how observations are obtained.
  • Include relevant safety precautions.

💡 Exam Tip

Avoid vague instructions such as "do the experiment and observe". State exactly what is measured or observed.

50. 🎯 How to Answer Practical Questions

1️⃣ Identify What Is Being Asked

Is the question asking for an apparatus, observation, inference, conclusion, precaution, calculation or explanation?

2️⃣ Look for Experimental Clues

Identify the substances, apparatus, measurements and changes described in the question.

3️⃣ Give Observable Results

If asked for an observation, describe what would actually be seen, heard or measured.

4️⃣ Give the Chemical Interpretation

If asked for an inference or conclusion, connect the observation to the relevant Chemistry.

5️⃣ Check Units and Data

Make sure numerical answers contain appropriate units and calculations use the information given.

51. ⚠️ Common Practical Chemistry Mistakes

Mistake Better Approach
Confusing observation with inference. Separate what is seen from what it means.
Using vague observations. Describe the actual colour, precipitate, gas or measurement.
Choosing the wrong apparatus. Match the apparatus to the required measurement.
Reading a scale from an angle. Read at eye level.
Forgetting units. Include units in measurements and calculations.
Changing several variables. Keep relevant control variables constant.
Calling every unusual result an error. Use the pattern of results to identify anomalies.
Giving a conclusion without evidence. Link the conclusion to the experimental results.
Using an unsuitable separation method. Choose a method based on physical properties.

🧠 Important Definitions

  • Filtration: A method of separating an insoluble solid from a liquid.
  • Residue: The solid retained by filter paper during filtration.
  • Filtrate: The liquid that passes through the filter paper.
  • Distillation: A separation method involving vaporisation and condensation, commonly based on differences in boiling points.
  • Distillate: The liquid collected after condensation during distillation.
  • Chromatography: A method used to separate and investigate components of a mixture.
  • Precipitate: An insoluble solid formed during a reaction in solution.
  • Effervescence: The formation of bubbles caused by gas being released.
  • Observation: Something directly seen, heard or measured during an experiment.
  • Inference: A conclusion drawn from observations and scientific knowledge.
  • Accuracy: How close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.
  • Precision: How closely repeated measurements agree with one another.
  • Anomalous result: A result that does not fit the general pattern of the data.
  • Independent variable: The variable deliberately changed in an investigation.
  • Dependent variable: The variable measured or observed.
  • Control variable: A relevant factor kept constant during an investigation.
  • Concordant results: Repeated measurements that closely agree.
  • Titre: The volume of solution delivered from a burette in a titration.
  • Rf value: The ratio of the distance travelled by a substance to the distance travelled by the solvent front in chromatography.
