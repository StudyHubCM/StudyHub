🧪 Biological Molecules & Food

Learn about carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, DNA, nutrients, food tests, their functions, sources and the key facts needed for your O/L Biology examination.

1. Introduction to Biological Molecules

Biological molecules are chemical substances found in living organisms. They are essential for building cells, releasing energy, carrying out life processes and controlling activities in the body.

The major biological molecules studied at O/L level are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and DNA.

⭐ Key Idea

Most biological molecules contain the element carbon (C). This is why many of them are described as organic molecules.

Important Terms

  • Monomer: a small molecule that can join with other similar molecules to form a larger molecule.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made from many smaller repeating units called monomers.
  • Biological molecule: a molecule produced by or found in living organisms.
  • Nutrient: a substance obtained from food that is needed by the body for energy, growth, repair and proper functioning.

2. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic compounds mainly made from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

Elements in Carbohydrates

  • Carbon (C)
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Oxygen (O)

Building Blocks of Carbohydrates

Simple sugars such as glucose are small carbohydrate molecules. Many glucose molecules can join together to form large carbohydrate molecules.

Glucose → Starch

Glucose → Glycogen

Glucose → Cellulose

Examples of Carbohydrates

  • Glucose – a simple sugar used in respiration.
  • Starch – a storage carbohydrate found mainly in plants.
  • Glycogen – a storage carbohydrate found mainly in animals.
  • Cellulose – forms the cell walls of plants.

Importance of Carbohydrates

  1. Provide energy for respiration.
  2. Glucose is an important fuel for cells.
  3. Starch provides an energy store in plants.
  4. Glycogen provides an energy store in animals.
  5. Cellulose provides strength and support to plant cell walls.
  6. Carbohydrates provide energy needed for physical activities such as movement.
  7. Glucose provides energy for active processes such as active transport.
  8. Carbohydrates can be broken down and used to release energy during respiration.

Sources of Carbohydrates

  • Rice
  • Corn / maize
  • Cassava
  • Plantain
  • Potatoes
  • Yams
  • Bread
  • Pasta
  • Fruits
O/L Exam Tip: Remember: Starch, glycogen and cellulose are made from glucose.

3. Lipids – Fats and Oils

Lipids include fats and oils. They are organic compounds mainly containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

Elements in Lipids

  • Carbon (C)
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Oxygen (O)

Building Blocks of Lipids

Lipids are made from fatty acids and glycerol.

Fatty acids + glycerol → Lipid

Fats and Oils

Fats are generally solid at room temperature, while oils are generally liquid at room temperature.

Importance of Lipids

  1. Provide a concentrated source of energy.
  2. Store energy for long-term use.
  3. Provide thermal insulation and help reduce heat loss from the body.
  4. Protect delicate organs from physical damage.
  5. Form an important part of cell membranes.
  6. Provide fatty acids needed for normal body functions.
  7. Help the body absorb fat-soluble vitamins.
  8. Provide energy when carbohydrate stores are limited.

Sources of Lipids

  • Vegetable oils
  • Groundnuts / peanuts
  • Avocado
  • Butter
  • Milk
  • Egg yolk
  • Meat
  • Fish
  • Nuts and seeds
O/L Exam Tip: Do not say that lipids are made from glucose. Lipids are made from fatty acids and glycerol.

4. Proteins

Proteins are large biological molecules made from smaller molecules called amino acids.

Elements in Proteins

  • Carbon (C)
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Oxygen (O)
  • Nitrogen (N)

Some proteins may also contain other elements such as sulfur, but at O/L level the important elements to remember are C, H, O and N.

Building Blocks of Proteins

Amino acids → Proteins

Importance of Proteins

  1. Needed for growth of the body.
  2. Used to repair damaged or worn-out tissues.
  3. Used to build new cells and tissues.
  4. Form enzymes that control chemical reactions.
  5. Form antibodies involved in defence against disease-causing organisms.
  6. Form important structures such as muscles.
  7. Help form some hormones involved in controlling body processes.
  8. Can be used as an energy source when necessary.

Sources of Proteins

  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Meat
  • Milk
  • Beans
  • Groundnuts / peanuts
  • Soya beans
  • Lentils
O/L Exam Tip: If asked: "What are proteins made from?" Answer: Amino acids.

5. DNA

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid.

DNA is an important biological molecule that contains genetic information used by cells.

Elements in DNA

  • Carbon (C)
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Oxygen (O)
  • Nitrogen (N)
  • Phosphorus (P)

Building Blocks of DNA

DNA is made from smaller units called nucleotides.

Nucleotides → DNA

Basic Structure of DNA

  1. DNA consists of two strands.
  2. The two strands are coiled around each other.
  3. This shape is called a double helix.
  4. Each strand contains chemical bases.
  5. Bases on opposite strands pair together.
  6. The paired bases help hold the two strands together.

Importance of DNA

  1. Stores genetic information.
  2. Contains instructions used by cells.
  3. Helps determine inherited characteristics.
  4. Allows genetic information to be passed from parents to offspring.
  5. Provides information needed for the production of proteins.
  6. Helps cells maintain their normal activities by controlling protein production.
O/L Exam Tip: The key words to remember are: DNA → nucleotides → genetic information → double helix.

7. Food Tests

Food tests are laboratory tests used to determine whether particular biological molecules are present in a food sample.

⭐ Four Important O/L Food Tests

  1. Iodine test → starch
  2. Benedict's test → reducing sugars
  3. Biuret test → protein
  4. Ethanol emulsion test → lipids

8. Test for Starch – Iodine Test

Reagent

Iodine solution

Procedure

  1. Place a small amount of the food sample on a suitable surface or in a test container.
  2. Add a few drops of iodine solution.
  3. Observe the colour change.

Results

Result Observation Conclusion
Positive Blue-black Starch is present
Negative Remains yellow-brown / orange-brown Starch is absent
Remember: Iodine + starch = blue-black.

9. Test for Reducing Sugar – Benedict's Test

Benedict's solution is used to test for reducing sugars, including glucose.

Reagent

Benedict's solution

Procedure

  1. Place the food sample or its solution in a test tube.
  2. Add Benedict's solution.
  3. Heat the mixture in a hot water bath.
  4. Observe the final colour.

Results

A positive test produces a colour change from blue through shades such as green, yellow or orange to brick-red, depending on the amount of reducing sugar present.

Observation Meaning
Blue Little or no reducing sugar detected
Green Small amount of reducing sugar
Yellow More reducing sugar
Orange High amount of reducing sugar
Brick-red precipitate Large amount of reducing sugar
Very Important: Benedict's test requires heating.

10. Test for Protein – Biuret Test

Reagent

Biuret reagent

Procedure

  1. Place the food sample or its solution in a test tube.
  2. Add biuret reagent.
  3. Mix gently.
  4. Observe the colour.

Results

Observation Conclusion
Lilac / purple Protein is present
Remains blue Protein is absent
Remember: Biuret + protein = lilac / purple.

11. Test for Lipids – Ethanol Emulsion Test

Reagents

Ethanol and water

Procedure

  1. Place the food sample in a test tube.
  2. Add ethanol.
  3. Shake the mixture thoroughly.
  4. Add water.
  5. Observe the mixture.

Results

Observation Conclusion
White / cloudy / milky emulsion Lipid is present
Remains clear Lipid is absent
Remember: Lipid + ethanol + water = cloudy white emulsion.

12. Food Tests – Master Table

Food Substance Reagent Important Procedure Positive Result
Starch Iodine solution Add iodine Blue-black
Reducing sugar Benedict's solution Add Benedict's and heat Green → yellow → orange → brick-red
Protein Biuret reagent Add biuret reagent Lilac / purple
Lipid Ethanol + water Shake with ethanol, then add water White / cloudy emulsion

I-B-B-E

Iodine → starch

Benedict's → reducing sugar

Biuret → protein

Ethanol emulsion → lipid

13. Other Important Nutrients in Food

Besides carbohydrates, proteins and lipids, a healthy diet also requires vitamins, mineral ions, fibre and water.

Vitamins

Vitamins are needed in relatively small amounts but are essential for normal body functions.

Vitamin C

  • Helps maintain healthy tissues.
  • Supports normal growth and development.
  • Helps maintain healthy skin and gums.
  • Supports wound healing.
  • Helps the body function normally.
  • Found in fruits and many vegetables.

Vitamin D

  • Helps the body absorb calcium.
  • Important for healthy bones.
  • Important for healthy teeth.
  • Supports normal growth.
  • Helps maintain calcium balance.
  • Can be obtained through sunlight exposure and food.

Calcium

  • Needed for strong bones.
  • Needed for strong teeth.
  • Important in muscle contraction.
  • Important in blood clotting.
  • Helps maintain normal cell function.
  • Important during growth and development.

Iron

  • Needed to make haemoglobin.
  • Haemoglobin is found in red blood cells.
  • Helps red blood cells transport oxygen.
  • Supports aerobic respiration by supplying oxygen to tissues.
  • Important for normal growth and development.
  • Iron deficiency can lead to anaemia.

Dietary Fibre / Roughage

  1. Adds bulk to food in the digestive system.
  2. Helps food move through the intestine.
  3. Helps prevent constipation.
  4. Helps maintain normal bowel movement.
  5. Supports a healthy digestive system.
  6. Found mainly in plant foods such as fruits, vegetables and whole grains.

Water

  1. Acts as a solvent for many substances.
  2. Provides a medium for many chemical reactions.
  3. Helps transport dissolved substances.
  4. Helps transport waste products.
  5. Helps regulate body temperature.
  6. Is needed for many processes in cells.
  7. Helps maintain the water content of cells.
  8. Is lost from the body through processes such as sweating and urination.

14. Balanced Diet

A balanced diet is a diet that contains the different nutrients needed by the body in the correct proportions for good health.

A Balanced Diet Should Provide

  • Carbohydrates
  • Proteins
  • Lipids
  • Vitamins
  • Mineral ions
  • Fibre
  • Water

Why a Balanced Diet is Important

  1. Provides energy for daily activities.
  2. Provides materials for growth.
  3. Allows repair of damaged tissues.
  4. Supports normal body functions.
  5. Helps maintain healthy bones and teeth.
  6. Supports the immune system.
  7. Helps prevent nutrient deficiency diseases.
  8. Helps maintain normal body weight and health.

15. Nutrient Deficiency

A deficiency occurs when the body does not receive enough of a particular nutrient.

Nutrient Deficiency Effect
Iron Anaemia
Vitamin D Weak / poorly developed bones
Calcium Weak bones and teeth
Vitamin C Poor health of gums and impaired wound healing
Protein Poor growth and tissue repair

16. How to Answer Food-Test Questions

In an examination, do not give only the name of the reagent. A strong answer normally includes the sample, reagent, important condition and observation/conclusion.

Example

Question: Describe how you would test a food sample for starch.

Answer: Add iodine solution to the food sample. If starch is present, the colour changes to blue-black.

For Benedict's Test

Always remember the word HEAT.

Add Benedict's solution to the food sample and heat the mixture in a hot water bath. A positive result is shown by a colour change from blue towards green, yellow, orange or brick-red.

For Protein

Mention biuret and purple/lilac.

For Lipid

Mention ethanol, water and cloudy white emulsion.

17. Common O/L Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Do not say iodine tests for glucose. Iodine tests for starch.
  2. Do not forget to heat Benedict's solution when testing for reducing sugar.
  3. Do not say biuret gives a blue-black colour. Protein gives a lilac/purple result.
  4. Do not confuse lipids with proteins.
  5. Do not say proteins are made from glucose. Proteins are made from amino acids.
  6. Do not say lipids are made from amino acids. Lipids are made from fatty acids and glycerol.
  7. Do not confuse glycogen with starch. Glycogen is mainly the storage carbohydrate in animals, while starch is mainly a storage carbohydrate in plants.
  8. Do not write only "colour changes" in a food-test question. State the actual colour.
