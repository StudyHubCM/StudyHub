BIOLOGY • A/L 0710

🌍 Classification & Diversity of Life

Understand biological diversity, taxonomy, classification, nomenclature, evolutionary relationships and biological keys.

🌍 A/L Biology Unit 1

Classification and diversity of life help biologists organise the enormous variety of organisms on Earth. At A/L level, classification is not simply memorising names. It involves identifying organisms, comparing their characteristics, understanding relationships between groups and using evidence to determine how organisms are related.

This unit introduces Biology at an advanced level and develops a detailed understanding of taxonomy, classification, nomenclature, biodiversity, evolutionary relationships and biological identification keys.

1. 🌱 Introduction to Biology

Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms. It investigates how organisms are structured, how they function, how they reproduce, how they interact with one another and their environment, and how they have changed over time.

At A/L level, Biology requires more than recalling facts. Students should be able to explain processes, interpret data, compare organisms, analyse evidence, identify structures and apply biological principles to unfamiliar situations.

🎯 What A/L Biology Requires

  • Accurate biological knowledge.
  • Understanding rather than simple memorisation.
  • Ability to interpret diagrams and biological specimens.
  • Ability to analyse tables, graphs and experimental results.
  • Correct use of biological terminology.
  • Ability to apply knowledge to unfamiliar organisms and situations.
  • Practical observation and identification skills.

2. 🧬 Characteristics of Living Organisms

Living organisms share a number of fundamental characteristics. These characteristics distinguish living systems from non-living objects.

Characteristic Meaning Example
Movement Ability to change position or move parts of the body. Animals moving; plant shoots growing towards light.
Respiration Chemical reactions that release energy from nutrients. Glucose being broken down to release energy.
Sensitivity Ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment. Withdrawal of a hand from a hot object.
Growth Permanent increase in size and dry mass through cell division and/or enlargement. A seedling developing into a mature plant.
Reproduction Production of new organisms. Sexual reproduction in humans or asexual reproduction in bacteria.
Excretion Removal of metabolic waste products and substances present in excess. Removal of carbon dioxide during respiration.
Nutrition Obtaining and using nutrients required for energy, growth and maintenance. Plants making glucose by photosynthesis.

🧠 A/L Point

The characteristics should be understood rather than memorised as isolated words. For example, growth in Biology means a permanent increase in size and/or dry mass, while simply becoming larger because of water absorption is not necessarily biological growth.

3. 🔬 Levels of Biological Organisation

Living systems are organised from very small levels to increasingly complex levels.

A useful sequence is:

⚛️ Atom → 🧪 Molecule → 🧬 Organelle → 🔬 Cell → 🧫 Tissue → 🫀 Organ → 🧠 Organ System → 🐾 Organism → 👥 Population → 🌿 Community → 🌍 Ecosystem → 🌎 Biosphere

Level Description Example
Atom Smallest unit of an element. Carbon atom.
Molecule Two or more atoms chemically joined. Water, glucose.
Organelle Specialised structure inside a cell. Mitochondrion.
Cell Basic structural and functional unit of life. Red blood cell.
Tissue Group of similar cells performing a particular function. Muscle tissue.
Organ Structure made from different tissues working together. Heart.
Organ system Several organs working together. Digestive system.
Organism An individual living organism. Human, mango tree, bacterium.
Population Members of the same species living in an area. All frogs of one species in a pond.
Community All populations of different species living and interacting in an area. Plants, insects, birds and microorganisms in a forest.
Ecosystem A community interacting with its non-living environment. A pond ecosystem.
Biosphere All regions of Earth where life exists. The global biosphere.

6. 📖 Biological Terminology & Conventions

Biology uses precise terminology so that scientists around the world can communicate accurately.

✍️ Important Conventions

  • Scientific names are normally written in Latinised form.
  • The genus name begins with a capital letter.
  • The species epithet begins with a lowercase letter.
  • The complete binomial name is italicised when typed.
  • When handwritten, scientific names are traditionally underlined separately.
  • Units should be written correctly and consistently.
  • Biological structures should be labelled clearly on diagrams.
  • Specialised biological terms should be used instead of vague everyday language.

🧬 TAXONOMY ⭐⭐⭐

Taxonomy is the branch of Biology concerned with the identification, naming and classification of organisms.

Taxonomy allows scientists to organise the enormous diversity of life into groups based on shared characteristics and relationships.

At A/L level, taxonomy should be understood as a scientific system rather than simply a list of names.

7. 🧬 What is Taxonomy?

Taxonomy is the scientific study of the principles and practice of identifying, naming and classifying organisms.

The three closely related activities are:

  • Identification — determining what an organism is.
  • Nomenclature — giving an organism a scientific name.
  • Classification — placing organisms into groups based on shared characteristics.

8. 🔍 Identification

Identification is the process of determining the identity of an unknown organism.

Identification may involve observing:

  • Body structure.
  • Number and arrangement of body parts.
  • Presence or absence of particular structures.
  • Type of symmetry.
  • Mode of nutrition.
  • Reproductive structures.
  • Cell type.
  • Genetic or molecular characteristics.
  • Ecological characteristics.

Identification keys are important tools used to identify unknown organisms.

9. 🏷️ Nomenclature

Nomenclature is the system of giving names to organisms according to internationally accepted rules.

Common names can cause confusion because the same organism may have different names in different regions or languages. Scientific names provide a more universal system.

10. 🗂️ Classification

Classification is the arrangement of organisms into groups according to their similarities, differences and biological relationships.

Classification makes the study of biodiversity easier and helps scientists recognise relationships between organisms.

🎯 Why Classify Organisms?

  • To organise the enormous diversity of life.
  • To make identification easier.
  • To provide a universal method of communication.
  • To recognise similarities and differences.
  • To show evolutionary relationships.
  • To predict characteristics of organisms based on their group.

11. 🪜 Taxonomic Hierarchy

Organisms are classified into a hierarchy of increasingly specific groups.

🪜 Main Hierarchy

Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

The higher ranks contain more organisms and are less specific. The lower ranks contain fewer organisms but share more characteristics.

12. 🌐 Domain

Domain is a taxonomic rank above Kingdom. In the three-domain system, organisms are divided into:

  • 🦠 Bacteria
  • 🧬 Archaea
  • 🌱 Eukarya

Bacteria and Archaea consist of prokaryotic organisms, while Eukarya contains organisms with eukaryotic cells.

13. 👑 Kingdom

A Kingdom is a major taxonomic group containing organisms that share broad characteristics.

Traditional five-kingdom classification commonly recognises:

  • 🦠 Monera / Prokaryotae
  • 🧫 Protoctista / Protista
  • 🍄 Fungi
  • 🌿 Plantae
  • 🦁 Animalia

⚠️ Important: Modern biological classification uses systems that recognise domains and may divide or reorganise groups differently from the traditional five-kingdom system. A/L answers should follow the classification system expected by the syllabus or examination question.

14. 🧩 Phylum

A phylum is a major subdivision of a kingdom. Organisms in the same phylum share important structural and developmental characteristics.

For example, Kingdom Animalia contains phyla such as Porifera, Cnidaria, Annelida, Mollusca, Arthropoda, Echinodermata and Chordata.

15. 🧱 Class

A class is a taxonomic rank below phylum. A phylum may contain several classes.

For example, Phylum Chordata includes Class Mammalia, Class Aves and other classes.

16. 📚 Order

An order is a group of related families.

For example, humans belong to Order Primates.

17. 👨‍👩‍👧 Family

A family contains closely related genera.

Humans belong to Family Hominidae, while lions belong to Family Felidae.

18. 🧬 Genus

A genus contains one or more closely related species.

In the scientific name Homo sapiens, Homo is the genus.

19. 🔬 Species

A species is generally regarded as a group of organisms that can interbreed naturally and produce fertile offspring, although the exact definition becomes more complicated for organisms that reproduce asexually or through other mechanisms.

Species is usually the most specific major rank in the basic taxonomic hierarchy.

22. ✍️ Binomial Nomenclature

Binomial nomenclature is the system of scientific naming in which each species is given a two-part name.

The two parts are:

  1. Genus name
  2. Specific epithet

Together they form the scientific name of the species.

🧑 Example

Homo sapiens

  • Homo = genus
  • sapiens = specific epithet

23. 📜 Rules of Scientific Naming

  • The genus begins with a capital letter.
  • The specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter.
  • The complete scientific name is italicised when typed.
  • When handwritten, the two words are traditionally underlined separately.
  • The genus may be abbreviated after it has already been written in full.
Correct Incorrect Reason
Homo sapiens homo sapiens Genus must begin with a capital letter.
Panthera leo Panthera Leo Specific epithet begins with lowercase.
Escherichia coli Escherichia Coli Specific epithet must be lowercase.

24. 🔤 Abbreviating Scientific Names

Once the genus has been written in full, it can usually be abbreviated to its initial letter.

Example:

Homo sapiens → H. sapiens

Escherichia coli → E. coli

The abbreviation should only be used when the genus is already clear from the context.

25. 🗃️ Taxonomic Groups

Each rank represents a taxonomic group. A taxon (plural: taxa) is a group of organisms recognised as a unit at a particular level of classification.

For example, Animalia is a taxon at the Kingdom level, while Mammalia is a taxon at the Class level.

26. 🧩 Nested Classification

Taxonomic groups are nested inside one another.

🌐 Domain
↳ 👑 Kingdom
↳ 🧩 Phylum
↳ 🧱 Class
↳ 📚 Order
↳ 👨‍👩‍👧 Family
↳ 🧬 Genus
↳ 🔬 Species

The closer two organisms are in the hierarchy, the more characteristics they generally share.

27. ⚖️ Artificial Classification

Artificial classification groups organisms using a small number of selected characteristics, often chosen for convenience.

For example, organisms could be grouped simply according to whether they live in water or on land.

⚠️ An artificial classification may be useful for practical identification, but it may not accurately represent evolutionary relationships.

28. 🌳 Natural Classification

Natural classification groups organisms using many characteristics so that the groups better reflect their natural similarities and relationships.

Characteristics may include:

  • Structural characteristics.
  • Cellular characteristics.
  • Biochemical characteristics.
  • Reproductive characteristics.
  • Developmental characteristics.
  • Genetic characteristics.

29. ⚖️ Artificial vs Natural Classification

Artificial Classification Natural Classification
Uses selected characteristics. Uses many characteristics.
Mainly designed for convenience. Attempts to reflect natural relationships.
May place unrelated organisms together. Groups organisms according to broader evidence.
Can be relatively simple. Usually requires more biological information.

30. 🔬 Modern Classification

Modern classification uses evidence from many areas of Biology. Scientists no longer depend only on visible appearance.

Evidence can include:

  • 🦴 Comparative anatomy.
  • 🧬 DNA and RNA sequences.
  • 🧪 Protein and biochemical similarities.
  • 🧫 Cell structure.
  • 🌱 Embryological development.
  • 🪨 Fossil evidence.
  • 🌳 Evolutionary relationships.

31. 🧬 Molecular Evidence in Classification

DNA provides powerful evidence for determining relationships between organisms.

Organisms with more similar DNA sequences are generally considered to have a more recent common ancestor than organisms with greater genetic differences, when the sequences being compared are appropriate for the question.

Scientists can compare genes, DNA sequences, RNA sequences or protein sequences to investigate evolutionary relationships.

32. 🌳 Evolutionary Relationships

Classification can be used to represent evolutionary relationships between organisms.

Organisms that share a more recent common ancestor are generally more closely related than organisms whose common ancestor lived much further in the past.

🧠 Key Idea

Classification is increasingly based on phylogeny — the evolutionary history and relationships of organisms.

33. 🌿 Homologous Structures

Homologous structures are structures in different organisms that have the same basic evolutionary origin but may have different functions.

The forelimbs of humans, bats and whales provide examples of structures based on the same underlying skeletal plan.

Homologous structures provide evidence of common ancestry.

34. 🔄 Analogous Structures

Analogous structures perform similar functions but have different evolutionary origins and structural plans.

They can arise when unrelated organisms experience similar environmental pressures.

🔑 Homologous = common evolutionary origin.
🔑 Analogous = similar function, different origin.

35. 🌳 Phylogenetic Classification

Phylogenetic classification attempts to group organisms according to their evolutionary relationships.

Modern phylogenetic studies commonly use molecular and anatomical evidence to reconstruct relationships.

36. 🌳 Cladograms

A cladogram is a branching diagram used to represent hypotheses about relationships between organisms or groups.

Organisms that branch from a more recent common point are generally interpreted as being more closely related than organisms that share only an older common point.

37. 🧬 Shared Derived Characteristics

A shared derived characteristic is a characteristic that evolved in a particular ancestral lineage and is shared by members of a later group.

These characteristics are useful when constructing phylogenetic hypotheses.

38. 🧬 Common Ancestors

Organisms belonging to related groups share common ancestors. The more recent the common ancestor, the more closely related the organisms are considered to be.

This explains why classification can provide information about evolutionary history.

44. 🍄 Classification of a Mushroom

A common mushroom can be classified within Kingdom Fungi. The exact classification depends on the species being considered.

Important fungal characteristics include:

  • Eukaryotic cells.
  • Cell walls containing chitin in most fungi.
  • No chlorophyll.
  • Heterotrophic nutrition.
  • Many fungi possess hyphae forming a mycelium.
  • Many reproduce using spores.

45. 🧫 Classification of Amoeba

Amoeboid organisms traditionally studied as protists belong to the broad group commonly referred to as Protista / Protoctista in traditional five-kingdom systems.

Important characteristics include:

  • Eukaryotic cell structure.
  • Usually unicellular.
  • Movement using temporary cytoplasmic extensions called pseudopodia.
  • Heterotrophic nutrition in typical free-living amoebae.

47. 🦋 Classification of an Insect

Insects belong to:

  • Domain: Eukarya
  • Kingdom: Animalia
  • Phylum: Arthropoda
  • Class: Insecta

The exact order and lower ranks depend on the particular insect. For example, butterflies belong to Order Lepidoptera, while houseflies belong to Order Diptera.

48. 🐟 Classification of a Fish

Fish are vertebrate chordates. Modern classification recognises several distinct groups of fishes rather than treating all fish as one single natural taxonomic group.

Typical fish have characteristics such as:

  • Vertebral column.
  • Gills for aquatic gas exchange.
  • Fins adapted for swimming.
  • Aquatic lifestyle.

49. 🐦 Classification of a Bird

Birds belong to:

  • Domain: Eukarya
  • Kingdom: Animalia
  • Phylum: Chordata
  • Class: Aves

Important bird characteristics include feathers, wings, beaks and reproduction using eggs with specialised coverings.

50. 🐘 Classification of a Mammal

Mammals belong to:

  • Domain: Eukarya
  • Kingdom: Animalia
  • Phylum: Chordata
  • Class: Mammalia

Mammalian characteristics include hair or fur at some stage of life and mammary glands that produce milk for offspring.

52. 🔑 Dichotomous Keys

A dichotomous key is an identification tool that uses a sequence of two contrasting choices to identify an organism.

The word dichotomous means divided into two parts.

🔑 Basic Pattern

Choose between statement A or statement B → follow the indicated number → make another choice → continue until the organism is identified.

53. 🔍 Biological / Diagnostic Keys

A biological key or diagnostic key is a tool used to identify an unknown organism using observable characteristics.

Keys may be used to identify:

  • Plants.
  • Animals.
  • Insects.
  • Microorganisms.
  • Leaves.
  • Seeds.
  • Shells.
  • Other biological specimens.

54. 🛠️ How to Construct a Dichotomous Key

  1. List the organisms that must be identified.
  2. Observe their characteristics carefully.
  3. Choose a characteristic that separates the organisms into two groups.
  4. Write two contrasting statements.
  5. Assign each choice a number or direction.
  6. Continue dividing each group until each organism can be identified.
  7. Use clear, observable and non-overlapping characteristics.

55. 🧠 Rules for Good Biological Keys

  • Use observable characteristics.
  • Use two contrasting alternatives at each step.
  • Avoid vague terms such as "large" unless size is clearly defined.
  • Avoid characteristics that are difficult to observe.
  • Do not make the two choices overlap.
  • Use the same characteristic consistently.
  • Each final pathway should lead to one identification.

56. 🔑 Example of a Simple Dichotomous Key

Consider four imaginary organisms: A, B, C and D.

1a. Has wings → go to 2
1b. Does not have wings → go to 3

2a. Has one pair of wings → Organism A
2b. Has two pairs of wings → Organism B

3a. Has segmented body → Organism C
3b. Does not have segmented body → Organism D

The important idea is that every decision separates the remaining organisms into two groups.

57. 🚶 How to Use a Biological Key

  1. Start at the first pair of statements.
  2. Observe the specimen.
  3. Choose the statement that correctly describes it.
  4. Follow the instruction to the next pair.
  5. Continue making choices.
  6. Stop when the organism has been identified.

58. ⚠️ Common Problems When Using Keys

  • Choosing a characteristic that the specimen does not actually possess.
  • Ignoring one of the two alternatives.
  • Skipping a numbered step.
  • Using assumptions rather than visible evidence.
  • Confusing similar structures.
  • Failing to read both alternatives before choosing.

60. 🧠 How to Read a Classification Table

When comparing organisms, look for the highest taxonomic rank they share.

For example, humans and lions share:

Domain → Eukarya
Kingdom → Animalia
Phylum → Chordata
Class → Mammalia

However, they differ at Order:

  • Human → Primates
  • Lion → Carnivora

Therefore, they are closely related as mammals but are not members of the same order.

61. 🧬 Taxonomic Distance

The number of taxonomic ranks shared by two organisms can provide a simple indication of their classification similarity.

Organisms sharing the same genus are classified more specifically together than organisms that only share the same kingdom.

🔑 Same species → extremely close classification
🔑 Same genus → very closely related group
🔑 Same family → related but less closely
🔑 Same kingdom only → broad relationship

62. 🧬 Taxonomy and Evolution

Modern taxonomy is closely connected with evolutionary Biology. Classification can provide hypotheses about the common ancestry of organisms.

Molecular evidence has changed some traditional classifications because organisms that appear similar may not always be closely related, while organisms that look different may share significant genetic similarities.

63. 🔬 Evidence Used in Modern Classification

Evidence How It Helps
Morphology Compares external structures and form.
Anatomy Compares internal structures.
Cell Biology Compares cellular structures.
Embryology Compares developmental patterns.
Fossils Provides evidence about organisms from the past.
DNA Allows comparison of genetic sequences.

64. 🧬 Similarity Does Not Always Mean Close Relationship

Organisms may independently evolve similar characteristics because they experience similar environmental pressures. This is known as convergent evolution.

Therefore, classification should consider multiple types of evidence rather than relying on one visible feature.

65. 🌍 Biological Diversity

Biodiversity is the variety of life in a particular area or on Earth as a whole.

Biodiversity can be considered at several levels:

  • 🧬 Genetic diversity.
  • 🐾 Species diversity.
  • 🌳 Ecosystem diversity.

Classification provides a systematic way of describing and studying this diversity.

66. 🌱 Genetic Diversity

Genetic diversity refers to differences in genetic information among individuals of the same species or between populations.

It provides the raw material on which natural selection can act and helps populations respond to environmental change.

67. 🐾 Species Diversity

Species diversity refers to the variety of species present in a particular habitat or region.

A tropical forest may contain many different species of plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms.

68. 🌍 Ecosystem Diversity

Ecosystem diversity refers to the variety of ecosystems found in a region.

Examples include:

  • 🌳 Forest ecosystems.
  • 🌊 Freshwater ecosystems.
  • 🌴 Coastal ecosystems.
  • 🌾 Grasslands.
  • 🏜️ Dry ecosystems.
  • 🐚 Marine ecosystems.

69. 📝 Worked Classification Question 1

Question

Two organisms belong to the same kingdom and phylum, but one belongs to Class Mammalia and the other to Class Aves. Are they in the same class?

Answer

No. They belong to different classes. They share the same kingdom and phylum but separate at the class level.

70. 📝 Worked Classification Question 2

Question

Two organisms belong to the same genus but have different species epithets. What can you conclude?

Answer

They are classified in the same genus but are different species.

71. 📝 Worked Classification Question 3

Question

An organism has the scientific name Panthera leo. Identify its genus.

Answer

The genus is Panthera.

72. 📝 Worked Classification Question 4

Question

Which is more specific: Kingdom or Family?

Answer

Family is more specific than Kingdom.

73. 📝 Worked Classification Question 5

Question

Why are scientific names useful when studying organisms from different countries?

Answer

Scientific names provide a standard naming system, reducing confusion caused by different common names used in different languages and regions.

74. 🔑 Worked Biological Key Question

Suppose an unknown specimen has:

  • No wings.
  • A segmented body.

Using the example key above:

Step 1 → no wings → go to 3
Step 3 → segmented body → Organism C

Therefore, the specimen is identified as Organism C.

75. 🧠 How to Answer Taxonomy Questions

  1. Read the question carefully.
  2. Identify the taxonomic rank being asked for.
  3. Check the characteristics provided.
  4. Compare the organism with the appropriate classification group.
  5. Use scientific terminology.
  6. Write scientific names using the correct convention.
  7. Do not confuse genus with species.
  8. For key questions, follow every step in order.

Phylogeny The evolutionary history and relationships of organisms.
Cladogram A branching diagram representing hypotheses about evolutionary relationships.
Dichotomous key An identification key based on successive pairs of contrasting choices.
Biodiversity The variety of life, including genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.
