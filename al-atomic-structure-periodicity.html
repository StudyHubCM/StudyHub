Advanced GCE-standard notes covering atomic structure, electronic configuration, ionisation energy and periodic trends.

1. ⚛️ Introduction to Atomic Structure

Atomic structure deals with the arrangement and behaviour of subatomic particles within atoms and explains how the electronic structure of an element determines its chemical properties.

🎯 A/L Focus

  • Subatomic particles and their properties
  • Atomic number and mass number
  • Isotopes and relative atomic mass
  • Mass spectrometry
  • Electromagnetic radiation and atomic spectra
  • Energy levels and orbitals
  • Electron configurations
  • Ion formation
  • Ionisation energy
  • Periodic trends and their explanations

2. 🔬 Subatomic Particles

An atom consists of a very small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons occupying regions of space called orbitals.

Particle Relative Charge Relative Mass Location
Proton +1 1 Nucleus
Neutron 0 1 Nucleus
Electron −1 Approximately 1/1836 Outside nucleus

🧠 Important Point

Almost all of an atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus because protons and neutrons have much greater masses than electrons.

3. 🔢 Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic Number, Z

The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

For a neutral atom:

Number of protons = Number of electrons

Mass Number, A

The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

A = number of protons + number of neutrons

Therefore:

Number of neutrons = A − Z

✏️ Example

Consider an atom written as ²³₁₁Na.

  • Protons = 11
  • Electrons = 11
  • Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12

4. 🧬 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Property Same in isotopes Different in isotopes
Atomic number Yes No
Number of protons Yes No
Number of electrons in neutral atoms Yes No
Mass number No Yes
Number of neutrons No Yes

⚗️ Chemical Behaviour of Isotopes

Isotopes of an element have essentially the same chemical behaviour because they have the same electronic configuration in neutral atoms.

Their physical properties, particularly mass-related properties, can differ.

5. ⚖️ Relative Atomic Mass, Aᵣ

The relative atomic mass of an element is the weighted mean mass of its naturally occurring isotopes compared with 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

🧮 Calculation

When isotopic abundances are given:

Aᵣ = Σ(isotopic mass × fractional abundance)

✏️ Example

Suppose an element has two isotopes:

  • Isotope 1: mass = 10, abundance = 20%
  • Isotope 2: mass = 11, abundance = 80%

Aᵣ = (10 × 0.20) + (11 × 0.80)

Aᵣ = 10.8

6. 🧪 Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry can be used to identify isotopes and determine their relative abundances.

🔄 Main Stages

  1. Ionisation
  2. Acceleration
  3. Deflection
  4. Detection

1️⃣ Ionisation

Atoms are converted into positive ions by removing electrons.

Word equation:

Atom → Positive ion + Electron

Symbol equation:

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

2️⃣ Acceleration

Positive ions are accelerated by an electric field so that they travel at high speed.

3️⃣ Deflection

The ions are deflected according to their mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.

Lighter ions are generally deflected more strongly than heavier ions under the same conditions.

4️⃣ Detection

Ions are detected and produce signals whose intensities are related to their relative abundances.

7. 🌈 Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic radiation transfers energy through oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

Region Relative Wavelength Relative Frequency
Radio waves Longest Lowest
Microwaves ↓ ↑
Infrared ↓ ↑
Visible light ↓ ↑
Ultraviolet ↓ ↑
X-rays ↓ ↑
Gamma rays Shortest Highest

📐 Relationship Between Frequency and Wavelength

c = fλ

  • c = speed of electromagnetic radiation
  • f = frequency
  • λ = wavelength

Therefore, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional when the speed of light is constant.

⚡ Photon Energy

E = hf

  • E = photon energy
  • h = Planck constant
  • f = frequency

Higher-frequency radiation has higher energy per photon.

8. 💡 Atomic Spectra and Energy Levels

When atoms absorb energy, electrons can move from lower energy levels to higher energy levels.

When electrons return to lower energy levels, energy is released as electromagnetic radiation.

🌟 Line Spectra

Excited atoms produce characteristic line spectra because electrons can only occupy specific allowed energy levels.

Each element therefore has a characteristic spectrum.

9. 🌀 Orbitals and Quantum Ideas

An orbital is a region of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.

An orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons, provided they have opposite spins.

🔵 s Orbital

An s orbital is approximately spherical in shape.

🟠 p Orbitals

There are three p orbitals in a p subshell.

  • pₓ
  • pᵧ
  • p𝓏

The three p orbitals have the same energy in an isolated atom but have different orientations in space.

🟣 d Orbitals

A d subshell contains five orbitals and can accommodate a maximum of ten electrons.

Subshell Number of Orbitals Maximum Electrons
s 1 2
p 3 6
d 5 10
f 7 14

10. 🧩 Electron Configuration

Electrons occupy orbitals in a way that gives the atom its lowest possible energy under the relevant conditions.

📌 Aufbau Principle

Electrons occupy lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy orbitals.

A commonly used filling order is:

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p

🧲 Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in the same atom can have an identical set of four quantum numbers.

Consequently, an orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons, and they must have opposite spins.

↔️ Hund's Rule

When electrons occupy orbitals of equal energy, they occupy separate orbitals singly before pairing occurs.

✏️ Example — Nitrogen

Nitrogen has atomic number 7.

Electron configuration:

1s² 2s² 2p³

The three 2p electrons occupy the three p orbitals singly before any pairing occurs.

12. 🔩 Transition-Element Electron Configurations

For many transition elements, the 4s orbital is filled before the 3d orbitals.

However, the relative energies of 4s and 3d orbitals change when electrons are removed or when atoms are ionised.

⚠️ Important Exceptions

Some transition elements have configurations that differ from the simple predicted filling order because arrangements with particular d-electron populations can be relatively stable.

📌 Common A/L Examples

Chromium: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹

Copper: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹

⚡ Forming Transition-Metal Ions

When transition-metal ions form, electrons are generally removed from the 4s orbital before the 3d orbital.

Example:

Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²

Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶

13. ➕ Ion Formation and Electronic Stability

Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons.

➕ Cations

A cation is formed when an atom loses one or more electrons.

Word equation:

Sodium atom → Sodium ion + Electron

Symbol equation:

Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻

➖ Anions

An anion is formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.

Word equation:

Chlorine atom + Electron → Chloride ion

Symbol equation:

Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g)

🔵 Isoelectronic Species

Species are isoelectronic when they have the same number of electrons and therefore the same electron configuration.

For example:

O²⁻, F⁻, Ne and Na⁺ all contain 10 electrons.

14. ⚡ First Ionisation Energy

The first ionisation energy is the enthalpy change required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

General equation:

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Ionisation energy is normally positive because energy must be supplied to overcome the attraction between the nucleus and the electron.

🎯 Factors Affecting Ionisation Energy

  • Nuclear charge
  • Atomic radius
  • Shielding effect
  • Subshell occupied by the electron
  • Electron-electron repulsion

🔵 Nuclear Charge

A greater nuclear charge generally produces stronger attraction between the nucleus and an outer electron, increasing ionisation energy.

🛡️ Shielding

Inner-shell electrons reduce the effective attraction between the nucleus and an outer electron.

📏 Distance from the Nucleus

An electron farther from the nucleus generally experiences weaker attraction and is easier to remove.

15. 📈 Successive Ionisation Energies

Successive ionisation energies are the energies required to remove electrons one at a time from one mole of gaseous ions.

Each successive ionisation usually requires more energy because the ion becomes increasingly positive and the remaining electrons are more strongly attracted to the nucleus.

📊 Large Jumps

A very large increase between two successive ionisation energies indicates that an electron is being removed from a new, inner shell.

✏️ Example — Sodium

Sodium has the configuration:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹

The first electron is removed from the third shell. After that, the next electron must be removed from an inner shell much closer to the nucleus.

Therefore, there is a very large jump between the first and second ionisation energies.

🧠 Using Successive Ionisation Energies

The position of a large jump can be used to determine the number of outer-shell electrons in an atom.

16. 📊 Periodic Trends

Periodicity is the repetition of similar physical and chemical properties at regular intervals when elements are arranged according to increasing atomic number.

➡️ Across a Period

Across a period, proton number increases while electrons are added to the same principal energy level.

Shielding does not increase greatly because the additional electrons enter the same main shell.

Consequently, effective nuclear attraction generally increases.

⬇️ Down a Group

Down a group, electrons occupy progressively higher principal energy levels.

Atomic radius and shielding generally increase.

17. 📏 Atomic and Ionic Radius

Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom.

Because the boundary of an atom is not sharply defined, atomic radius is commonly determined from measured internuclear distances between bonded atoms.

➡️ Trend Across a Period

Atomic radius generally decreases across a period because nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same main shell.

⬇️ Trend Down a Group

Atomic radius generally increases down a group because additional electron shells are occupied.

➕ Cations

Cations are smaller than their corresponding neutral atoms because electron loss reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove an entire outer shell.

➖ Anions

Anions are generally larger than their corresponding neutral atoms because additional electrons increase electron-electron repulsion.

🔵 Isoelectronic Ions

For isoelectronic ions, ionic radius decreases as nuclear charge increases.

For example, among O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺, all have 10 electrons, but Mg²⁺ has the greatest nuclear charge and therefore the smallest radius.

18. ⚡ First Ionisation Energy Across the Periodic Table

➡️ Across a Period

First ionisation energy generally increases across a period.

This is mainly because nuclear charge increases while the outer electrons remain in the same principal energy level.

⬇️ Down a Group

First ionisation energy generally decreases down a group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and experiences greater shielding.

19. ⚠️ Ionisation Energy Anomalies

The overall increase in first ionisation energy across a period is not perfectly smooth.

🟠 Beryllium and Boron

Boron has a lower first ionisation energy than beryllium even though boron has a greater nuclear charge.

In beryllium, the electron removed is from a 2s subshell. In boron, the electron removed is from a higher-energy 2p subshell.

Therefore, the outer electron in boron is easier to remove.

🟢 Nitrogen and Oxygen

Oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen.

Nitrogen has a half-filled 2p subshell with one electron in each orbital. Oxygen has one paired 2p orbital.

Repulsion between paired electrons in oxygen makes one of them easier to remove.

🔵 Period 3 Analogies

Similar anomalies occur between magnesium and aluminium and between phosphorus and sulfur.

Pair Reason for the anomaly
Be → B Electron removed from higher-energy 2p subshell in B
N → O Paired-electron repulsion in O
Mg → Al Electron removed from higher-energy 3p subshell in Al
P → S Paired-electron repulsion in S

20. 🧲 Electron Affinity

Electron affinity describes the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom to form a gaseous negative ion.

General process:

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

The energy change depends on the balance between attraction from the nucleus and repulsions involving electrons.

📌 Important Distinction

Electron affinity concerns the addition of an electron, whereas ionisation energy concerns the removal of an electron.

21. 🧲 Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom in a molecule to attract the bonding pair of electrons towards itself.

➡️ Across a Period

Electronegativity generally increases across a period because effective nuclear attraction increases and atomic radius decreases.

⬇️ Down a Group

Electronegativity generally decreases down a group because atomic size and shielding increase.

⭐ Fluorine

Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

22. 🧪 Periodicity and Chemical Properties

Changes in electronic structure across periods lead to systematic changes in chemical behaviour.

🧪 Period 3 Oxides

The oxides of Period 3 elements demonstrate a general change from basic through amphoteric to acidic character.

Element Oxide General Character
Na Na₂O Basic
Mg MgO Basic
Al Al₂O₃ Amphoteric
Si SiO₂ Acidic
P P₄O₁₀ Acidic
S SO₂ / SO₃ Acidic

🧪 Example Reactions

Word equation:

Sodium oxide + water → sodium hydroxide

Symbol equation:

Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH

Word equation:

Silicon dioxide + sodium hydroxide → sodium silicate + water

Symbol equation:

SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + H₂O

24. 📝 GCE Exam-Focus Worked Examples

Question 1 — Electron Configuration

Write the electron configuration of chlorine, atomic number 17.

Answer:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵

Question 2 — Neutrons

How many neutrons are present in ³⁵₁₇Cl?

Answer:

Neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18

Question 3 — Ionisation Energy

Explain why the first ionisation energy generally increases across a period.

Key points:

  • Nuclear charge increases.
  • Electrons are added to the same principal shell.
  • Shielding changes relatively little.
  • Effective nuclear attraction increases.
  • The atomic radius generally decreases.
  • More energy is therefore required to remove the outer electron.

Question 4 — Successive Ionisation Energies

An element has a very large increase between its second and third ionisation energies. What does this suggest?

Answer:

The first two electrons were removed from the outer shell, while the third electron is being removed from an inner shell. The atom therefore has two outer-shell electrons.

25. ⚠️ Common GCE Mistakes

  • Confusing atomic number with mass number.
  • Forgetting that isotopes have the same number of protons.
  • Saying isotopes have different numbers of electrons when they are neutral atoms.
  • Confusing an orbital with an energy level.
  • Putting more than two electrons in one orbital.
  • Ignoring Hund's rule when filling p or d orbitals.
  • Removing 3d electrons before 4s electrons when forming common transition-metal ions.
  • Giving only the trend in ionisation energy without explaining it.
  • Forgetting shielding when explaining group trends.
  • Saying ionisation energy decreases across every period without considering the known anomalies.
  • Confusing electron affinity with electronegativity.
  • Giving a trend without linking it to nuclear charge, shielding, distance or electron-electron repulsion.

26. 🧠 Important Definitions

  • Atomic number: the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Mass number: the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Isotope: an atom of an element having the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons.
  • Orbital: a region of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
  • First ionisation energy: the enthalpy change required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Successive ionisation energies: the energies required to remove successive electrons from one mole of gaseous ions.
  • Electron affinity: the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom to form a gaseous negative ion.
  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom in a molecule to attract the bonding pair of electrons towards itself.
  • Periodicity: the repetition of similar properties at regular intervals when elements are arranged according to increasing atomic number.
  • Shielding effect: the reduction in attraction between the nucleus and an outer electron caused by electrons in inner shells.
  • Isoelectronic species: species containing the same number of electrons and therefore the same electron configuration.

27. 🔄 Quick Revision

⚛️ Atomic Structure

  • Protons determine atomic number.
  • Protons + neutrons determine mass number.
  • Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers.
  • Electrons occupy orbitals.
  • An orbital holds a maximum of two electrons.
  • s, p and d subshells contain 1, 3 and 5 orbitals respectively.

📊 Periodicity

  • Atomic radius generally decreases across a period.
  • Atomic radius generally increases down a group.
  • First ionisation energy generally increases across a period.
  • First ionisation energy generally decreases down a group.
  • Electronegativity generally increases across a period.
  • Electronegativity generally decreases down a group.
  • Shielding increases down a group.
  • Successive ionisation-energy jumps reveal shell structure.

⚠️ Remember the Ionisation-Energy Anomalies

  • Be → B
  • N → O
  • Mg → Al
  • P → S

🎓 Final GCE Takeaway

The central idea of this topic is that electronic structure controls periodic behaviour. When explaining trends, do not simply state that a property increases or decreases. Link the trend to nuclear charge, electron shielding, distance from the nucleus, subshell energy and electron-electron repulsion.

This reasoning is essential for high-level A/L Chemistry questions involving electron configurations, ionisation energies, atomic size and periodic properties.
