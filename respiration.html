💨 Respiration

Learn how living organisms release energy from food, aerobic and anaerobic respiration, human gas exchange, breathing, exercise and respiration experiments.

💨 1. What is Respiration?

Respiration is the chemical process by which energy is released from food substances in living cells.

The energy released during respiration is used by cells for many life processes, including growth, movement, active transport, cell division and the maintenance of body temperature.

⭐ Key Definition

Respiration is the release of energy from food substances in living cells.

⚠️ Do Not Confuse Respiration with Breathing

Breathing is the physical movement of air into and out of the lungs.

Respiration is a chemical process in cells that releases energy from food.

⚡ 2. Why is Respiration Important?

All living cells need energy to carry out their activities. Respiration provides this energy.

Use of Energy Importance
Movement Provides energy for muscle contraction and movement.
Active transport Provides energy for movement of substances against a concentration gradient.
Growth Provides energy needed for growth and synthesis of new materials.
Cell division Provides energy needed during cell division.
Protein synthesis Provides energy for making proteins.
Maintaining body temperature Energy released during respiration contributes to maintaining body temperature.

🔄 3. Types of Respiration

There are two major forms of respiration studied at O/L:

  • Aerobic respiration
  • Anaerobic respiration
Aerobic Respiration Anaerobic Respiration
Uses oxygen. Does not use oxygen.
Releases a relatively large amount of energy. Releases a relatively small amount of energy.
Glucose is completely broken down. Glucose is only partially broken down.
Carbon dioxide and water are produced. Different products are formed depending on the organism.

🫁 4. Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration is the release of a relatively large amount of energy from the breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen.

📌 Definition to Remember

Aerobic respiration is the breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen to release a relatively large amount of energy.

🧪 5. Aerobic Respiration Equation

The word equation for aerobic respiration is:

Word Equation

Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy

The balanced chemical equation is:

Chemical Equation

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy

⭐ Exam Tip

Do not write oxygen as a product of aerobic respiration. Oxygen is a reactant.

🍬 6. Glucose as a Respiratory Substrate

Glucose is an important respiratory substrate.

A respiratory substrate is a substance that can be broken down during respiration to release energy.

Glucose is obtained from food. In plants, glucose is produced during photosynthesis.

🔬 7. Respiration Occurs in Living Cells

Respiration occurs in the cells of living organisms.

This includes:

  • Plants
  • Animals
  • Fungi
  • Many microorganisms

Respiration is therefore not simply a process carried out by the lungs. The lungs are involved in supplying oxygen and removing carbon dioxide in humans, but the energy-releasing chemical reactions occur in cells.

🌿 8. Respiration in Plants

Plants respire continuously, during both day and night.

During the day, a green plant may carry out both photosynthesis and respiration.

At night, photosynthesis stops because there is no light, but respiration continues.

⭐ Important

Plants do not only respire at night. Respiration occurs continuously.

🔄 9. Photosynthesis and Respiration

Photosynthesis Respiration
Makes glucose. Breaks down glucose to release energy.
Requires light. Does not require light.
Uses carbon dioxide. Aerobic respiration produces carbon dioxide.
Produces oxygen. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen.
Stores energy in food. Releases energy from food.

⚡ 10. Energy Released During Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration releases a relatively large amount of energy because glucose is broken down extensively.

The released energy is made available to cells for carrying out life processes.

Some of the energy may also be released as heat.

🧪 11. Anaerobic Respiration

Anaerobic respiration is the release of a relatively small amount of energy from the breakdown of glucose without using oxygen.

📌 Definition to Remember

Anaerobic respiration is the breakdown of glucose without using oxygen, releasing a relatively small amount of energy.

🏃 12. Anaerobic Respiration in Human Muscles

During vigorous exercise, muscles may require energy faster than oxygen can be supplied to them.

Muscle cells can therefore respire anaerobically for a short period.

Equation in Human Muscles

Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy

Anaerobic respiration allows energy to be released without using oxygen, but it releases much less energy than aerobic respiration.

🧪 13. Lactic Acid During Vigorous Exercise

When muscles respire anaerobically, lactic acid is produced.

During vigorous exercise, lactic acid can accumulate in the muscles and blood.

This is associated with muscle fatigue and discomfort.

⭐ Key Sequence

Vigorous exercise → oxygen supply becomes insufficient → anaerobic respiration increases → lactic acid accumulates.

🫁 14. Oxygen Debt

After vigorous exercise, breathing and heart rate remain elevated for some time.

This helps provide the oxygen needed to deal with the lactic acid produced during anaerobic respiration.

The additional oxygen required after exercise is commonly described at O/L as the oxygen debt.

Oxygen Debt — Simple Explanation

During vigorous exercise, anaerobic respiration may occur and lactic acid accumulates. After exercise, extra oxygen is required to help remove the effects of this anaerobic respiration.

❤️ 15. Removal of Oxygen Debt

After exercise, the body continues to breathe faster and more deeply.

The heart also continues beating rapidly.

Body Response Importance
Faster breathing Increases oxygen intake.
Deeper breathing Increases the amount of air reaching the lungs.
Faster heartbeat Transports blood and substances more rapidly.
Increased oxygen supply Helps the body deal with lactic acid produced during anaerobic respiration.

🍞 16. Anaerobic Respiration in Yeast

Yeast can respire anaerobically when oxygen is unavailable or very limited.

This process is commonly called fermentation.

Equation in Yeast

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy

The carbon dioxide produced by yeast is useful in bread making because it causes dough to rise.

Fermentation is also important in the production of some foods and beverages.

📊 17. Aerobic vs Anaerobic Respiration

Feature Aerobic Anaerobic
Oxygen Required. Not used.
Energy released Relatively large amount. Relatively small amount.
Glucose breakdown More complete. Incomplete.
Human muscles Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy. Glucose → lactic acid + energy.
Yeast Can carry out aerobic respiration when oxygen is available. Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy.

🫁 18. Human Respiratory System

The human respiratory system allows oxygen from the air to enter the body and carbon dioxide to be removed.

🖼️ Respiratory System Diagram

A labelled diagram of the human respiratory system should show the major structures involved in breathing and gas exchange.

Diagram labels to know:

  • Nose / nasal cavity
  • Mouth
  • Pharynx
  • Larynx
  • Trachea
  • Bronchi
  • Bronchioles
  • Alveoli
  • Lungs
  • Diaphragm
  • Ribs
  • Intercostal muscles

🌬️ 19. Pathway of Air into the Lungs

During inhalation, air follows a pathway through the respiratory system.

Air Pathway

Nose / Mouth → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli

Gas exchange occurs mainly at the alveoli.

👃 20. Nose and Nasal Cavity

The nose provides an entrance for air and helps prepare air before it reaches the lungs.

The nasal passages contain mucus and cilia that help trap particles and microorganisms.

Air is also warmed and moistened as it passes through the nasal passages.

🗣️ 21. Larynx

The larynx is part of the airway between the pharynx and trachea.

It contains the vocal cords and is involved in producing sound.

🫁 22. Trachea

The trachea, or windpipe, carries air between the larynx and the bronchi.

The walls of the trachea are supported by rings of cartilage. These help prevent the airway from collapsing.

The lining contains mucus and cilia.

Function of Cilia

Cilia beat to move mucus containing trapped particles towards the throat, where it can be removed.

🌿 23. Bronchi

The trachea divides into two main bronchi.

One bronchus carries air towards each lung.

🌿 24. Bronchioles

The bronchi divide into smaller tubes called bronchioles.

Bronchioles distribute air throughout the lungs and lead to regions containing alveoli.

🫧 25. Alveoli

Alveoli are tiny air sacs in the lungs where gaseous exchange takes place.

⭐ Main Function

Alveoli provide a large surface where oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveolar air.

🔬 26. Adaptations of Alveoli for Gas Exchange

Alveoli are highly adapted to allow rapid gaseous exchange.

Adaptation Advantage
Very large number of alveoli Provides a large surface area for gas exchange.
Thin walls Provides a short diffusion distance.
Moist lining Allows gases to dissolve before diffusing.
Rich blood supply Maintains concentration gradients for oxygen and carbon dioxide.
Good ventilation Maintains concentration differences between alveolar air and blood.

🔄 27. Gaseous Exchange at the Alveoli

Gaseous exchange occurs mainly by diffusion.

Oxygen concentration is higher in the air inside the alveoli than in the deoxygenated blood arriving at the lungs.

Oxygen therefore diffuses from the alveoli into the blood.

Carbon dioxide concentration is higher in the blood arriving at the lungs than in the alveolar air.

Carbon dioxide therefore diffuses from the blood into the alveoli and is breathed out.

⭐ Remember the Directions

Oxygen: Alveoli → Blood

Carbon dioxide: Blood → Alveoli

🔬 28. Diffusion and Gas Exchange

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Diffusion does not require energy from respiration.

The concentration gradient across the alveolar wall helps oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse rapidly.

🩸 29. Transport of Oxygen in the Blood

Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into nearby blood capillaries.

Most oxygen is transported by haemoglobin in red blood cells.

Oxygen combines reversibly with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.

Oxygen Transport

Alveoli → Blood → Haemoglobin → Body tissues

🧬 30. How Oxygen Reaches Respiring Cells

Oxygen follows a pathway from the atmosphere to cells where respiration occurs.

⭐ Step-by-Step Pathway

  1. Oxygen is present in atmospheric air.
  2. Air is inhaled into the lungs.
  3. Oxygen reaches the alveoli.
  4. Oxygen diffuses across the alveolar wall into the blood.
  5. Oxygen combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells.
  6. Blood transports oxygen around the body.
  7. Oxygen leaves the blood and diffuses into body cells.
  8. Cells use oxygen for aerobic respiration.

🌬️ 31. Removal of Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is produced as a waste product of aerobic respiration.

It must be transported from respiring cells to the lungs and removed from the body.

Carbon Dioxide Pathway

Respiring cells → Blood → Lungs → Alveoli → Outside air

⬇️ 32. Inspiration

Inspiration, or inhalation, is the process of taking air into the lungs.

During inspiration:

  1. The external intercostal muscles contract.
  2. The ribs move upwards and outwards.
  3. The diaphragm contracts and becomes flatter.
  4. The volume of the thoracic cavity increases.
  5. Pressure inside the lungs decreases.
  6. Air moves into the lungs.

⭐ Inspiration Sequence

Muscles contract → Volume increases → Pressure decreases → Air enters

⬆️ 33. Expiration

Expiration, or exhalation, is the process of removing air from the lungs.

During normal expiration:

  1. The external intercostal muscles relax.
  2. The ribs move downwards and inwards.
  3. The diaphragm relaxes and becomes more dome-shaped.
  4. The volume of the thoracic cavity decreases.
  5. Pressure inside the lungs increases.
  6. Air moves out of the lungs.

⭐ Expiration Sequence

Muscles relax → Volume decreases → Pressure increases → Air leaves

🫁 34. Diaphragm

The diaphragm is a sheet of muscle separating the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity.

It plays an important role in ventilation.

Inspiration Expiration
Diaphragm contracts. Diaphragm relaxes.
Becomes flatter. Becomes dome-shaped.
Thoracic volume increases. Thoracic volume decreases.

🦴 35. Ribs and Intercostal Muscles

The ribs form part of the chest wall and help protect the lungs.

Intercostal muscles are muscles between the ribs.

Their contraction and relaxation help move the ribs during breathing.

📊 36. Inspiration vs Expiration

Feature Inspiration Expiration
Diaphragm Contracts and flattens. Relaxes and becomes dome-shaped.
Ribs Move up and out. Move down and in.
Thoracic volume Increases. Decreases.
Lung pressure Decreases. Increases.
Air movement Into lungs. Out of lungs.

🔄 37. Breathing is Not Respiration

These two terms are frequently confused in examinations.

Breathing Respiration
Physical process. Chemical process.
Moves air into and out of lungs. Releases energy from food.
Involves respiratory organs. Occurs in living cells.
Does not itself release energy from glucose. Releases energy from respiratory substrates.

🌬️ 38. Inspired Air and Expired Air

Inspired air is the air breathed into the lungs. Expired air is the air breathed out.

During gas exchange, oxygen is removed from the air and carbon dioxide is added to it.

Feature Inspired Air Expired Air
Oxygen Higher. Lower.
Carbon dioxide Lower. Higher.
Water vapour Less. More.
Temperature Usually cooler than expired air. Usually warmer.

🧪 39. Testing for Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide can be tested using limewater.

Limewater Test

When carbon dioxide is bubbled through limewater, the limewater turns milky.

Since expired air contains more carbon dioxide than inspired air, expired air turns limewater milky more quickly.

🔬 40. Comparing Inspired and Expired Air Experiment

Basic Experiment

  1. Prepare two containers of clear limewater.
  2. Pass inspired air through one sample.
  3. Pass expired air through the other sample.
  4. Observe the changes in the limewater.

Expected Result

The sample receiving expired air becomes milky more quickly because expired air contains more carbon dioxide.

Conclusion

Respiring cells produce carbon dioxide, which is removed from the body through the lungs.

🧪 41. Demonstrating that Respiration Produces Carbon Dioxide

Respiration can be demonstrated by showing that a respiring organism produces carbon dioxide.

Example Experimental Design

  1. Place a respiring organism, such as germinating seeds, in a suitable container.
  2. Arrange the apparatus so that gases from the container can pass through limewater.
  3. Keep suitable conditions for respiration.
  4. Observe the limewater.

Result

The limewater turns milky.

Conclusion

The respiring seeds produced carbon dioxide.

🌡️ 42. Demonstrating that Respiration Releases Heat

Respiration releases energy, and some of this energy appears as heat.

Germinating seeds can be used to demonstrate this.

Experiment

  1. Obtain two similar insulated containers.
  2. Place germinating seeds in one container.
  3. Place an appropriate control, such as boiled seeds, in the other container.
  4. Insert thermometers so that the temperature can be measured.
  5. Keep both containers under the same conditions.
  6. Record the temperatures at regular intervals.

Expected Result

The temperature of the container containing living germinating seeds increases more than the control.

Conclusion

Respiration in the germinating seeds releases energy, some of which is released as heat.

🌱 43. Why Use Germinating Seeds?

Germinating seeds are actively growing and therefore have a high rate of respiration.

They require energy for growth and other cellular activities.

Why Use Boiled Seeds as a Control?

Boiling kills the seeds, so they cannot carry out normal respiration. The control allows the effect of respiration to be distinguished from changes caused by the surrounding conditions.

🌡️ 44. Respiration and Temperature

Respiration releases energy and some of this energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat.

The amount of heat released can therefore provide evidence that respiration is occurring.

🏃 45. Respiration During Exercise

During physical activity, muscles require more energy.

The rate of respiration therefore increases.

The body responds by increasing breathing rate and heart rate so that more oxygen can reach the muscles and more carbon dioxide can be removed.

Change During Exercise Reason
Breathing rate increases To take in more oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
Breathing becomes deeper To increase the amount of air entering the lungs.
Heart rate increases To transport oxygen and other substances more rapidly.
More energy is released Muscles require more energy for contraction.

🫁 46. Why Does Breathing Remain Fast After Exercise?

After vigorous exercise, breathing does not immediately return to its resting rate.

Extra oxygen is required to help the body recover from the increased demand for energy and to deal with lactic acid produced during anaerobic respiration.

Exam Answer

Breathing remains rapid after vigorous exercise because extra oxygen is required to help remove the effects of anaerobic respiration and restore the body to its normal condition.

⚙️ 47. Factors Affecting the Rate of Respiration

The rate of respiration can be affected by environmental and biological factors.

Factor General Effect
Temperature Respiration involves enzyme-controlled reactions, so temperature affects the rate.
Oxygen availability Lack of oxygen can cause greater dependence on anaerobic respiration in organisms that can carry it out.
Availability of respiratory substrate Glucose and other substrates provide material for energy release.
Activity level Active tissues generally require more energy and therefore have a higher respiration rate.

🍞 48. Investigating Respiration in Yeast

Yeast can be used to investigate respiration and fermentation.

Basic Experimental Design

  1. Place yeast in a warm solution containing glucose.
  2. Maintain suitable conditions for yeast activity.
  3. Collect or observe the gas produced.
  4. Test the gas using limewater if appropriate.

Expected Result

Carbon dioxide is produced during fermentation.

Conclusion

Yeast can break down glucose anaerobically, producing ethanol, carbon dioxide and a small amount of energy.

🧫 49. Fermentation

Fermentation is the anaerobic breakdown of glucose by microorganisms such as yeast.

Yeast Fermentation

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy

Fermentation is useful in food production, including bread making.

🍞 50. Importance of Yeast in Bread Making

Yeast is added to dough because it can ferment sugars.

During fermentation, yeast produces carbon dioxide.

The carbon dioxide forms bubbles in the dough, causing it to expand and rise.

⭐ Remember

The gas responsible for making bread dough rise is carbon dioxide.

🔬 51. Characteristics of a Good Respiratory Surface

A good respiratory surface allows gases to diffuse rapidly between the environment and the organism.

Characteristic Why It Helps
Large surface area Allows more gas exchange to occur at the same time.
Thin Provides a short diffusion distance.
Moist Allows gases to dissolve before diffusing.
Good blood supply Maintains concentration gradients.
Ventilation Maintains a concentration gradient between the respiratory surface and the environment.

🫁 52. Adaptations of the Human Respiratory System

The human respiratory system is adapted to provide efficient gaseous exchange.

  • The lungs contain a very large number of alveoli.
  • Alveoli provide a large surface area.
  • Alveolar walls are very thin.
  • Alveoli have a moist surface.
  • Alveoli have a rich blood supply.
  • Breathing continuously replaces air in the alveoli.
  • The respiratory system has mechanisms such as mucus and cilia that help protect the lungs.

🚭 53. Effects of Tobacco Smoke on the Respiratory System

Tobacco smoke contains harmful substances that can damage the respiratory system.

Substance Effect
Tar Can damage lung tissues and interfere with normal ciliary action.
Carbon monoxide Reduces the blood's ability to transport oxygen because it binds strongly with haemoglobin.
Nicotine Causes dependence and can increase heart rate and blood pressure.

Smoking increases the risk of serious respiratory and cardiovascular diseases.

🦠 54. Respiratory Diseases

The respiratory system can be affected by infectious diseases, harmful substances and other conditions.

Condition General Description
Asthma A condition involving narrowing and inflammation of the airways, which can make breathing difficult.
Bronchitis Inflammation of the bronchi, often causing coughing and increased mucus.
Pneumonia Infection causing inflammation in lung tissue and potentially interfering with gas exchange.
Tuberculosis An infectious disease that commonly affects the lungs.

🌱 55. Evidence of Respiration in Germinating Seeds

Germinating seeds provide useful experimental evidence for respiration because they are actively growing.

They can be used to demonstrate:

  • Production of carbon dioxide
  • Release of heat
  • Use of oxygen

🫁 56. Demonstrating that Respiration Uses Oxygen

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen.

An experiment with actively respiring organisms can be used to demonstrate that oxygen is consumed.

Experimental Principle

If a respiring organism is placed in a sealed apparatus, oxygen is gradually removed from the air by respiration. A suitable indicator of gas-volume or pressure change can then be used to detect the change.

A control should be included where appropriate to ensure that the observed change is caused by respiration.

🔬 57. Variables in Respiration Experiments

Good biological experiments control variables so that the results are reliable.

Variable Meaning
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed by the investigator.
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed.
Control variables Factors kept the same to make the comparison fair.

📈 58. Measuring Respiration Rate

Respiration rate can be estimated by measuring changes associated with respiration.

Depending on the experiment, this may include measuring:

  • Oxygen uptake
  • Carbon dioxide production
  • Temperature increase caused by released heat
  • Other measurable changes associated with respiration

⚡ 59. Respiration and Energy Transfer

The chemical energy stored in glucose is released during respiration.

The released energy becomes available for cellular activities.

Some energy is also transferred to the surroundings as heat.

Simple Idea

Food contains stored chemical energy → Respiration releases energy → Cells use the released energy.

🧠 62. Complete Respiration Process

Food provides glucose .

Glucose enters living cells and can be broken down during respiration .

In aerobic respiration , oxygen is used and carbon dioxide and water are produced.

A relatively large amount of energy is released.

When oxygen is insufficient, some cells such as human muscle cells can carry out anaerobic respiration for a limited period.

In human muscles, this produces lactic acid .

In yeast, anaerobic respiration produces ethanol and carbon dioxide .

⚠️ 66. Common O/L Mistakes

❌ Mistake 1

Saying respiration means breathing.

Correct: Respiration is a chemical process that releases energy from food in cells.

❌ Mistake 2

Saying plants do not respire during the day.

Correct: Plants respire continuously, day and night.

❌ Mistake 3

Saying anaerobic respiration produces more energy than aerobic respiration.

Correct: Aerobic respiration releases a relatively large amount of energy, while anaerobic respiration releases a relatively small amount.

❌ Mistake 4

Saying carbon dioxide enters the blood at the alveoli.

Correct: Oxygen enters the blood from the alveoli, while carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli.

❌ Mistake 5

Saying oxygen is produced during aerobic respiration.

Correct: Oxygen is used during aerobic respiration.

❌ Mistake 6

Saying lactic acid is produced during aerobic respiration in muscles.

Correct: Lactic acid is produced during anaerobic respiration in human muscle cells.

❌ Mistake 7

Saying the diaphragm moves air directly into the lungs.

Correct: Movement of the diaphragm changes the volume and pressure of the thoracic cavity, causing air to move.

❌ Mistake 8

Saying limewater tests for oxygen.

Correct: Limewater is used to test for carbon dioxide.
