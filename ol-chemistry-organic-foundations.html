🧪 Organic Chemistry Foundations

O/L Chemistry — 0515

Learn the foundations of organic chemistry, hydrocarbons, homologous series, functional groups, naming, reactions and important organic compounds.

1. 🌿 Introduction to Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals mainly with carbon compounds, especially compounds containing carbon and hydrogen.

Carbon forms a very large number of compounds because its atoms can form strong covalent bonds with one another and with atoms of other elements.

Why is carbon special?

  • Carbon has four valence electrons.
  • Carbon can form four covalent bonds.
  • Carbon atoms can bond to other carbon atoms.
  • Carbon can form straight chains, branched chains and rings.
  • Carbon can form single, double and triple bonds.
  • Carbon forms compounds with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and halogens.

Carbon–carbon bonding

The ability of carbon atoms to join together to form chains and structures is called catenation.

2. 🔗 Bonding in Organic Compounds

Most simple organic compounds contain covalent bonds. These bonds are formed when atoms share electrons.

Single covalent bonds

A single covalent bond involves one shared pair of electrons.

Example: ethane, C₂H₆

Double covalent bonds

A double bond involves two shared pairs of electrons.

Example: ethene, C₂H₄

Triple covalent bonds

A triple bond involves three shared pairs of electrons.

Triple bonds are important in some unsaturated hydrocarbons.

3. ⛽ Hydrocarbons

A hydrocarbon is an organic compound containing only carbon and hydrogen.

Type Main feature Example
Alkanes Only carbon–carbon single bonds Methane, CH₄
Alkenes Contain at least one C=C double bond Ethene, C₂H₄
Alkynes Contain at least one C≡C triple bond Ethyne, C₂H₂

Saturated hydrocarbons

A saturated hydrocarbon contains only carbon–carbon single bonds.

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons.

Unsaturated hydrocarbons

An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon multiple bond.

Alkenes contain C=C bonds, while alkynes contain C≡C bonds.

4. 🧬 Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds having the same functional group and general formula, with successive members differing by CH₂.

Key characteristics of a homologous series

  • Members have the same functional group.
  • Members have the same general formula.
  • Successive members differ by CH₂.
  • Members have similar chemical properties.
  • Physical properties show a gradual change as molecular size increases.
  • Members can be represented by related structural formulae.

Alkane homologous series

Alkanes have the general formula:

CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Member Formula
Methane CH₄
Ethane C₂H₆
Propane C₃H₈
Butane C₄H₁₀
Pentane C₅H₁₂
Hexane C₆H₁₄

Alkene homologous series

The simple acyclic alkenes containing one C=C bond have the general formula:

CₙH₂ₙ

Member Formula
Ethene C₂H₄
Propene C₃H₆
Butene C₄H₈
Pentene C₅H₁₀

Relationship between successive homologues

Each successive member of a homologous series differs from the previous member by CH₂.

For example:

CH₄ → C₂H₆ → C₃H₈ → C₄H₁₀

Each step adds CH₂.

Exam Focus 🎯

If asked to identify a homologous series, look for the same functional group, same general formula and CH₂ difference between successive members.

5. 🧩 Functional Groups

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of an organic compound.

Family Functional group Example
Alkanes C–C single bonds Ethane
Alkenes C=C Ethene
Alcohols –OH Ethanol
Carboxylic acids –COOH Ethanoic acid

Hydroxyl group

The –OH group is the hydroxyl group found in alcohols.

Carboxyl group

The –COOH group is the carboxyl group found in carboxylic acids.

Carbon–carbon double bond

The C=C bond is the characteristic functional feature of alkenes.

7. ⛽ Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only carbon–carbon single bonds.

Their general formula is:

CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Physical properties of alkanes

  • They are generally non-polar.
  • They are insoluble in water.
  • They are soluble in many organic solvents.
  • Boiling points generally increase as molecular size increases.
  • Small members are gases at room temperature.
  • Intermediate members are liquids.
  • Larger members are solids.

Combustion of alkanes

Alkanes burn in oxygen and release energy.

Word equation:

Methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Incomplete combustion

When oxygen is insufficient, incomplete combustion may occur and carbon monoxide and/or carbon may form.

Word equation:

Methane + oxygen → carbon monoxide + water

Symbol equation:

2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O

⚠️ Important

Carbon monoxide is poisonous because it reduces the blood's ability to transport oxygen effectively.

8. 🔵 Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond.

The simple acyclic alkene homologous series has the general formula:

CₙH₂ₙ

Examples

  • Ethene — C₂H₄
  • Propene — C₃H₆
  • Butene — C₄H₈
  • Pentene — C₅H₁₀

Test for an alkene

Alkenes decolourise bromine water because the bromine reacts with the carbon–carbon double bond.

Word equation:

Ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane

Symbol equation:

C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂

🧪 Practical Observation

Bromine water changes from orange/brown to colourless when it reacts with an alkene under the usual test conditions.

Addition reactions

Alkenes commonly undergo addition reactions because the double bond can react with other molecules.

Word equation:

Ethene + hydrogen → ethane

Symbol equation:

C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆

Word equation:

Ethene + steam → ethanol

Symbol equation:

C₂H₄ + H₂O ⇌ C₂H₅OH

9. 🍶 Alcohols

Alcohols are organic compounds containing the hydroxyl functional group, –OH.

Examples

Alcohol Formula
Methanol CH₃OH
Ethanol C₂H₅OH
Propanol C₃H₇OH
Butanol C₄H₉OH

Properties of ethanol

  • Ethanol is a colourless liquid.
  • It is miscible with water.
  • It is volatile and flammable.
  • It is used as a solvent.
  • It can be used as a fuel.

Combustion of ethanol

Word equation:

Ethanol + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Oxidation of ethanol

Ethanol can be oxidised to ethanoic acid.

Word equation:

Ethanol + oxygen → ethanoic acid + water

Symbol equation:

C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O

This represents the overall oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid.

10. 🍋 Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids are organic compounds containing the –COOH functional group.

Examples

Acid Formula
Methanoic acid HCOOH
Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH
Propanoic acid CH₃CH₂COOH
Butanoic acid CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH

Properties of ethanoic acid

  • It is a weak acid in aqueous solution.
  • It has a characteristic vinegar-like smell.
  • It reacts with reactive metals.
  • It reacts with bases.
  • It reacts with carbonates.

Reaction with a metal

Word equation:

Ethanoic acid + sodium → sodium ethanoate + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

2CH₃COOH + 2Na → 2CH₃COONa + H₂

Reaction with an alkali

Word equation:

Ethanoic acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium ethanoate + water

Symbol equation:

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O

Reaction with a carbonate

Word equation:

Ethanoic acid + sodium carbonate → sodium ethanoate + carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O

11. 🧵 Polymers and Polymerisation

A polymer is a very large molecule formed by joining many smaller molecules called monomers.

Polymerisation is the process by which monomers join together to form polymers.

Addition polymerisation

Alkenes can undergo addition polymerisation because their carbon–carbon double bonds can open and link many monomer molecules together.

For example, ethene forms polyethene.

Word equation:

Ethene → polyethene

Symbol equation:

nCH₂=CH₂ → (–CH₂–CH₂–)ₙ

Uses of polymers

  • Packaging
  • Plastic containers
  • Electrical insulation
  • Pipes
  • Household products
  • Textiles and synthetic fibres

Environmental concerns

Many synthetic polymers are resistant to natural decomposition. Poor disposal can therefore contribute to environmental pollution.

12. 🔥 Cracking of Hydrocarbons

Cracking is the decomposition of large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more useful molecules.

Cracking can produce smaller alkanes and alkenes.

Why cracking is useful

  • It produces smaller hydrocarbons that are in greater demand.
  • It produces alkenes used to manufacture polymers.
  • It produces useful fuels.

Example word equation:

Decane → octane + ethene

Example symbol equation:

C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄

The exact products depend on the hydrocarbon and cracking conditions.

13. 🔀 Isomerism

Isomers are compounds that have the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements of their atoms.

Example: butane

Butane has the molecular formula C₄H₁₀.

It can exist as different structural arrangements.

14. 🧪 Practical Organic Chemistry

Testing for unsaturation

Bromine water can be used to test for a carbon–carbon double bond.

An alkene decolourises bromine water.

Testing for carbon dioxide from combustion

Carbon dioxide produced during combustion can be passed through limewater.

Limewater turns milky in the presence of carbon dioxide.

Word equation:

Calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water

Symbol equation:

Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

Testing for water

Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate changes from white to blue when water is present.

Word equation:

Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate + water → hydrated copper(II) sulfate

Symbol equation:

CuSO₄ + 5H₂O → CuSO₄·5H₂O

🧠 Important Definitions

  • Organic chemistry: The branch of chemistry dealing mainly with carbon compounds.
  • Hydrocarbon: A compound containing only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Catenation: The ability of carbon atoms to bond to one another to form chains, branches and rings.
  • Saturated hydrocarbon: A hydrocarbon containing only carbon–carbon single bonds.
  • Unsaturated hydrocarbon: A hydrocarbon containing at least one carbon–carbon multiple bond.
  • Homologous series: A family of organic compounds with the same functional group and general formula, in which successive members differ by CH₂.
  • Functional group: An atom or group of atoms responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of an organic compound.
  • Alkane: A saturated hydrocarbon containing only carbon–carbon single bonds.
  • Alkene: An unsaturated hydrocarbon containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond.
  • Alcohol: An organic compound containing the hydroxyl group, –OH.
  • Carboxylic acid: An organic compound containing the carboxyl group, –COOH.
  • Polymer: A very large molecule formed from many repeating monomer units.
  • Monomer: A small molecule that can join with other molecules to form a polymer.
  • Polymerisation: The process in which many monomer molecules join together to form a polymer.
  • Cracking: The decomposition of large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more useful molecules.
  • Isomers: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements.
  • Combustion: A reaction in which a substance burns in oxygen and releases energy.
  • Addition reaction: A reaction in which atoms or groups are added across a multiple bond to form one main product.

19. ⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing alkanes and alkenes: Alkanes contain only single C–C bonds, while alkenes contain a C=C bond.
  • Forgetting the CH₂ pattern: Successive members of a homologous series differ by CH₂.
  • Calling every carbon compound a hydrocarbon: A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Confusing molecular and structural formulae: A molecular formula gives the number of each type of atom, while a structural formula shows how atoms are arranged.
  • Forgetting to balance equations: The number of atoms of every element must be conserved.
  • Confusing bromine water with limewater: Bromine water is useful for testing unsaturation, while limewater is used to test for carbon dioxide.
  • Assuming all combustion is complete: Insufficient oxygen can cause incomplete combustion.
  • Confusing monomers and polymers: A monomer is a small starting molecule; a polymer contains many repeating units.
