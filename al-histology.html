🔬 Histology & Microscopy

A/L Biology 0710 — the study of biological tissues, microscopic structure, specimen preparation, tissue adaptations and identification from microscope images.

🔬 1. Meaning of Histology

📖 Definition

Histology is the branch of Biology concerned with the microscopic study of the structure and organisation of tissues.

A tissue is a group of similar cells, together with their extracellular materials where present, that are organised to perform one or more particular functions.

🧠 Histology and related terms

Term Meaning
🔬 Histology Study of tissues using microscopic techniques.
🧫 Cytology Study of cells.
🫀 Anatomy Study of the structure of organisms and their parts.
⚙️ Physiology Study of how living structures and systems function.
🔬 Microscopy Use of microscopes to observe objects too small to be seen clearly with the naked eye.

🧬 2. Levels of Organisation in Tissues

Biological organisation becomes more complex as cells become specialised and work together.

🧫 Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism

Level Example
Cell Red blood cell
Tissue Blood tissue
Organ Heart
Organ system Circulatory system
Organism Human

⭐ Key idea

Histology is mainly concerned with the level between cells and organs, although tissues must be understood in relation to the organs and systems they form.

🧩 3. Cell Specialisation and Tissue Formation

Multicellular organisms contain many different cell types. These cells become specialised for particular functions.

🌱 Examples of specialised cells

Cell Specialised function
🌿 Root hair cell Absorbs water and mineral ions from soil.
🌱 Palisade cell Efficient photosynthesis.
🩸 Red blood cell Transport of oxygen.
🧠 Neurone Transmission of electrical impulses.
💪 Muscle cell Contraction and movement.
🛡️ Epithelial cell Protection, absorption, secretion or exchange depending on location.

Cells with similar structures and functions may become organised into tissues. Different tissues then combine to form organs.

🧪 4. Preparation of Biological Specimens

Biological tissues are often too thick, opaque or delicate to observe directly under a light microscope. A specimen therefore has to be prepared carefully.

🔬 General sequence

Collection → Fixation → Dehydration → Embedding → Sectioning → Staining → Mounting → Microscopic examination

The exact method varies according to the specimen and the type of microscopy being used.

🧴 5. Fixation

📌 Meaning

Fixation is the treatment of a specimen to preserve its structure and prevent decomposition.

🎯 Functions of fixation

  • Preserves cellular structures.
  • Prevents decay and bacterial decomposition.
  • Stops many chemical changes occurring after death.
  • Helps maintain the tissue in a condition suitable for preparation.

Common fixatives used in histological work include formaldehyde-based fixatives and other specialised laboratory fixatives.

💧 6. Dehydration and Clearing

💧 Dehydration

Water is removed from the tissue, commonly by passing it through increasing concentrations of alcohol.

🧴 Clearing

The dehydrating agent is replaced with a substance that is compatible with the embedding medium.

⭐ Why is this necessary?

Many embedding materials do not mix with water. Therefore, water must be removed before embedding.

🧱 7. Embedding

After preparation, the specimen may be embedded in a firm medium, such as paraffin wax, to provide support.

🎯 Purpose of embedding

  • Supports delicate tissues.
  • Allows very thin sections to be produced.
  • Maintains the specimen's shape during sectioning.

🔪 8. Sectioning

📖 Definition

Sectioning is the cutting of a specimen into very thin slices so that light can pass through the specimen during microscopic examination.

🔬 Microtome

A microtome is an instrument used to cut very thin sections of embedded tissue.

⭐ Why thin sections are important

  • Allow light to pass through the specimen.
  • Reduce overlapping of structures.
  • Make individual cells easier to distinguish.
  • Improve the quality of the microscopic image.

🎨 9. Staining

📖 Definition

Staining is the use of dyes or stains to increase contrast between different structures in a biological specimen.

🎯 Why are tissues stained?

  • Many biological tissues are almost transparent.
  • Stains increase contrast.
  • Different cell structures may take up stains differently.
  • Important structures become easier to identify.

🔬 Examples of stains

Stain Common use / feature highlighted
Iodine solution Useful in plant material and some practical investigations; also used in starch tests.
Methylene blue Provides contrast in cells and can make nuclei easier to see.
Safranin Commonly used in plant histology, especially for lignified structures.
Haematoxylin and eosin Widely used in animal histology to distinguish different tissue components.

🧫 10. Mounting

After staining, the specimen is mounted on a microscope slide.

🔬 Temporary mount

A temporary mount is prepared for relatively quick observation. The specimen is placed in a suitable liquid and covered with a coverslip.

🔬 Permanent mount

A permanent preparation is processed and mounted in a medium that allows the specimen to be stored for a long period.

Temporary preparation Permanent preparation
Usually quick to prepare. Requires more extensive processing.
Often used for immediate observation. Can be stored for future study.
May use water or another temporary mounting liquid. Uses a suitable permanent mounting medium.

🔬 11. Microscopic Examination

Once the specimen has been prepared, it can be examined using a suitable microscope.

🔎 Light microscope

A light microscope uses visible light and lenses to magnify a specimen.

⚡ Electron microscope

An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons rather than visible light and can reveal much smaller structures and greater detail.

Feature Light microscope Electron microscope
Illumination Visible light Electrons
Lenses Glass lenses Electromagnetic lenses
Resolution Lower Much higher
Specimen Can be living or preserved Usually requires specialised preparation and vacuum conditions
Image May be viewed directly through the eyepiece Recorded electronically or on a screen

📏 12. Magnification and Resolution

📐 Magnification formula

Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

Therefore:

Image size = Magnification × Actual size

Actual size = Image size ÷ Magnification

🔍 Resolution

Resolution is the ability to distinguish two very close points as separate points.

⚠️ Magnification ≠ Resolution

Increasing magnification does not necessarily increase the amount of useful detail visible. A highly magnified image can still be blurred if resolution is poor.

🌱 13. Plant Tissues

Plant tissues can broadly be divided into meristematic tissues and permanent tissues.

Type Main feature
🌱 Meristematic tissue Contains actively dividing, relatively unspecialised cells.
🌿 Permanent tissue Cells have differentiated and generally perform specialised functions.

🌱 14. Meristematic Tissues

📖 Definition

Meristematic tissue consists of cells capable of repeated cell division and contributes to plant growth.

🔬 Characteristics of meristematic cells

  • Small cells.
  • Thin cell walls.
  • Dense cytoplasm.
  • Relatively large nucleus.
  • Little or no large permanent vacuole.
  • Closely packed cells with little intercellular space.
  • High rate of cell division.

📍 15. Types of Meristem

Meristem Location Main role
Apical meristem Tips of roots and shoots Increase in length; primary growth.
Lateral meristem Sides of stems and roots Increase in thickness; secondary growth.
Intercalary meristem Between mature tissues, especially in some grasses Allows growth of particular regions and regrowth after cutting or grazing.

🌿 16. Permanent Tissues

Permanent tissues develop when meristematic cells differentiate and become specialised.

Important plant tissues include:

  • Parenchyma
  • Collenchyma
  • Sclerenchyma
  • Xylem
  • Phloem
  • Epidermal tissue

🍃 17. Simple Permanent Tissues

Tissue Characteristics Functions
Parenchyma Living cells, generally thin walls and often prominent vacuoles. Storage, photosynthesis and packing.
Collenchyma Living cells with unevenly thickened walls. Flexible support in growing plant parts.
Sclerenchyma Usually dead at maturity with thick, lignified walls. Mechanical strength and support.

🚰 18. Xylem Tissue ⭐⭐⭐

📖 Definition

Xylem is a vascular tissue responsible mainly for transporting water and mineral ions from the roots towards the rest of the plant and for providing mechanical support.

🔬 Main xylem elements

  • Vessel elements
  • Tracheids
  • Xylem fibres
  • Xylem parenchyma

🚰 Vessel elements

  • Form long tubes for water transport.
  • Usually dead at maturity.
  • Have thick lignified walls.
  • End walls are perforated or absent between vessel elements.
  • Lignification provides strength and helps prevent collapse.

🔬 Xylem adaptation

The combination of a hollow conducting pathway and lignified walls makes xylem well suited for long-distance water transport and support.

🍬 19. Phloem Tissue ⭐⭐⭐

📖 Definition

Phloem is a vascular tissue responsible mainly for the translocation of organic solutes, especially sugars, between sources and sinks.

🔬 Main phloem elements

  • Sieve tube elements
  • Companion cells
  • Phloem fibres
  • Phloem parenchyma

🍬 Sieve tube elements

  • Living cells at maturity.
  • Have reduced internal contents.
  • End walls contain sieve plates.
  • Form continuous pathways for translocation.

🧬 Companion cells

Companion cells are closely associated with sieve tube elements and help maintain their functioning.

⚖️ 20. Xylem vs Phloem

Feature Xylem Phloem
Main transport Water and mineral ions Organic solutes, especially sugars
Major conducting cells Vessel elements and tracheids Sieve tube elements
Cell status Main conducting elements usually dead at maturity Conducting elements are living
Walls Strongly lignified in major conducting elements Not lignified in sieve tubes
Support Major role in mechanical support Less important as a support tissue

🛡️ 21. Epidermal Tissue

The epidermis forms a protective outer covering of many plant organs.

🌿 Functions

  • Protection.
  • Reduction of water loss when covered by a cuticle.
  • Control of gas exchange through stomata.
  • Absorption in roots.

🌱 Specialised epidermal structures

  • Guard cells
  • Stomata
  • Root hair cells
  • Trichomes/hairs in some plants

🧬 22. Tissue Systems in Plants

Plant tissues can also be considered as tissue systems.

Tissue system Major components Main role
Dermal tissue system Epidermis and associated structures Protection and exchange.
Ground tissue system Parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma Photosynthesis, storage and support.
Vascular tissue system Xylem and phloem Transport and support.

🫀 23. Animal Tissues

The four major categories of animal tissue are:

Tissue type Main function
🛡️ Epithelial tissue Covering, lining, protection, absorption and secretion.
🧱 Connective tissue Support, binding, transport, storage and protection.
💪 Muscle tissue Contraction and movement.
🧠 Nervous tissue Reception, processing and transmission of information.

🛡️ 24. Epithelial Tissue ⭐⭐⭐

📖 Definition

Epithelial tissue consists of cells arranged closely together to cover body surfaces, line internal surfaces and form many glands.

🔬 General characteristics

  • Cells are closely packed.
  • Very little extracellular space between cells.
  • Usually rests on a basement membrane.
  • Provides barriers between body compartments.
  • Some epithelial tissues are specialised for absorption or secretion.

🧱 25. Types of Epithelial Tissue

Type Structure Example/location Function
Simple squamous Single layer of thin flattened cells Alveoli, capillary walls Rapid diffusion and filtration.
Simple cuboidal Single layer of cube-shaped cells Kidney tubules and some glands Absorption and secretion.
Simple columnar Single layer of tall cells Intestine Absorption and secretion.
Ciliated epithelium Cells bearing cilia Respiratory tract; oviduct Moves material along the surface.
Stratified squamous Many layers; surface cells flattened Skin and other protective surfaces Protection against abrasion.

🫁 26. Epithelial Adaptations

🫁 Alveolar epithelium

  • Very thin.
  • Provides a short diffusion pathway.
  • Forms a large exchange surface.

🍞 Intestinal epithelium

  • Specialised for absorption.
  • Associated with villi and microvilli.
  • Large surface area.
  • Rich blood supply supports absorption and transport.

👂 Ciliated epithelium

  • Cilia beat in coordinated patterns.
  • Move mucus or other material along the epithelial surface.

🧱 27. Connective Tissue ⭐⭐⭐

📖 Definition

Connective tissue supports, connects, separates or protects other tissues and organs. It is characterised by cells embedded in an extracellular matrix.

🔬 Major examples

  • Areolar tissue
  • Adipose tissue
  • Tendon
  • Ligament
  • Cartilage
  • Bone
  • Blood

🧬 28. Components of Connective Tissue

Connective tissues commonly contain:

  • Cells
  • Protein fibres
  • Ground substance

🧵 Important fibres

Fibre Property
Collagen fibres Strong; resist tension.
Elastic fibres Stretch and recoil.
Reticular fibres Form supportive networks in certain tissues.

🦴 29. Bone and Cartilage

Feature Bone Cartilage
Matrix Hard and mineralised Firm but more flexible
Support Strong structural support Flexible support and cushioning
Blood supply Vascular Generally lacks direct blood vessels within the cartilage
Example Skeleton Tracheal rings and joint surfaces

🩸 30. Blood as a Connective Tissue

Blood is classified as connective tissue because its cells are suspended in an extracellular fluid matrix called plasma.

Component Main function
Red blood cells Transport oxygen.
White blood cells Defence against pathogens and abnormal cells.
Platelets Important in blood clotting.
Plasma Transports dissolved substances.

🧵 31. Tendons and Ligaments

Feature Tendon Ligament
Connects Muscle to bone Bone to bone
Main fibres Many collagen fibres Collagen and elastic fibres
Function Transmits force from muscle to bone. Stabilises joints while allowing controlled movement.

💪 32. Muscle Tissue ⭐⭐⭐

Muscle tissue is specialised for contraction.

Type Structure Control Typical location
Skeletal muscle Long, striated fibres; many nuclei Usually voluntary Attached to bones
Cardiac muscle Striated, branched cells with specialised junctions Involuntary Heart
Smooth muscle Spindle-shaped, non-striated cells Involuntary Walls of organs such as intestine and blood vessels

❤️ 33. Cardiac Muscle Adaptations

  • Cells are interconnected for coordinated contraction.
  • Specialised junctions help electrical signals spread between cells.
  • High numbers of mitochondria support continuous activity.
  • Blood supply provides oxygen and nutrients.
  • Specialised structure allows rhythmic contraction.

🧠 Main cells

  • Neurones — transmit electrical signals.
  • Glial cells — support, protect and maintain neurones.

🔬 Parts of a typical neurone

  • Cell body
  • Dendrites
  • Axon
  • Axon terminals

Structure Adaptation/function

🔬 36. Histological Drawings

In practical Biology, you may be asked to make a biological drawing from a prepared slide or micrograph.

✏️ Rules for biological drawings

  • Use a sharp pencil.
  • Draw large enough to show important structures.
  • Use clear, continuous lines.
  • Do not shade or colour the drawing.
  • Do not use artistic effects.
  • Label important structures with straight lines.
  • Keep labels outside the drawing where possible.
  • Do not allow label lines to cross unnecessarily.
  • Include a clear title.
  • State the magnification or scale where required.

⭐ Examiner's principle

A biological drawing should show the structure and arrangement of the specimen, not artistic beauty.

📐 37. Drawing to Scale

When a specimen or micrograph has a scale bar, it can be used to determine the size of structures and construct an appropriate drawing.

📏 Scale calculation

Magnification = Drawing size ÷ Actual size

⚠️ Common mistake

Do not confuse the length of a scale bar in the printed image with the actual length represented by the scale bar.

🔎 38. Identifying Tissues from Micrographs ⭐⭐⭐

A micrograph is an image produced using a microscope. Examination questions may provide an unknown tissue and ask you to identify it.

🧠 Identification strategy

  1. Look at the overall organisation of the tissue.
  2. Identify whether the cells are tightly or loosely packed.
  3. Observe cell shape and arrangement.
  4. Look for specialised structures.
  5. Look for thickened, lignified or mineralised walls.
  6. Identify visible nuclei or other structures.
  7. Compare the observed features with known tissue characteristics.
  8. Use several features before making your final identification.

🌱 39. Plant Tissue Identification

Tissue Features to look for
Meristem Small, closely packed cells, dense cytoplasm, prominent nuclei.
Xylem Large hollow vessels or tracheids, thick lignified walls.
Phloem Smaller living conducting cells associated with companion cells.
Parenchyma Living cells with relatively thin walls and larger spaces/vacuoles.
Sclerenchyma Thick, lignified walls and relatively small lumen.
Epidermis Usually a continuous outer layer of cells.

🫀 40. Animal Tissue Identification

Tissue Key identifying features
Simple squamous epithelium Very thin flattened cells forming a single layer.
Simple cuboidal epithelium Cube-shaped cells in a single layer.
Simple columnar epithelium Tall cells arranged in a layer.
Ciliated epithelium Cells with visible cilia on the free surface.
Skeletal muscle Long fibres with clear striations.
Cardiac muscle Striated, branched fibres with specialised junctions.
Smooth muscle Spindle-shaped cells without obvious striations.
Nervous tissue Large neurone cell bodies with processes and supporting cells.
Bone Hard matrix with organised microscopic spaces around bone cells.

⚖️ 41. Comparing Plant and Animal Tissues

Feature Plant tissues Animal tissues
Cell wall Present in plant cells. Absent in animal cells.
Major growth regions Meristems. Growth involves specialised tissues and cell proliferation depending on the organ.
Transport tissues Xylem and phloem. Blood and lymphatic systems perform major transport functions.
Mechanical support Cell walls, xylem and supporting tissues. Connective tissues such as bone, cartilage and connective fibres.

🧬 42. Tissue Adaptation

A tissue is adapted when its structure is suited to the function it performs.

Tissue Structural feature Functional advantage
Xylem Lignified walls and hollow conducting pathways Efficient water transport and support.
Phloem Sieve tubes associated with companion cells Efficient translocation of organic solutes.
Alveolar epithelium Very thin cells Rapid gas exchange.
Intestinal epithelium Large surface area and specialised absorptive cells Efficient nutrient absorption.
Skeletal muscle Long fibres containing contractile structures Produces force and movement.
Neurone Long axon and specialised processes Rapid communication over distance.

🔬 43. Histology and Microscopy Practical Skills

Histology is closely connected to practical Biology because the quality of microscopic observations depends on correct specimen preparation and microscope use.

🧪 Important practical skills

  • Preparing thin specimens.
  • Making temporary slides.
  • Using stains correctly.
  • Placing coverslips carefully.
  • Focusing the microscope.
  • Changing objective lenses appropriately.
  • Calculating magnification.
  • Estimating cell size.
  • Making accurate biological drawings.
  • Identifying tissues from micrographs.

🔎 44. Correct Use of a Light Microscope

  1. Place the microscope on a stable surface.
  2. Ensure the lowest-power objective is selected.
  3. Place the slide securely on the stage.
  4. Use the stage controls to position the specimen.
  5. Look from the side when necessary while bringing the objective close to the slide.
  6. Use the coarse adjustment at low power to obtain initial focus.
  7. Use fine adjustment for a sharp image.
  8. Move to higher power when required.
  9. At high power, mainly use fine adjustment.
  10. Adjust illumination and diaphragm as appropriate.

⚠️ Important

At high magnification, the field of view becomes smaller and the image may become dimmer. Careful focusing is therefore essential.

💡 45. Field of View

The field of view is the circular area visible through the microscope at a particular magnification.

Increasing magnification generally causes the field of view to become smaller.

🔍 Higher magnification → smaller field of view

📏 46. Estimating Cell Size

Cell size can be estimated from a known scale or from the microscope field of view.

📐 General approach

  1. Determine the size represented by a scale bar or calibrated field.
  2. Estimate how many cells fit across the measured distance.
  3. Divide the total distance by the number of cells.
  4. Give the answer with an appropriate unit.

📌 Common units

1 mm = 1000 μm

1 μm = 1000 nm

🧠 47. How to Answer Micrograph Questions

A/L examination questions may provide a micrograph and ask you to identify structures, compare tissues or explain how structure relates to function.

📝 Recommended method

  1. Observe: Describe what you can actually see.
  2. Identify: Name the tissue or structure.
  3. Give evidence: State the visible features supporting your answer.
  4. Explain: Connect the structure to its function.

⭐ Example

Question: Identify a tissue containing large hollow tubes with thick walls.

Answer: Xylem, because the large conducting elements have thick, lignified walls and a hollow lumen, allowing water transport while providing support.

⚖️ 48. Comparing Tissues in Exams

When asked to compare tissues, do not simply describe each tissue separately. Use corresponding characteristics.

📝 Good comparison categories

  • Cell shape
  • Cell arrangement
  • Cell wall thickness
  • Presence or absence of nuclei
  • Presence of specialised structures
  • Extracellular matrix
  • Location
  • Function
  • Adaptations

⚠️ 49. Common Histology Mistakes

  • ❌ Confusing histology with cytology.
  • ❌ Assuming all tissues contain identical cells.
  • ❌ Saying xylem transports only water and ignoring mineral ions.
  • ❌ Saying phloem always transports substances only upwards.
  • ❌ Confusing xylem vessels with phloem sieve tubes.
  • ❌ Calling every plant tissue a meristem.
  • ❌ Confusing epithelial tissue with connective tissue.
  • ❌ Saying all muscle tissue is voluntary.
  • ❌ Confusing magnification with resolution.
  • ❌ Drawing microscopic specimens with unnecessary shading.
  • ❌ Identifying a tissue from only one weak feature.
  • ❌ Forgetting units when calculating magnification or cell size.

📝 51. A/L Exam-Style Practice

Question 1

A student prepares a thin section of plant tissue for microscopic observation. Explain why the section must be thin.

Answer guide: A thin section allows light to pass through the specimen and reduces overlapping of structures, making individual cells and tissues easier to observe.

Question 2

Explain why xylem vessels are well adapted for water transport.

Answer guide: They form continuous hollow pathways and have thick lignified walls that provide support and resist collapse.

Question 3

Give two differences between xylem and phloem.

Answer guide: Xylem mainly transports water and mineral ions, whereas phloem translocates organic solutes. The main xylem conducting elements are generally dead at maturity, while sieve tube elements are living.

Question 4

A micrograph shows a single layer of very thin flattened cells. Suggest the tissue and explain why its structure is useful.

Answer guide: Simple squamous epithelium. Its thin layer provides a short diffusion pathway.

Question 5

Explain the difference between magnification and resolution.

Answer guide: Magnification is the enlargement of an image relative to the actual specimen, whereas resolution is the ability to distinguish two closely positioned points as separate.

🏁 54. Final A/L Takeaway

Histology is not simply about memorising tissue names. At A/L level, you should be able to move between:

🔬 microscopic appearance → 🧬 structure → ⚙️ function → 🌱/🫀 adaptation → 🧪 practical method → 📝 examination evidence

If you can look at an unfamiliar micrograph, describe what you see, identify the tissue using evidence, explain its function and relate its structure to that function, you have mastered the central skill of A/L Histology.
