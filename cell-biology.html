🧬 Cell Biology

Learn the basic structure and functions of cells.

🧬 1. Introduction to Cells

All living organisms are made up of cells. A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. This means that cells form the structures of living organisms and carry out the activities necessary for life.

Some organisms are made up of only one cell. These are called unicellular organisms. Other organisms are made up of many cells and are called multicellular organisms.

🧠 Key Definition

Cell: The basic structural and functional unit of a living organism.

🔬 Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms

🦠 Unicellular organisms

A unicellular organism consists of only one cell. That single cell performs all the activities necessary for the organism to survive.

Examples include bacteria and some microscopic organisms such as Amoeba.

👥 Multicellular organisms

A multicellular organism consists of many cells. Different cells may become specialized to perform particular functions.

Examples include humans, maize plants and many other plants and animals.

📊 Quick Comparison

  • Unicellular: made up of one cell.
  • Multicellular: made up of many cells.
  • In multicellular organisms, cells can become specialized for different functions.

🧩 Levels of Organization

In multicellular organisms, cells are organized into increasingly complex levels.

➡️ The organization pathway

Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism

  • Cell: The basic unit of life.
  • Tissue: A group of similar cells working together to perform a particular function.
  • Organ: A structure made from different tissues working together.
  • Organ system: A group of organs working together to carry out major functions.
  • Organism: A complete living individual.

📖 Cell Theory

Cell theory explains the importance of cells in living organisms. The main ideas are:

🧠 Remember These

  • All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
  • New cells arise from pre-existing cells.

🔬 2. Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Cells can be divided into two major groups based on their internal organization: prokaryotic cells and eukaryotic cells.

The most important difference is that eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, while prokaryotic cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus.

🦠 Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells are generally small and have a relatively simple internal structure. Bacteria are examples of organisms that contain prokaryotic cells.

🧠 Important Features

  • They do not have a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Their genetic material is found in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid.
  • They do not contain membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria or chloroplasts.
  • They contain ribosomes for protein synthesis.
  • Many bacteria have a cell wall.
  • Some bacteria have structures such as a capsule, flagellum or pili.

🧬 Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells are generally more complex. Their genetic material is contained inside a membrane-bound nucleus.

Animals, plants, fungi and many protists contain eukaryotic cells.

🧠 Important Features

  • They have a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • They contain membrane-bound organelles.
  • They contain ribosomes for protein synthesis.
  • Plant cells have structures such as chloroplasts and a cellulose cell wall.
  • Animal cells do not have a cellulose cell wall or chloroplasts.

📊 Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

🔍 Key Differences

  • Nucleus: Prokaryotic cells have no membrane-bound nucleus; eukaryotic cells do.
  • Size: Prokaryotic cells are generally smaller than eukaryotic cells.
  • Organelles: Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles, while eukaryotic cells contain them.
  • Genetic material: Prokaryotic DNA is located in the nucleoid region, while eukaryotic DNA is mainly contained within the nucleus.
  • Examples: Bacteria are prokaryotic, while plants and animals are eukaryotic.

🌱 3. Plant and Animal Cells

Study the labelled structures carefully. You should be able to identify important cell structures in an O/L Biology examination.

Plant and animal cells are both eukaryotic cells. Therefore, they share many structures, but plant cells also contain structures that allow them to carry out functions such as photosynthesis and provide support.

🌱 Plant Cells

A typical plant cell contains a cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, a cell wall, a large permanent vacuole and, in photosynthetic cells, chloroplasts.

🔬 Important Plant Cell Structures

  • Cell wall: A rigid layer outside the cell membrane that provides support and helps the cell maintain its shape.
  • Cell membrane: Controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell.
  • Cytoplasm: Contains the cell organelles and is the site of many chemical reactions.
  • Nucleus: Contains genetic material and controls the activities of the cell.
  • Mitochondria: Site of aerobic respiration and release of energy for cellular activities.
  • Ribosomes: Sites of protein synthesis.
  • Chloroplasts: Contain chlorophyll and are the main site of photosynthesis in plant cells.
  • Large permanent vacuole: Contains cell sap and helps maintain pressure inside the cell, helping the plant cell remain firm.

🐾 Animal Cells

Animal cells are also eukaryotic. They contain many of the same structures as plant cells, including the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes.

However, typical animal cells do not have a cellulose cell wall or chloroplasts, and they do not normally have one large permanent central vacuole like a typical plant cell.

📊 Plant Cell vs Animal Cell

Feature Plant Cell Animal Cell
Cell membrane Present Present
Cytoplasm Present Present
Nucleus Present Present
Mitochondria Present Present
Ribosomes Present Present
Cell wall Present Absent
Chloroplasts Present in photosynthetic plant cells Absent
Large permanent vacuole Usually present Absent in typical animal cells

🧠 Similarities

Both Plant and Animal Cells Have:

  • Cell membrane
  • Cytoplasm
  • Nucleus
  • Mitochondria
  • Ribosomes

🧬 4. Specialized Cells

In multicellular organisms, cells become specialized to perform particular functions. A specialized cell has features that make it well adapted to carry out its specific job.

🧠 Key Idea: Structure → Function

When studying a specialized cell, always ask:

What feature does the cell have, and how does that feature help it perform its function?

🌱 1. Root Hair Cell

Root hair cells are found near the tips of plant roots. Their main functions are to absorb water and mineral ions from the soil.

🔬 Adaptations

  • Long root hair: Provides a large surface area for absorption.
  • Thin cell wall: Gives water a short distance to travel into the cell.
  • Large vacuole: Helps maintain a low water potential, supporting water uptake by osmosis.
  • Many mitochondria: Provide energy for active transport of mineral ions.

🌿 2. Palisade Cell

Palisade cells are found near the upper surface of leaves. They are specialized for photosynthesis.

🔬 Adaptations

  • Many chloroplasts: Absorb light energy for photosynthesis.
  • Long shape: Helps the cells become closely packed near the upper surface of the leaf.
  • Large vacuole: Pushes the cytoplasm and chloroplasts towards the edge of the cell, helping efficient light absorption.

🍃 3. Guard Cells

Guard cells are specialized cells found in pairs around stomata. They control whether the stomatal pore is open or closed.

🔬 Adaptations

  • Guard cells can change shape when their water content changes.
  • They contain chloroplasts and can carry out photosynthesis.
  • Their arrangement allows them to open and close the stomatal pore.

🩸 4. Red Blood Cell

Red blood cells are specialized for transporting oxygen around the body.

🔬 Adaptations

  • Biconcave shape: Provides a large surface area for gas exchange and gives a short diffusion distance.
  • No nucleus in mature mammalian red blood cells: Provides more space for haemoglobin.
  • Haemoglobin: Combines with oxygen for transport.
  • Flexible membrane: Allows the cell to pass through narrow blood vessels.

🧬 5. Sperm Cell

The sperm cell is the male gamete. Its function is to reach and fertilize the female gamete.

🔬 Adaptations

  • Flagellum: Allows the sperm cell to swim towards the egg.
  • Many mitochondria: Provide energy for movement.
  • Acrosome: Contains substances that help the sperm penetrate the egg during fertilization.
  • Streamlined shape: Helps movement through fluid.

🔬 5. Microscopy

Study the labelled parts carefully. You should be able to identify their functions in an O/L Biology examination.

A microscope is an instrument used to make very small objects appear larger so that their structures can be observed. Microscopes are especially useful when studying cells and microorganisms.

🧠 Why are microscopes important?

  • They allow structures that are too small to be seen clearly with the naked eye to be observed.
  • They help scientists and students study cells, tissues and microorganisms.
  • They allow biological specimens to be examined in greater detail.

🔍 Main Parts of a Light Microscope

A light microscope uses visible light and lenses to produce a magnified image of a specimen.

⚙️ Important Parts and Their Functions

  • Eyepiece lens: The lens through which the specimen is viewed.
  • Objective lenses: Magnify the specimen. A microscope may have different objective lenses with different magnifications.
  • Stage: Supports the microscope slide containing the specimen.
  • Stage clips: Hold the slide securely on the stage.
  • Light source: Provides light that passes through the specimen.
  • Coarse adjustment knob: Moves the lens or stage by a relatively large amount to bring the specimen into approximate focus.
  • Fine adjustment knob: Makes small adjustments to obtain a clearer, sharper image.
  • Diaphragm: Controls the amount of light passing through the specimen.

🧫 Preparing a Temporary Slide

A specimen must usually be placed on a microscope slide before it can be observed.

  1. Place a thin specimen on a clean microscope slide.
  2. Add a suitable drop of water or other mounting liquid if required.
  3. Lower a coverslip carefully over the specimen.
  4. Remove excess liquid if necessary.

⚠️ Good Microscope Practice

  • Begin with the lowest-power objective lens.
  • Make sure the specimen is properly positioned over the light.
  • Use the coarse adjustment first when appropriate, then use the fine adjustment to obtain a sharp image.
  • Handle slides and coverslips carefully.

✏️ Biological Drawings

When making a biological drawing, the drawing should represent what is actually observed rather than an artistic version of the specimen.

🎯 Rules for a Good Biological Drawing

  • Use clear, single lines.
  • Do not shade the drawing.
  • Draw only structures that are actually observed.
  • Make the drawing large enough to show important structures clearly.
  • Use straight label lines where possible.
  • Keep label lines from crossing each other.
  • Include a suitable title.
  • Include magnification or scale information when required.

📏 6. Magnification

Magnification tells us how many times larger an image is compared with the actual object.

🧮 The Main Formula

Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

The same relationship can be rearranged to find the image size or actual size.

Image size = Magnification × Actual size

Actual size = Image size ÷ Magnification

📐 Units of Measurement

Biological structures are often extremely small, so different units are used to measure them.

🔢 Important Conversions

  • 1 millimetre (mm) = 1,000 micrometres (μm)
  • 1 micrometre (μm) = 1,000 nanometres (nm)
  • 1 mm = 1,000,000 nm

🧠 Example Calculation

A cell has an actual length of 50 μm. Its image is 10 mm long. What is its magnification?

First convert 10 mm to μm:

10 mm = 10,000 μm

Now use:

Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size

Magnification = 10,000 ÷ 50 = 200

Therefore, the magnification is: ×200
