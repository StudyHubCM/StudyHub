🧪 Chemical Formulae & Equations

O/L Chemistry — 0515

Learn how to write chemical symbols, formulae, word equations, balanced symbol equations, ionic formulae and state symbols.

1. 🧪 Introduction to Chemical Formulae & Equations

Chemistry uses symbols, formulae and equations to represent elements, compounds and chemical reactions.

A chemical formula tells us:

  • Which elements are present.
  • How many atoms of each element are present in a particle.
  • The ratio of ions in an ionic compound.

A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using chemical formulae and symbols.

2. 🔤 Chemical Symbols

Each element has a chemical symbol.

A symbol usually contains one or two letters.

  • The first letter is always capitalized.
  • The second letter, if present, is lowercase.

4. 🔢 What Is a Chemical Formula?

A chemical formula uses element symbols and numbers to show the composition of a substance.

Examples:

  • Water — H₂O
  • Carbon dioxide — CO₂
  • Sodium chloride — NaCl
  • Calcium carbonate — CaCO₃
  • Sulfuric acid — H₂SO₄

5. 🔍 Subscripts in Formulae

A small number written after an element symbol is called a subscript.

It tells us how many atoms of that element are represented.

Example: H₂O

H₂O contains:

  • 2 hydrogen atoms
  • 1 oxygen atom

Example: CO₂

CO₂ contains:

  • 1 carbon atom
  • 2 oxygen atoms

6. 🧮 Coefficients vs Subscripts

Do not confuse a coefficient with a subscript.

In:

2H₂O

  • 2 is the coefficient.
  • ₂ is the subscript.

Therefore 2H₂O represents two water molecules, containing a total of four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms.

7. 🧱 Formulae of Ionic Compounds

The formula of an ionic compound must have an overall charge of zero.

The charges of the ions are used to determine the simplest whole-number ratio.

Example: Sodium chloride

Sodium ion = Na⁺

Chloride ion = Cl⁻

The charges balance in a 1:1 ratio: NaCl

8. ⚡ Writing Formulae Using Ion Charges

Example: Magnesium chloride

Magnesium ion = Mg²⁺

Chloride ion = Cl⁻

Two chloride ions are needed to balance one magnesium ion.

Formula: MgCl₂

Word equation:

Magnesium + chlorine → magnesium chloride

Symbol equation:

Mg + Cl₂ → MgCl₂

9. 🧪 Formula of Aluminium Oxide

Aluminium forms Al³⁺ ions.

Oxygen forms O²⁻ ions.

The smallest ratio that balances the charges is:

2Al³⁺ : 3O²⁻

Formula: Al₂O₃

Word equation:

Aluminium + oxygen → aluminium oxide

Symbol equation:

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

10. 🧮 Simplifying Ionic Ratios

Always reduce the ratio of ions to the simplest whole-number ratio.

For example, Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ have equal and opposite charges.

Therefore:

Ca²⁺ : O²⁻ = 1 : 1

Formula: CaO

Word equation:

Calcium + oxygen → calcium oxide

Symbol equation:

2Ca + O₂ → 2CaO

Some ions contain more than one atom. These are called polyatomic ions.

13. 🧠 Formulae with Brackets

Brackets are needed when more than one polyatomic ion is present.

Example: Calcium hydroxide

Calcium = Ca²⁺

Hydroxide = OH⁻

Two hydroxide ions are needed:

Ca(OH)₂

The subscript 2 applies to the entire OH group.

Word equation:

Calcium + water → calcium hydroxide + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂

17. 📝 What Is a Chemical Equation?

A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using chemical formulae and symbols.

The substances on the left are the reactants.

The substances on the right are the products.

The arrow means: produces / reacts to form.

18. ➡️ Reactants and Products

Consider:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

  • Reactants: hydrogen and oxygen
  • Product: water

Word equation:

Hydrogen + oxygen → water

Symbol equation:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

19. 🗣️ Word Equations

A word equation describes a chemical reaction using the names of substances.

Example:

Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Word equations are useful before converting the reaction into chemical symbols and formulae.

20. 🔤 Converting Word Equations to Symbol Equations

Use this method:

  1. Write the correct formula for each reactant.
  2. Write the correct formula for each product.
  3. Place the correct substances on each side.
  4. Balance the equation.
  5. Check that the atoms are equal on both sides.

Example

Word equation:

Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Unbalanced symbol equation:

Mg + O₂ → MgO

Balanced symbol equation:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

21. ⚖️ The Law of Conservation of Mass

In a chemical reaction, atoms are not created or destroyed.

They are rearranged to form new substances.

Therefore, a chemical equation must contain the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.

22. ⚖️ Balancing Chemical Equations

A chemical equation is balanced when the number of atoms of every element is the same on both sides.

Important: Never change subscripts to balance an equation.

Use coefficients placed in front of formulae.

23. 🧮 Simple Balancing Example

Word equation:

Hydrogen + oxygen → water

Start:

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

There are 2 oxygen atoms on the left but only 1 on the right.

Put 2 before H₂O:

H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Now there are 4 hydrogen atoms on the right, so put 2 before H₂:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Hydrogen atoms = 4 on each side.

Oxygen atoms = 2 on each side.

24. 🔢 Coefficients in Balanced Equations

A coefficient multiplies the entire formula.

For example:

3H₂O

represents:

  • 6 hydrogen atoms
  • 3 oxygen atoms

because the coefficient 3 multiplies both atoms in H₂O.

25. 🧪 Balancing Magnesium + Oxygen

Word equation:

Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Symbol equation:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Element Left Right
Mg 2 2
O 2 2

26. 🧪 Balancing Sodium + Chlorine

Word equation:

Sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride

Symbol equation:

2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl

Chlorine exists as Cl₂, so two chlorine atoms must appear on each side.

27. 💧 State Symbols

State symbols tell us the physical state of each substance in an equation.

Symbol Meaning
(s) Solid
(l) Liquid
(g) Gas
(aq) Aqueous solution

28. 💧 Meaning of Aqueous

Aqueous means that a substance is dissolved in water.

The symbol is: (aq)

Example:

NaCl(aq)

This means sodium chloride is present in aqueous solution.

29. 🧪 Equations with State Symbols

Word equation:

Magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

The state symbols provide additional information about the substances during the reaction.

30. 🫧 Gas Formation

A gas formed during a reaction may be shown using (g).

Word equation:

Zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

31. 🧪 Precipitate Symbol

In equations, a solid formed from a solution can be described as a precipitate.

It may be represented by (s) when its solid state is relevant.

Example:

Word equation:

Silver nitrate + sodium chloride → silver chloride + sodium nitrate

Symbol equation:

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

Silver chloride is formed as a solid precipitate.

32. 🔥 Conditions Above the Arrow

Some reactions require particular conditions such as heat, light or a catalyst.

These conditions can be written above the reaction arrow.

Example:

Word equation:

Calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation:

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

The reaction requires strong heating.

33. 🧪 Common Reaction Patterns

Recognizing common reaction patterns makes equation writing easier.

Reaction Type General Pattern
Combination A + B → AB
Decomposition AB → A + B
Metal + acid Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen
Acid + base Acid + base → salt + water
Acid + carbonate Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
Metal + oxygen Metal + oxygen → metal oxide

34. 🔥 Metal + Oxygen

Word equation:

Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Symbol equation:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Many metals react with oxygen to form metal oxides.

35. 🧪 Metal + Acid

A reactive metal can react with an acid to form a salt and hydrogen gas.

Word equation:

Magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

Symbol equation:

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

The exact salt depends on the acid and metal involved.

36. 💧 Acid + Base

An acid can react with a base in a neutralization reaction.

Word equation:

Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

Symbol equation:

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

37. 🪨 Acid + Carbonate

Acids react with carbonates to produce a salt, water and carbon dioxide.

Word equation:

Hydrochloric acid + calcium carbonate → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation:

2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

38. 🧪 Acid + Metal Oxide

A metal oxide can react with an acid to form a salt and water.

Word equation:

Copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid → copper(II) sulfate + water

Symbol equation:

CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O

39. 🧪 Acid + Metal Hydroxide

Word equation:

Sulfuric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium sulfate + water

Symbol equation:

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

40. 🔥 Thermal Decomposition

Thermal decomposition is the breakdown of a compound when heated.

Example:

Word equation:

Calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

41. 🧪 Decomposition of Copper(II) Carbonate

Word equation:

Copper(II) carbonate → copper(II) oxide + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation:

CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂

42. 🧲 Displacement Reactions

A more reactive element can displace a less reactive element from one of its compounds.

Example:

Word equation:

Zinc + copper(II) sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper

Symbol equation:

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu

43. 🧪 Metal Displacement and the Reactivity Series

The reactivity series helps predict whether one metal can displace another from a compound.

A metal higher in the reactivity series can generally displace a metal lower in the series from its salt solution.

Example:

Zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc can displace copper from copper(II) sulfate.

44. 🧪 Combustion Reactions

Combustion is a reaction in which a substance burns in oxygen.

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water.

Word equation:

Methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Symbol equation:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

45. 🧪 Symbol Equations Must Be Balanced

Consider:

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

This is not balanced because the number of hydrogen and oxygen atoms differs between the two sides.

The balanced equation is:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

46. 🧮 Step-by-Step Balancing Method

  1. Write the correct formulae for all reactants and products.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  3. Choose one element that is not balanced.
  4. Add coefficients in front of formulae.
  5. Recount all atoms.
  6. Continue until every element is balanced.
  7. Reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio.

⚠️ Remember

Never change the subscripts inside a chemical formula while balancing an equation.

47. 🔢 Balancing More Complex Equations

Word equation:

Aluminium + oxygen → aluminium oxide

Symbol equation:

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Check aluminium: 4 atoms on each side.

Check oxygen: 6 atoms on each side.

48. 🧠 Balancing Equations with Polyatomic Groups

If a polyatomic ion remains unchanged on both sides of an equation, it can sometimes be treated as a single unit while balancing.

However, always check the individual atoms at the end.

Example:

Word equation:

Sulfuric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium sulfate + water

Symbol equation:

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

49. 🧪 Ionic Equations

An ionic equation shows the particles that actually take part in an ionic reaction.

It can be obtained by removing spectator ions from a complete ionic equation.

At O/L level, focus especially on recognizing ions and understanding that aqueous ionic substances can separate into ions.

50. ⚡ Simple Ionic Representation of Neutralization

In an aqueous neutralization reaction, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to form water.

Word equation:

Hydrogen ions + hydroxide ions → water

Symbol equation:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

51. 🧪 Spectator Ions

A spectator ion is an ion that remains unchanged during a reaction.

For example, in:

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ remain in solution and are not changed in the neutralization itself.

52. 🧪 Complete vs Ionic Equations

A complete symbol equation shows the substances involved in the overall reaction.

An ionic equation focuses on the ions and particles that actually undergo chemical change.

These are different representations of the same chemical process.

53. 🔄 Reversible Reactions

Some reactions can proceed in both directions.

Such reactions are represented using a reversible arrow:

⇌

The forward reaction produces the products, while the reverse reaction converts products back into reactants.

54. 🧮 Formulae and Relative Molecular Mass

Chemical formulae can be used to calculate relative molecular mass, Mr.

Add the relative atomic masses of all atoms represented in the formula.

Example: H₂O

Using H = 1 and O = 16:

Mr(H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16

Mr(H₂O) = 18

55. 🧮 Relative Formula Mass of Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds do not consist of separate molecules, so the term relative formula mass, Mr, is commonly used for the mass represented by the formula unit.

Example: NaCl

Na = 23

Cl = 35.5

Mr(NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5

56. 🧮 Calculating Formula Mass with Brackets

Example: Ca(OH)₂

Ca = 40

O = 16

H = 1

Mr[Ca(OH)₂] = 40 + 2(16 + 1)

Mr[Ca(OH)₂] = 40 + 34 = 74

57. 🧪 Chemical Formulae and Mole Ratios

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the mole ratio between reacting substances.

For:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

the mole ratio is:

H₂ : O₂ : H₂O = 2 : 1 : 2

58. 🧮 Using Equations for Simple Calculations

Suppose 2 moles of hydrogen react completely with 1 mole of oxygen:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The equation shows that 2 moles of H₂ produce 2 moles of H₂O, provided oxygen is sufficient.

Balanced equations therefore provide useful quantitative information.

59. 🔬 Empirical and Molecular Formulae

An empirical formula gives the simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a compound.

A molecular formula gives the actual number of each type of atom in one molecule.

Example

Hydrogen peroxide has molecular formula H₂O₂.

Its simplest atom ratio is 1:1, so its empirical formula is HO.

60. 🔢 Determining an Empirical Formula

A common method is:

  1. Convert each mass or percentage into moles.
  2. Divide all mole values by the smallest value.
  3. Obtain a simple whole-number ratio.
  4. Write the empirical formula.

This method becomes especially important in quantitative chemistry.

61. 🧪 Molecular Formula from Empirical Formula

If the empirical formula and relative molecular mass are known:

Molecular formula = empirical formula × n

where:

n = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass

62. ⚠️ Common Formula-Writing Mistakes

  • ❌ Writing NaCl as NACl.
    ✅ Element symbols use correct capitalization.
  • ❌ Writing MgCl instead of MgCl₂.
    ✅ Mg²⁺ requires two Cl⁻ ions.
  • ❌ Writing CaOH₂.
    ✅ Use Ca(OH)₂ because the subscript applies to the whole hydroxide ion.
  • ❌ Changing subscripts to balance equations.
    ✅ Change coefficients only.
  • ❌ Forgetting that elements such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine commonly occur as diatomic molecules.
    ✅ Use H₂, O₂, N₂ and Cl₂ where appropriate.
  • ❌ Forgetting charges when constructing ionic formulae.
    ✅ Make the overall charge zero.

63. ⚠️ Common Equation-Balancing Mistakes

  • ❌ Changing chemical formulae instead of coefficients.
  • ❌ Forgetting diatomic elements.
  • ❌ Failing to count atoms after balancing.
  • ❌ Using fractional coefficients when whole-number coefficients are expected.
  • ❌ Forgetting state symbols when they are required.
  • ❌ Writing products with incorrect formulae.
  • ❌ Balancing one element and forgetting to recheck the others.

65. 🧪 State Symbols and Physical States

State symbols are particularly useful when a question asks about:

  • Whether a substance is solid, liquid or gas.
  • Whether a substance is dissolved in water.
  • Whether a precipitate forms.
  • Whether a gas is produced.

Example:

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

66. 🧪 Full Worked Equation Example

Question

Write a balanced equation for the reaction between calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid.

Step 1: Write the word equation

Calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

Step 2: Write the formulae

CaCO₃ + HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Step 3: Balance

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Final answer

Word equation: Calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

Symbol equation: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

68. 🧠 How to Approach an Equation Question

  1. Read the question carefully.
  2. Identify all reactants.
  3. Identify all products.
  4. Write the correct word equation.
  5. Convert each substance to its correct formula.
  6. Check whether any element is naturally diatomic.
  7. Balance using coefficients.
  8. Add state symbols if required.
  9. Count every atom on both sides.

75. 🌟 Final Takeaway

The key method is:

🧠 Name the substances → write the correct formulae → balance the equation → check the atoms → add state symbols when required.

🧪 Whenever a reaction is given, learn it in both forms:

Word equation: names of the substances

Symbol equation: chemical formulae and balanced coefficients
