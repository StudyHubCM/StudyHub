BIOLOGY • O/L 0510

🧠 Coordination & Response

Understand the nervous system, reflex actions, the brain, sense organs, hormones, plant responses and coordination in living organisms.

1. 🧠 What is Coordination?

Coordination is the way an organism detects changes in its internal or external environment and produces an appropriate response.

Coordination allows different parts of the body to work together effectively.

📌 Key Idea

Coordination involves:

  1. Detecting a change.
  2. Sending information to a coordinating centre.
  3. Processing the information.
  4. Sending instructions to an effector.
  5. Producing a response.

2. 🌡️ Stimulus

A stimulus is a change in the environment that can be detected by an organism.

Examples include:

  • 🌡️ A change in temperature.
  • 💡 Light.
  • 🔊 Sound.
  • 🖐️ Touch or pressure.
  • 🧪 Chemicals.
  • ⚠️ Painful or harmful changes.

3. ⚡ Response

A response is a change in an organism caused by a stimulus.

For example, when bright light enters the eye, the pupil becomes smaller. This is a response to the stimulus of bright light.

4. 🔄 The Basic Coordination Pathway

A simple coordination pathway can be represented as:

Stimulus → Receptor → Coordinator → Effector → Response

  • Stimulus: a change detected by the organism.
  • Receptor: detects the stimulus.
  • Coordinator: processes information and coordinates the response.
  • Effector: carries out the response.
  • Response: the resulting action or change.

5. 👁️ Receptors

Receptors are specialised cells or structures that detect specific stimuli.

Receptor Stimulus Detected Example Location
Photoreceptors Light Retina of eye
Mechanoreceptors Pressure, touch, vibration Skin and ear
Thermoreceptors Temperature changes Skin
Chemoreceptors Chemicals Tongue and nose

6. ⚡ Nervous Coordination

Nervous coordination uses electrical impulses carried by neurones.

The nervous system allows organisms to respond rapidly to changes in their surroundings.

📌 Main Features

  • Very rapid communication.
  • Uses electrical impulses.
  • Usually produces a short-lived response.
  • Can act on specific effectors.

7. 🔬 Neurones

A neurone is a specialised nerve cell that carries electrical impulses through the nervous system.

There are three main types of neurone studied at O/L:

  1. Sensory neurone
  2. Relay neurone
  3. Motor neurone

8. 🧬 Structure of a Neurone

Neurones are adapted to transmit electrical impulses efficiently.

🖼️ Diagram: Structure of a Neurone

Diagram labels to know:

  • Cell body
  • Nucleus
  • Dendrites
  • Axon
  • Myelin sheath
  • Axon terminals

9. 🌿 Dendrites

Dendrites are branched extensions that receive electrical impulses from receptors or other neurones.

10. 🧬 Cell Body

The cell body contains the nucleus and other structures needed to maintain the neurone.

11. ⚡ Axon

The axon is a long fibre that carries electrical impulses away from the cell body.

12. 🛡️ Myelin Sheath

The myelin sheath surrounds many axons and helps protect them and increase the speed at which impulses travel.

13. 📡 Sensory Neurones

A sensory neurone carries impulses from a receptor towards the central nervous system.

Direction:

Receptor → Sensory neurone → CNS

14. 🔗 Relay Neurones

A relay neurone carries impulses between neurones inside the central nervous system.

Relay neurones are particularly important in reflex arcs.

15. 💪 Motor Neurones

A motor neurone carries impulses from the central nervous system to an effector.

Direction:

CNS → Motor neurone → Effector

16. 🧠 Central Nervous System

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of:

  • 🧠 Brain
  • 🦴 Spinal cord

The CNS receives information, processes it and coordinates appropriate responses.

17. 🕸️ Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system (PNS) consists mainly of nerves that connect the CNS to receptors and effectors.

18. 🧠 The Human Brain

The brain is the main coordinating centre of the nervous system. It receives information from receptors, interprets information and coordinates responses.

🖼️ Diagram: Human Brain

Main regions to know:

  • Cerebrum
  • Cerebellum
  • Medulla oblongata
  • Spinal cord

19. 🧠 Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain.

It is involved in:

  • Conscious thought.
  • Memory.
  • Learning.
  • Intelligence.
  • Interpreting sensory information.
  • Voluntary movement.

20. ⚖️ Cerebellum

The cerebellum coordinates muscular movement and helps maintain balance and posture.

21. ❤️ Medulla Oblongata

The medulla oblongata controls many involuntary activities, including:

  • Breathing.
  • Heartbeat.
  • Some digestive activities.

22. 🦴 Spinal Cord

The spinal cord is a long bundle of nervous tissue extending from the brain through the vertebral column.

It carries impulses between the brain and the rest of the body and acts as an important coordinating centre for reflex actions.

23. ⚡ Electrical Impulses

Information is transmitted along neurones as electrical impulses.

The impulse travels along the neurone and may be passed to another neurone at a synapse.

24. 🔗 Synapses

A synapse is the junction between two neurones.

The small gap between the neurones is called the synaptic gap.

When an electrical impulse reaches the end of a neurone, chemicals called neurotransmitters are released. They cross the synaptic gap and trigger an impulse in the next neurone.

📌 Important

Transmission across a synapse is effectively one-way.

25. ⚡ Reflex Actions

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic and involuntary response to a stimulus.

Reflex actions protect the body from potentially harmful changes in the environment.

Examples include:

  • Pulling your hand away from a hot object.
  • Blinking when something approaches the eye.
  • The pupil becoming smaller in bright light.
  • Coughing when the airway is irritated.

26. 🔄 Reflex Arc

A reflex arc is the pathway followed by an impulse during a reflex action.

Receptor → Sensory neurone → Relay neurone → Motor neurone → Effector

The response occurs rapidly because the pathway can be coordinated through the spinal cord without waiting for the brain to initiate the immediate withdrawal response.

27. 🔥 Reflex Arc Example: Touching a Hot Object

Imagine a person accidentally places their hand on a very hot object.

The body must react quickly to reduce the chance of injury.

🔥 Step-by-Step Process

  1. Heat stimulates receptors in the skin.

    Temperature and pain-sensitive receptors in the skin detect the harmful heat.

  2. An electrical impulse is generated.

    The receptor converts the stimulus into a nerve impulse.

  3. The sensory neurone carries the impulse.

    The impulse travels along a sensory neurone from the receptor towards the spinal cord.

  4. The impulse reaches the spinal cord.

    The sensory neurone passes the information to a relay neurone inside the central nervous system.

  5. The relay neurone passes the impulse.

    The relay neurone connects the sensory neurone to a motor neurone.

  6. The motor neurone carries the impulse to the effector.

    The motor neurone carries the impulse from the spinal cord to the appropriate arm muscle.

  7. The muscle contracts.

    The muscle acts as the effector. It contracts and pulls the hand away from the hot object.

  8. The hand is withdrawn.

    The response happens rapidly and automatically, helping to protect the body.

🧠 What happens in the brain?

Information about the harmful stimulus is also sent towards the brain, where it can be consciously perceived as pain.

However, the rapid withdrawal response does not have to wait for the brain to make the conscious decision to move the hand.

Therefore, the main reflex pathway can be remembered as:

🔥 Hot object → Skin receptor → Sensory neurone → Relay neurone in spinal cord → Motor neurone → Arm muscle → Hand withdrawn

29. 🛡️ Importance of Reflex Actions

Reflex actions are important because they provide rapid protection from potentially harmful stimuli.

  • They are rapid.
  • They are automatic.
  • They do not require conscious decision-making before the initial response.
  • They help prevent or reduce injury.

30. 🧠 Voluntary Actions

A voluntary action is an action that is consciously controlled.

Examples include:

  • Walking.
  • Writing.
  • Picking up an object.
  • Raising your hand.

31. ⚡ Involuntary Actions

An involuntary action occurs automatically and is not consciously controlled.

Examples include:

  • Heartbeat.
  • Breathing rate adjustments.
  • Some reflex actions.
  • Pupil response to light.

32. 🔄 Reflex vs Voluntary Actions

Reflex Action Voluntary Action
Automatic Consciously controlled
Usually very rapid Usually slower
Protective in many cases Often performed intentionally
Uses a reflex pathway Usually involves conscious processing in the brain

33. 👁️ The Human Eye

The eye is a sense organ that detects light and allows us to see.

  • Blind spot
  • Aqueous humour
  • Vitreous humour

34. 👁️ Cornea

The cornea is the transparent curved front surface of the eye.

It refracts light as light enters the eye.

35. 🟤 Iris

The iris is the coloured part of the eye that controls the size of the pupil.

36. ⚫ Pupil

The pupil is the opening through which light enters the eye.

37. 🔍 Lens

The lens is transparent and changes shape to focus light onto the retina.

38. 🎯 Retina

The retina contains light-sensitive receptor cells that detect light.

The receptors convert information from light into nerve impulses.

39. 🧠 Optic Nerve

The optic nerve carries nerve impulses from the retina to the brain.

40. 💡 Pupil Reflex

The pupil changes size automatically according to the intensity of light.

Light Condition Circular Muscles Radial Muscles Pupil
Bright light Contract Relax Constricts
Dim light Relax Contract Dilates

41. ☀️ Bright-Light Reflex

When bright light enters the eye:

  1. Light is detected by receptors in the retina.
  2. Impulses are sent to the brain.
  3. The brain sends impulses to the iris.
  4. Circular muscles of the iris contract.
  5. Radial muscles relax.
  6. The pupil becomes smaller.
  7. Less light enters the eye.

42. 🌙 Dim-Light Response

In dim light:

  1. Circular muscles relax.
  2. Radial muscles contract.
  3. The pupil becomes larger.
  4. More light enters the eye.

43. 🔍 Accommodation

Accommodation is the process by which the eye changes the shape of its lens so that objects at different distances can be focused clearly on the retina.

44. 📖 Viewing a Near Object

When focusing on a nearby object:

  1. Ciliary muscles contract.
  2. Suspensory ligaments become slack.
  3. The lens becomes thicker and more rounded.
  4. The lens refracts light more strongly.
  5. A clear image is focused on the retina.

45. 🌄 Viewing a Distant Object

When focusing on a distant object:

  1. Ciliary muscles relax.
  2. Suspensory ligaments become tight.
  3. The lens becomes thinner and flatter.
  4. The lens refracts light less strongly.
  5. A clear image is focused on the retina.

46. 👂 The Human Ear

The ear is a sense organ involved mainly in hearing and balance.

🖼️ Diagram: Human Ear

Important structures:

  • Pinna
  • Ear canal
  • Eardrum
  • Ossicles
  • Cochlea
  • Auditory nerve
  • Semicircular canals

47. 🔊 Hearing

Sound waves enter the ear through the ear canal and cause the eardrum to vibrate.

The vibrations are passed through the ossicles to the cochlea.

Receptor cells in the cochlea detect the vibrations and generate nerve impulses.

The auditory nerve carries these impulses to the brain, where they are interpreted as sound.

48. 👂 Eardrum and Ossicles

The eardrum vibrates when sound waves reach it.

The three small bones called the ossicles transmit and amplify these vibrations towards the inner ear.

49. 🌀 Cochlea

The cochlea contains receptor cells that detect vibrations and convert them into nerve impulses.

50. ⚖️ Balance

The semicircular canals and other structures of the inner ear provide information about head movement and help maintain balance.

51. 🌱 Coordination in Plants

Plants do not have a nervous system like animals. They coordinate responses mainly using plant hormones and changes in growth.

52. 🌱 Tropisms

A tropism is a growth response of a plant towards or away from a directional stimulus.

Growth towards a stimulus is called a positive tropism.

Growth away from a stimulus is called a negative tropism.

53. ☀️ Phototropism

Phototropism is a growth response to light.

Shoots usually show positive phototropism because they grow towards light.

This helps the plant obtain light for photosynthesis.

54. 🌍 Geotropism

Geotropism, also called gravitropism, is a growth response to gravity.

  • Roots usually show positive geotropism because they grow in the direction of gravity.
  • Shoots usually show negative geotropism because they grow away from gravity.

55. 🧪 Auxin

Auxin is a plant hormone involved in controlling growth.

Auxin is produced mainly in growing shoot tips and can influence the rate of cell elongation.

56. ☀️ How Shoots Bend Towards Light

When light comes mainly from one side:

  1. Auxin becomes distributed unevenly in the shoot.
  2. More auxin accumulates on the shaded side.
  3. Cells on the shaded side elongate more.
  4. The shaded side grows faster.
  5. The shoot bends towards the light.

57. 🧪 Investigating Phototropism

A simple experiment can be used to investigate the effect of directional light on plant shoots.

🔬 Basic Method

  1. Place young seedlings in suitable conditions.
  2. Allow light to enter mainly from one direction.
  3. Observe the direction of shoot growth.
  4. Compare with seedlings receiving light from different directions.

📌 Variables

  • Independent variable: direction of light.
  • Dependent variable: direction or amount of shoot growth.
  • Control variables: plant species, age, water, temperature and growth conditions.

58. 🧠 Nervous vs Hormonal Coordination

Nervous Coordination Hormonal Coordination
Uses electrical impulses and neurotransmitters Uses chemical hormones
Usually very rapid Usually slower
Effects are often short-lived Effects may last longer
Usually acts on specific effectors Hormones can travel through body fluids

60. 📚 Important Definitions

Coordination: The control and organisation of responses to stimuli.

Stimulus: A change in the environment detected by an organism.

Response: A change produced by an organism following a stimulus.

Receptor: A specialised cell or structure that detects a stimulus.

Effector: A muscle or gland that carries out a response.

Neurone: A specialised nerve cell that transmits electrical impulses.

Synapse: A junction between neurones across which signals are transmitted using neurotransmitters.

Reflex action: A rapid, automatic and involuntary response to a stimulus.

Reflex arc: The pathway followed by nerve impulses during a reflex action.

Tropism: A directional growth response of a plant to a stimulus.

Phototropism: A growth response to light.

Geotropism: A growth response to gravity.

Auxin: A plant hormone involved in controlling growth.

62. 📝 How to Answer a Reflex Arc Question

If an exam asks what happens when a person touches a hot object, do not simply write: "The hand moves away."

Give the complete pathway:

Hot object → receptor in skin → sensory neurone → relay neurone in spinal cord → motor neurone → arm muscle → contraction → hand withdrawn

Also remember that the brain receives information so the person can become consciously aware of the painful stimulus.

63. ❌ Common O/L Mistakes

  • ❌ Saying that a receptor is an effector.
  • ❌ Saying that sensory neurones carry impulses from the CNS to muscles.
  • ❌ Saying that motor neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS.
  • ❌ Forgetting the relay neurone in a basic reflex arc.
  • ❌ Saying the brain must consciously decide before the withdrawal reflex occurs.
  • ❌ Mixing up ciliary muscles and iris muscles.
  • ❌ Saying that the pupil itself contracts.
  • ❌ Confusing phototropism with geotropism.
  • ❌ Forgetting that roots are generally positively geotropic.
  • ❌ Forgetting that shoots generally show positive phototropism.
